Question 1

Example
The graph of \( y = f(x) \) has:
• a horizontal asymptote \( y = 2 \) as \( x \to +\infty \)
• a local minimum at \( (-3, -4) \)
• a non-stationary point of inflexion at \( (0, 0) \) where \( f \) is decreasing through it
• a local maximum at \( (2, 5) \)
• \( f \) is increasing on \( (-\infty, -3) \cup (-\infty, \ldots) \) — deduce the remaining regions.

(i) Complete the intervals where \( f \) is increasing / decreasing. [2]
(ii) Sketch \( y = f'(x) \) indicating all features. [4]
(iii) Sketch \( y = |f'(x)| \). [2]
Solution with mark scheme
Concept — the translation table
Feature of \( y = f(x) \)Feature of \( y = f'(x) \)
Local max or min at \( (a, k) \)\( f'(a) = 0 \) — curve TOUCHES x-axis (crosses if sign of \( f' \) changes)
Non-stationary inflexion at \( x = c \)\( f''(c) = 0 \) → \( f' \) has a TURNING POINT at \( x = c \) (not a zero)
Stationary inflexion at \( x = c \)\( f'(c) = 0 \) AND \( f'' \) doesn't change sign → \( f' \) TOUCHES axis without crossing
\( f \) increasing on interval\( f' > 0 \) on interval → \( f' \) is ABOVE x-axis
\( f \) decreasing on interval\( f' < 0 \) → \( f' \) is BELOW x-axis
Horizontal asymptote \( y = k \)\( f'(x) \to 0 \) as \( x \to \infty \) → \( f' \) has horizontal asymptote at \( y = 0 \)
Trap
Non-stationary inflexions at \( x = c \) do NOT put a zero of \( f' \) at \( c \) — instead, they put a TURNING POINT of \( f' \) at \( c \). Common error: putting \( f'(0) = 0 \) at a non-stationary inflexion.
Step 1 — (i) Deduce intervals
M1 A1
Between min at -3 and max at 2, \( f \) increases. After max at 2, \( f \) decreases (approaching asymptote from above). At inflexion (0,0), decreasing through it — wait, but between -3 and 2, f should be increasing. So the inflexion at 0 must be a moment where the CONCAVITY changes, not the direction of f.
Actually if \( f \) goes from min at (-3, -4) up to max at (2, 5), then it's increasing throughout (-3, 2). The non-stationary inflexion at (0, 0) is within this rising region — f is still increasing but the concavity flips.
Corrected: f increases on (-3, 2), decreases on (-∞, -3) ∪ (2, ∞).
Step 2 — (ii) Sketch f'
B1 B1 B1 B1
Features of \( y = f'(x) \):
  • \( f'(-3) = 0 \) crossing − to + (min of f)
  • \( f'(2) = 0 \) crossing + to − (max of f)
  • Turning point of \( f' \) at \( x = 0 \) (non-stationary inflexion of f — f' has local max/min here)
  • \( f'(x) \to 0 \) as \( x \to +\infty \) (from below, since f decreases toward asymptote)
Step 3 — (iii) Sketch |f'(x)|
Reflect everything below x-axis to above. Zeros stay as zeros. B1 B1

Question 2

Example
A function \( f \) satisfies \( f'(x) = e^{-x}(x^{2} - 4) \) for all \( x \). Given \( f(0) = 3 \):
(i) Find all stationary points of \( f \) and classify each. [4]
(ii) Sketch \( y = f'(x) \). [2]
(iii) Determine, showing all reasoning, whether \( f \) has an absolute maximum on \( \mathbb{R} \). If so, state the x-value. [3]
(iv) Show that the absolute maximum value of \( f \) is \( 3 + \int_{0}^{-2} e^{-t}(t^{2} - 4)\,dt \). Do NOT evaluate. [2]
Solution with mark scheme
Trap
For an unbounded domain, "absolute max" requires checking behaviour as \( x \to \pm\infty \). Just finding a local max isn't enough. Also: signs of \( e^{-x} \) always positive, so sign of \( f' \) depends entirely on \( x^{2} - 4 \).
Step 1 — (i) Stationary points
M1 A1
\( f'(x) = 0 \Rightarrow x^{2} - 4 = 0 \Rightarrow x = \pm 2 \). (\( e^{-x} > 0 \) always.)
Step 2 — Classify
M1 A1
Sign of \( x^{2} - 4 \): positive for \( |x| > 2 \), negative for \( |x| < 2 \).
At \( x = -2 \): sign − → + wait, at \( x < -2 \), \( x^{2} > 4 \) so \( f' > 0 \). Between \( -2 \) and \( 2 \), \( x^{2} < 4 \) so \( f' < 0 \). So \( f' \) changes + → − at \( x = -2 \). → local max at \( x = -2 \).
At \( x = 2 \): \( f' \) changes − → + → local min at \( x = 2 \).
Step 3 — (ii) Sketch f'
B1 B1
Positive for \( x < -2 \), zero at \( -2 \) crossing to negative, negative on \( (-2, 2) \), zero at 2 crossing to positive, then \( f'(x) \to 0 \) as \( x \to \infty \) (dominated by \( e^{-x} \)) and \( f'(x) \to +\infty \) as \( x \to -\infty \) (both factors grow).
Step 4 — (iii) Absolute max
M1 M1 A1
As \( x \to -\infty \): \( e^{-x} \to \infty \) and \( x^{2} - 4 \to \infty \), so \( f' \to +\infty \) — \( f \) is increasing very fast to the left. But going leftward means \( f \) grows toward \( +\infty \).
Wait — that's counterintuitive. Let me reverse: as we go LEFT (x decreasing), moving from 0 to -2 to -∞, and \( f' > 0 \) on \( (-\infty, -2) \). If we start at x = 0 and walk LEFT, x is decreasing. Change in f = \( \int f'(x)\,dx \) — but direction matters. Going left from -2 to -∞, x is decreasing, so \( f(x) \) is decreasing (since \( f' > 0 \) means \( f \) increases as x increases, i.e. decreases as x decreases).
So going left from -2, f decreases toward some limit as \( x \to -\infty \).
Going right from -2 to 2, \( f' < 0 \) so f decreases.
Going right from 2 to ∞, \( f' > 0 \) so f increases. As \( x \to \infty \), \( f'(x) \to 0 \) (\( e^{-x} \) dominates), so f approaches a horizontal asymptote.
Local max at \( x = -2 \) is a candidate for absolute max. Need to check it's higher than any limit values.
As \( x \to -\infty \), \( f' \to +\infty \) but this is the DERIVATIVE. Actual f-value: integrating from -∞ to -2 with positive f' means \( f(-2) - f(-\infty) > 0 \), so \( f(-\infty) < f(-2) \). And \( f(-\infty) \) might even be \( -\infty \).
Similarly on the right: \( f(\infty) < f(-2) \) if the local max at -2 is higher than the asymptote value.
Absolute maximum exists at \( x = -2 \).
Step 5 — (iv) Value via integral
M1 A1
\( f(-2) - f(0) = \int_{0}^{-2} f'(t)\,dt = \int_{0}^{-2} e^{-t}(t^{2} - 4)\,dt \).
\( f(-2) = 3 + \int_{0}^{-2} e^{-t}(t^{2} - 4)\,dt \). AG. ✓

Question 3

Example
Find (i) \( \displaystyle\int \sec^{2}(2x)\tan(2x)\,dx \) [3]
(ii) \( \displaystyle\int \sec^{4}(x)\tan^{3}(x)\,dx \) [5]
Solution with mark scheme
Concept — sec/tan integration patterns
Two useful patterns:
  • \( \dfrac{d}{dx}[\sec x] = \sec x\tan x \). So \( \int \sec^{n-1}x \cdot \sec x\tan x\,dx \) suggests \( u = \sec x \).
  • \( \dfrac{d}{dx}[\tan x] = \sec^{2}x \). So \( \int \tan^{n}x \cdot \sec^{2}x\,dx \) suggests \( u = \tan x \).
The trick: LOOK at the powers. If sec has odd degree with a \( \tan \) attached, use \( u = \sec x \). If sec has even degree, use \( u = \tan x \) and rewrite excess \( \sec^{2} = 1 + \tan^{2} \).
Trap
Never rewrite \( \sec^{2}x \) as \( 1/\cos^{2}x \) then try power rule — you'll go in circles. Recognise the sec-tan-composite as a reverse chain rule.
Step 1 — (i)
M1
Recognise pattern: \( \sec(2x)\tan(2x) \) is 1/2 the derivative of \( \sec(2x) \). And there's an extra \( \sec(2x) \) factor. So overall integrand looks like \( \sec(2x) \cdot \sec(2x)\tan(2x) \). Let \( u = \sec(2x) \), then \( du = 2\sec(2x)\tan(2x)\,dx \).
Rewrite: \( \int \sec^{2}(2x)\tan(2x)\,dx = \tfrac{1}{2}\int \sec(2x) \cdot 2\sec(2x)\tan(2x)\,dx = \tfrac{1}{2}\int u\,du \).
Step 2
M1 A1
\( = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{u^{2}}{2} + C = \tfrac{1}{4}\sec^{2}(2x) + C \). \[ \boxed{\int \sec^{2}(2x)\tan(2x)\,dx = \tfrac{1}{4}\sec^{2}(2x) + C} \]
Step 3 — (ii)
M1
\( \sec^{4}x = \sec^{2}x \cdot \sec^{2}x = (1 + \tan^{2}x)\sec^{2}x \). Substitute this:
\( \int \sec^{4}(x)\tan^{3}(x)\,dx = \int (1 + \tan^{2}x)\sec^{2}x \cdot \tan^{3}x\,dx = \int \sec^{2}x(\tan^{3}x + \tan^{5}x)\,dx \).
Step 4
Let \( u = \tan x \), \( du = \sec^{2}x\,dx \). M1
\( = \int u^{3} + u^{5}\,du = \tfrac{u^{4}}{4} + \tfrac{u^{6}}{6} + C \).
Step 5
M1 A1
\[ \boxed{\int \sec^{4}(x)\tan^{3}(x)\,dx = \tfrac{1}{4}\tan^{4}x + \tfrac{1}{6}\tan^{6}x + C} \]

Question 4

Example
Given the Maclaurin series \( \tan x = x + \tfrac{x^{3}}{3} + \tfrac{2x^{5}}{15} + \tfrac{17x^{7}}{315} + \cdots \) (valid for \( |x| < \pi/2 \)):
(i) Show, by division of the series for \( \sin x \) and \( \cos x \), that \( \tan x = x + \tfrac{x^{3}}{3} + O(x^{5}) \). [4]
(ii) Use the series to find \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{\tan x - \sin x}{x^{5}} \). [4]
Solution with mark scheme
Concept — tan x is NOT in MF27
MF27 gives you sin x, cos x, e^x, ln(1+x), (1+x)^n. Notice: no tan x. Standard series for \( \tan x \) (memorise): \[ \tan x = x + \dfrac{x^{3}}{3} + \dfrac{2x^{5}}{15} + \cdots \quad (|x| < \pi/2) \] You can derive it as \( \sin x / \cos x \) by long division of series, but memorise the first two terms (\( x + x^{3}/3 \)) at minimum — that's what most exam questions need.
Trap
Don't just use \( \tan x \approx x \) when the question asks for higher order — you'll get zero for expressions where leading terms cancel.
Step 1 — (i) Division
M1 M1
\( \sin x = x - \tfrac{x^{3}}{6} + O(x^{5}) \), \( \cos x = 1 - \tfrac{x^{2}}{2} + O(x^{4}) \).
\( \tan x = \sin x \cdot (\cos x)^{-1} \). Use \( (1 - u)^{-1} = 1 + u + u^{2} + \cdots \) with \( u = \tfrac{x^{2}}{2} - O(x^{4}) \):
\( (\cos x)^{-1} = 1 + \tfrac{x^{2}}{2} + \tfrac{x^{4}}{4} + O(x^{6}) - O(x^{4}) \) — actually let's keep to \( 1 + \tfrac{x^{2}}{2} + \tfrac{5x^{4}}{24} + O(x^{6}) \) (from full expansion).
Multiply: \( (x - \tfrac{x^{3}}{6})(1 + \tfrac{x^{2}}{2}) = x + \tfrac{x^{3}}{2} - \tfrac{x^{3}}{6} = x + \tfrac{3x^{3} - x^{3}}{6} = x + \tfrac{x^{3}}{3} \). M1 A1
Step 2 — (ii) The limit
M1
\( \tan x = x + \tfrac{x^{3}}{3} + \tfrac{2x^{5}}{15} + \cdots \)
\( \sin x = x - \tfrac{x^{3}}{6} + \tfrac{x^{5}}{120} - \cdots \)
Subtract: \( \tan x - \sin x = 0 + \tfrac{x^{3}}{3} + \tfrac{x^{3}}{6} + \tfrac{2x^{5}}{15} - \tfrac{x^{5}}{120} + O(x^{7}) \).
Simplify: \( \tan x - \sin x = \tfrac{x^{3}}{2} + \left(\tfrac{2}{15} - \tfrac{1}{120}\right)x^{5} + O(x^{7}) \). M1
Step 3
Divide by \( x^{5} \). M1
\( \dfrac{\tan x - \sin x}{x^{5}} = \dfrac{1}{2x^{2}} + \left(\tfrac{2}{15} - \tfrac{1}{120}\right) + O(x^{2}) \).
Step 4
As \( x \to 0 \), first term \( \to \infty \). A1
Limit does not exist — it diverges to \( +\infty \). Note the leading behaviour is \( \tan x - \sin x \sim x^{3}/2 \), which divided by \( x^{5} \) gives \( 1/(2x^{2}) \) diverging.
(Alternative interpretation: user's intent was probably \( (\tan x - \sin x)/x^{3} = 1/2 \). If \( x^{5} \) really was in denominator, limit = ∞.)

Question 5

Example
A curve is given by \( \sin(xy) + x^{2} = 1 \). The point \( P(1, 0) \) is on the curve.
(i) Find \( \dfrac{dy}{dx} \) at \( P \). [4]
(ii) Find \( \dfrac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} \) at \( P \). [6]
(iii) Hence find the Maclaurin series for \( y \) up to the term in \( x^{2} \), where \( y \) is defined implicitly by the equation with initial condition \( y(1) = 0 \). Note: this is a Maclaurin AROUND \( x = 1 \) (Taylor series). Comment on validity. [3]
Solution with mark scheme
Trap
\( \sin(xy) \) requires TWO chain rules simultaneously: outer chain (differentiate sin), inner product rule (xy is a product of x and y). Errors: forgetting the cos(xy) factor, or forgetting product rule inside.
Step 1 — (i)
Differentiate implicitly. M1 M1
\( \cos(xy) \cdot (y + x\dfrac{dy}{dx}) + 2x = 0 \).
Step 2
Substitute \( x = 1, y = 0 \). \( \cos(0) = 1 \). M1 A1
\( 1 \cdot (0 + \dfrac{dy}{dx}) + 2 = 0 \Rightarrow \dfrac{dy}{dx} = -2 \).
Step 3 — (ii) Differentiate again
Pre-grouped form \( \cos(xy)(y + xy') + 2x = 0 \). Differentiate both sides. M1 M1
\( \dfrac{d}{dx}[\cos(xy)] \cdot (y + xy') + \cos(xy)\dfrac{d}{dx}[y + xy'] + 2 = 0 \).
\( \dfrac{d}{dx}[\cos(xy)] = -\sin(xy)(y + xy') \).
\( \dfrac{d}{dx}[y + xy'] = y' + y' + xy'' = 2y' + xy'' \).
Step 4
Assemble. M1
\( -\sin(xy)(y + xy')^{2} + \cos(xy)(2y' + xy'') + 2 = 0 \).
Step 5
Substitute \( x = 1, y = 0, y' = -2 \). \( \sin 0 = 0, \cos 0 = 1 \). M1
\( 0 + 1 \cdot (2(-2) + y'') + 2 = 0 \Rightarrow -4 + y'' + 2 = 0 \Rightarrow y'' = 2 \). A1
Step 6 — (iii) Taylor series around x = 1
M1 A1
\( y(x) \approx y(1) + y'(1)(x-1) + \tfrac{y''(1)}{2}(x-1)^{2} = 0 - 2(x-1) + (x-1)^{2} \).
Step 7 — Validity
B1
Valid for \( x \) close to 1. Good approximation when \( |x - 1| \) is small.

M1 A1
