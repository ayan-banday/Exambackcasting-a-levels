Question 1 [6]
Gap drill · fresh
(i) Differentiate \(\tan^{-1}(2x)\) with respect to \(x\). [1] (ii) Hence, or otherwise, find \(\displaystyle\int \tan^{-1}(2x)\,\mathrm dx\). [3] (iii) Find the exact value of \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1/2} \tan^{-1}(2x)\,\mathrm dx\). [2]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) inverse-trig derivative, chain rule
MF27: \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm du}\tan^{-1}u=\frac{1}{1+u^2}\), here \(u=2x\) so \(u'=2\). \[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\tan^{-1}(2x)=\frac{2}{1+4x^2}. \](1)
(ii) the inverse-trig is \(u\) in by parts; \(\mathrm dv=\mathrm dx\)
A lone inverse-trig integrand is always integration by parts with the inverse function as \(u\) (you can only differentiate it, not integrate it) and \(\mathrm dv=\mathrm dx\), so \(v=x\). \[ \int \tan^{-1}(2x)\,\mathrm dx = x\tan^{-1}(2x)-\int x\cdot\frac{2}{1+4x^2}\,\mathrm dx. \](1) The remaining integral is \(\frac{f'}{f}\) form: \(\int\frac{2x}{1+4x^2}\,\mathrm dx=\frac14\int\frac{8x}{1+4x^2}\,\mathrm dx=\frac14\ln(1+4x^2)\). \[ = x\tan^{-1}(2x)-\frac14\ln(1+4x^2)+C. \](1)(1)
(iii) apply the limits
At \(x=\tfrac12\): \(\tan^{-1}1=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) and \(1+4(\tfrac14)=2\). At \(x=0\) both terms vanish. \[ \Big[x\tan^{-1}(2x)-\tfrac14\ln(1+4x^2)\Big]_{0}^{1/2}=\Big(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{4}-\tfrac14\ln 2\Big)-0=\frac{\pi}{8}-\frac{\ln 2}{4}. \](1)(1)
Closes: the #1 gap — a lone inverse-trig integral is integration by parts with the inverse function as \(u\) and \(\mathrm dv=\mathrm dx\); the leftover resolves as \(\frac{f'}{f}\to\ln\).

Solution

Question 3 [7]
Gap drill · fresh
The curve \(C\) has equation \(y=x^3 e^{-x}\). (i) Find the exact coordinates of the stationary points of \(C\). [3] (ii) Using the second derivative test, determine the nature of each stationary point. [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) product rule, set \(\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=0\)
\(y=x^3e^{-x}\): product of \(x^3\) (derivative \(3x^2\)) and \(e^{-x}\) (derivative \(-e^{-x}\)). \[ \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=3x^2e^{-x}-x^3e^{-x}=x^2e^{-x}(3-x). \](1) \(e^{-x}\neq0\), so \(x^2(3-x)=0\Rightarrow x=0\) or \(x=3\). \[ (0,0)\quad\text{and}\quad\big(3,\,27e^{-3}\big). \](1)(1)
(ii) second derivative
Differentiate \(\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=e^{-x}(3x^2-x^3)\) by the product rule again. \[ \frac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}=-e^{-x}(3x^2-x^3)+e^{-x}(6x-3x^2)=e^{-x}\big(x^3-6x^2+6x\big). \](1) At \(x=3\): \(e^{-3}(27-54+18)=-9e^{-3}<0\). \[ \frac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}\Big|_{x=3}<0\Rightarrow (3,27e^{-3})\text{ is a maximum.} \](1) At \(x=0\): \(\frac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}=0\), so the test is inconclusive — fall back to the first derivative test. Near \(x=0\), \(\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=x^2e^{-x}(3-x)\): \(x^2\ge0\) and \(3-x>0\), so \(\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}\ge0\) on both sides. \[ (0,0)\text{ is a stationary point of inflexion.} \](1)(1)
Closes: the second-derivative test end to end — including the trap where \(\frac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}=0\) forces you back to the first-derivative sign test.

Question 4 [8]
Gap drill · fresh
A curve \(C\) has parametric equations \(x=\cos^2\theta\), \(y=\theta+\tfrac12\sin 2\theta\), for \(0\le\theta\le\tfrac12\pi\). (i) Show that \(\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=-\cot\theta\). [3] (ii) The normal to \(C\) at the point where \(\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) meets the \(x\)-axis at the point \(A\). Find the exact coordinates of \(A\). [5]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) parametric differentiation, then simplify with double-angle identities
\(x=\cos^2\theta\Rightarrow\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}=2\cos\theta(-\sin\theta)=-\sin 2\theta\). \(y=\theta+\tfrac12\sin 2\theta\Rightarrow\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm d\theta}=1+\cos 2\theta\). \[ \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{1+\cos 2\theta}{-\sin 2\theta}. \](1)(1) Use \(1+\cos 2\theta=2\cos^2\theta\) and \(\sin 2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta\). \[ =\frac{2\cos^2\theta}{-2\sin\theta\cos\theta}=-\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}=-\cot\theta. \](1)
(ii) gradient of the normal = \(-1/(\text{gradient of tangent})\)
At \(\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\): tangent gradient \(=-\cot\tfrac{\pi}{4}=-1\), so normal gradient \(=-\frac{1}{-1}=1\). \[ m_{\text{normal}}=1. \](1) The point on \(C\) — state both coordinates: \(x=\cos^2\tfrac{\pi}{4}=\tfrac12\); \(y=\tfrac{\pi}{4}+\tfrac12\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}=\tfrac{\pi}{4}+\tfrac12\). \[ \left(\tfrac12,\ \tfrac{\pi}{4}+\tfrac12\right). \](1) Normal: \(y-\big(\tfrac{\pi}{4}+\tfrac12\big)=1\big(x-\tfrac12\big)\). Meets the \(x\)-axis where \(y=0\). \[ 0-\big(\tfrac{\pi}{4}+\tfrac12\big)=x-\tfrac12\ \Rightarrow\ x=\tfrac12-\tfrac{\pi}{4}-\tfrac12=-\frac{\pi}{4}. \](1)(1) \[ A=\left(-\frac{\pi}{4},\,0\right). \](1)
Closes: parametric normal — normal gradient is \(-1/(\text{tangent gradient})\), a point means both \(x\) and \(y\), and the double-angle identities that collapse the gradient.

Question 5 [8]

A curve \(C\) has parametric equations \(x=2(\theta-\sin\theta)\), \(y=2(1-\cos\theta)\), for \(0\le\theta\le 2\pi\). (i) Show that \(\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\cot\tfrac{\theta}{2}\). [3] (ii) Find the exact area of the region bounded by \(C\) and the \(x\)-axis. [5]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) half-angle identities
\(\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}=2(1-\cos\theta)\), \(\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm d\theta}=2\sin\theta\). \[ \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{2\sin\theta}{2(1-\cos\theta)}=\frac{\sin\theta}{1-\cos\theta}. \](1) Use \(\sin\theta=2\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\) and \(1-\cos\theta=2\sin^2\tfrac{\theta}{2}\). \[ =\frac{2\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}}{2\sin^2\tfrac{\theta}{2}}=\frac{\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}}{\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}}=\cot\tfrac{\theta}{2}. \](1)(1)
(ii) parametric area — \(\int y\,\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}\,\mathrm d\theta\)
For a curve given parametrically, area under it \(=\displaystyle\int y\,\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}\,\mathrm d\theta\): swap \(\mathrm dx\) for \(\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}\,\mathrm d\theta\) so everything is in \(\theta\). Limits \(\theta:0\to2\pi\). \[ \text{Area}=\int_{0}^{2\pi} 2(1-\cos\theta)\cdot 2(1-\cos\theta)\,\mathrm d\theta=4\int_{0}^{2\pi}(1-\cos\theta)^2\,\mathrm d\theta. \](1)(1) Expand and drop the square with \(\cos^2\theta=\tfrac12(1+\cos 2\theta)\): \((1-\cos\theta)^2=1-2\cos\theta+\cos^2\theta=\tfrac32-2\cos\theta+\tfrac12\cos 2\theta\). \[ 4\Big[\tfrac32\theta-2\sin\theta+\tfrac14\sin 2\theta\Big]_{0}^{2\pi}=4\big(3\pi\big)=12\pi\ \text{units}^2. \](1)(1)
Closes: the parametric-area gap — \(\int y\,\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}\,\mathrm d\theta\) — plus \(1-\cos\theta=2\sin^2\tfrac{\theta}{2}\) and the \(\cos^2\) double angle you keep needing.

Gap drill · fresh
A hemispherical bowl of radius \(6\) cm is modelled by rotating the lower half of the circle \(x^2+(y-6)^2=36\) about the \(y\)-axis. Water is poured in; the depth measured from the bottom of the bowl is \(h\) cm, where \(0\le h\le 6\). (i) Show, by integration, that the volume of water is \(V=\pi\!\left(6h^2-\tfrac13 h^3\right)\). [4] (ii) Water is poured in at a constant rate of \(4\) cm\(^3\)s\(^{-1}\). Find the exact rate at which the depth is increasing at the instant when \(h=3\). [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) volume of revolution about the \(y\)-axis: \(V=\pi\int x^2\,\mathrm dy\)
Rotating about the \(y\)-axis makes \(x\) the radius of each disc, so integrate \(\pi x^2\) with respect to \(y\). From the circle, \(x^2=36-(y-6)^2\). Water fills from \(y=0\) up to \(y=h\). \[ V=\pi\int_{0}^{h}\big[36-(y-6)^2\big]\,\mathrm dy=\pi\Big[36y-\tfrac13(y-6)^3\Big]_{0}^{h}. \](1)(1) At \(y=0\): \(-\tfrac13(-6)^3=\tfrac{216}{3}=72\). Expand \((h-6)^3=h^3-18h^2+108h-216\). \[ V=\pi\Big[36h-\tfrac13(h-6)^3-72\Big]=\pi\Big[36h-\tfrac13 h^3+6h^2-36h+72-72\Big]=\pi\!\left(6h^2-\tfrac13 h^3\right). \](1)(1)
(ii) connected rates: \(\frac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}\div\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dh}\)
\(\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=4\) (given, poured in); \(\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dh}=\pi(12h-h^2)\). Chain rule: \(\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dh}\cdot\frac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}\). \[ \frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dh}=\pi\big(12h-h^2\big),\qquad \frac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{4}{\pi(12h-h^2)}. \](1)(1) At \(h=3\): \(12(3)-3^2=27\). \[ \frac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{4}{27\pi}\ \text{cm s}^{-1}. \](1)
Closes: the bowl — prove a volume by rotating a curve about the \(y\)-axis (\(\pi\int x^2\,\mathrm dy\)), then feed \(\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dh}\) into a connected rate of change.

Question 7 [7]
Gap drill · fresh
(i) Show that \(\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\ln\!\big(x+\sqrt{x^2+4}\,\big)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+4}}\). [2] (ii) The region \(R\) is bounded by the curve \(y=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{x^2+4}}\), the \(x\)-axis, and the lines \(x=0\) and \(x=2\). Using the substitution \(x=2\tan\theta\), find the exact area of \(R\). [5]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) differentiate the log to expose the integrand
\(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\ln f=\frac{f'}{f}\) with \(f=x+\sqrt{x^2+4}\), \(f'=1+\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+4}}=\frac{\sqrt{x^2+4}+x}{\sqrt{x^2+4}}\). \[ \frac{f'}{f}=\frac{\sqrt{x^2+4}+x}{\sqrt{x^2+4}}\cdot\frac{1}{x+\sqrt{x^2+4}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+4}}. \](1)(1)
(ii) the three substitutions: \(\mathrm dx\), the surd, the limits
\(x=2\tan\theta\Rightarrow\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}=2\sec^2\theta\), so \(\mathrm dx=2\sec^2\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\). Surd: \(x^2+4=4\tan^2\theta+4=4\sec^2\theta\) (using \(1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta\)), so \(\sqrt{x^2+4}=2\sec\theta\). Limits: \(x=0\Rightarrow\theta=0\); \(x=2\Rightarrow\tan\theta=1\Rightarrow\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\). \[ \text{Area}=\int_{0}^{2}\frac{2}{\sqrt{x^2+4}}\,\mathrm dx=\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\frac{2}{2\sec\theta}\cdot 2\sec^2\theta\,\mathrm d\theta=\int_{0}^{\pi/4}2\sec\theta\,\mathrm d\theta. \](1)(1) MF27: \(\int\sec\theta\,\mathrm d\theta=\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|\). At \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\): \(\sec\tfrac{\pi}{4}=\sqrt2\), \(\tan\tfrac{\pi}{4}=1\). \[ =2\Big[\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|\Big]_{0}^{\pi/4}=2\big(\ln(\sqrt2+1)-\ln 1\big)=2\ln(1+\sqrt2)\ \text{units}^2. \](1)(1)(1)
Closes: the \(x=a\tan\theta\) substitution done in full — change \(\mathrm dx\), collapse the surd with \(1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta\), and change the limits; plus differentiating a log to expose \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+a^2}}\).

Question 8 [8]

(a) Find \(\displaystyle\int x^2 e^{-3x}\,\mathrm dx\). [4] (b) Hence obtain a formula for \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{n} x^2 e^{-3x}\,\mathrm dx\) in terms of \(n\), where \(n>0\). [2] (c) Deduce the exact value of \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty} x^2 e^{-3x}\,\mathrm dx\), given that \(n^k e^{-3n}\to 0\) as \(n\to\infty\). [2]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(a) integration by parts twice
\(u=x^2\) (simpler when differentiated), \(\mathrm dv=e^{-3x}\mathrm dx\Rightarrow v=-\tfrac13 e^{-3x}\). \[ \int x^2 e^{-3x}\,\mathrm dx=-\tfrac13 x^2 e^{-3x}+\tfrac23\int x e^{-3x}\,\mathrm dx. \](1) Again on \(\int x e^{-3x}\mathrm dx\): \(u=x\), \(\mathrm dv=e^{-3x}\mathrm dx\Rightarrow v=-\tfrac13 e^{-3x}\); \(\int xe^{-3x}=-\tfrac13 xe^{-3x}-\tfrac19 e^{-3x}\). \[ =-\tfrac13 x^2 e^{-3x}-\tfrac29 x e^{-3x}-\tfrac{2}{27}e^{-3x}+C=-\frac{e^{-3x}}{27}\big(9x^2+6x+2\big)+C. \](1)(1)(1)
(b) apply the limits \(0\) to \(n\)
At \(x=0\): \(-\tfrac{1}{27}(2)=-\tfrac{2}{27}\). \[ \int_{0}^{n} x^2 e^{-3x}\,\mathrm dx=\frac{2}{27}-\frac{e^{-3n}}{27}\big(9n^2+6n+2\big). \](1)(1)
(c) let \(n\to\infty\)
Each \(n^k e^{-3n}\to0\), so the whole second term vanishes. \[ \int_{0}^{\infty} x^2 e^{-3x}\,\mathrm dx=\frac{2}{27}. \](1)(1)
Closes: repeated by-parts building a formula in \(n\), then reading off the limit as \(n\to\infty\) — the Q13/Q14 pair collapsed into one.

Question 9 [7]
Gap drill · fresh
The integral is \(I=\displaystyle\int x\sqrt{2-x}\,\mathrm dx\). (i) Find \(I\) using integration by parts. [3] (ii) Find \(I\) using the substitution \(u^2=2-x\). [3] (iii) Show that your two answers differ only by a constant. [1]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) choose \(u\) = the part that gets simpler when differentiated
Both factors are algebraic, so the cardinal rule decides: take \(u=x\) (derivative \(1\), simpler) and \(\mathrm dv=(2-x)^{1/2}\mathrm dx\Rightarrow v=-\tfrac23(2-x)^{3/2}\). \[ I=-\tfrac23 x(2-x)^{3/2}+\tfrac23\int(2-x)^{3/2}\,\mathrm dx=-\tfrac23 x(2-x)^{3/2}-\tfrac{4}{15}(2-x)^{5/2}+C. \](1)(1)(1)
(ii) substitution — change everything to \(u\)
\(u^2=2-x\Rightarrow x=2-u^2\), \(\mathrm dx=-2u\,\mathrm du\), \(\sqrt{2-x}=u\). Replace \(x\), the surd and \(\mathrm dx\). \[ I=\int(2-u^2)(u)(-2u)\,\mathrm du=-2\int(2u^2-u^4)\,\mathrm du=-\tfrac43 u^3+\tfrac25 u^5+C. \](1)(1) Back-substitute \(u=(2-x)^{1/2}\). \[ I=-\tfrac43(2-x)^{3/2}+\tfrac25(2-x)^{5/2}+C. \](1)
(iii) reconcile
Put \(t=(2-x)^{1/2}\), so \(x=2-t^2\), in the (i) answer: \(-\tfrac23(2-t^2)t^3-\tfrac{4}{15}t^5=-\tfrac43 t^3+\tfrac23 t^5-\tfrac{4}{15}t^5=-\tfrac43 t^3+\tfrac25 t^5\). \[ \text{Identical to (ii): the two antiderivatives differ by }0\text{, a constant.} \](1)
Closes: choosing \(u\) by "simpler when differentiated", changing every \(x\) (including inside \(\mathrm dx\)) to \(u\), and reconciling two methods up to a constant.

Question 10 [7]
Gap drill · fresh
(a) Express \(\dfrac{5x^2-5x+6}{(x-2)(x^2+4)}\) in partial fractions. [3] (b) Hence find \(\displaystyle\int \frac{5x^2-5x+6}{(x-2)(x^2+4)}\,\mathrm dx\). [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(a) linear factor over an irreducible quadratic factor
The quadratic \(x^2+4\) does not factorise, so its numerator is \(Bx+C\): \(\frac{5x^2-5x+6}{(x-2)(x^2+4)}=\frac{A}{x-2}+\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+4}\), i.e. \(5x^2-5x+6=A(x^2+4)+(Bx+C)(x-2)\). \(x=2\): \(20-10+6=16=8A\Rightarrow A=2\). Compare \(x^2\): \(5=A+B\Rightarrow B=3\). Compare constants: \(6=4A-2C\Rightarrow C=1\). \[ \frac{5x^2-5x+6}{(x-2)(x^2+4)}=\frac{2}{x-2}+\frac{3x+1}{x^2+4}. \](1)(1)(1)
(b) split the quadratic piece into \(\frac{f'}{f}\) (log) + constant (arctan)
\(\int\frac{2}{x-2}\mathrm dx=2\ln|x-2|\). Split \(\frac{3x+1}{x^2+4}=\frac{3x}{x^2+4}+\frac{1}{x^2+4}\): first is \(\frac32\cdot\frac{2x}{x^2+4}\to\frac32\ln(x^2+4)\); second is MF27 \(\int\frac{1}{x^2+a^2}\mathrm dx=\frac1a\tan^{-1}\frac{x}{a}\) with \(a=2\). \[ \int\frac{5x^2-5x+6}{(x-2)(x^2+4)}\,\mathrm dx=2\ln|x-2|+\tfrac32\ln(x^2+4)+\tfrac12\tan^{-1}\!\tfrac{x}{2}+C. \](1)(1)(1)(1)
Closes: partial fractions with an irreducible quadratic, then splitting \(\frac{3x+1}{x^2+4}\) into a \(\frac{f'}{f}\) log and an arctan standard form.
