Toolkit — the identities and formulas you will need
Unit vector \( \hat{\mathbf{v}} \)\( \dfrac{\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|} \). Has magnitude exactly 1: \( |\hat{\mathbf{v}}| = |\mathbf{v}|/|\mathbf{v}| = 1 \). Strips length, keeps direction.
Scalar projection of \( \mathbf{u} \) onto \( \mathbf{w} \)\( \mathbf{u}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{w}} = \dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w}}{|\mathbf{w}|} \)   — a signed length (the "shadow length" of \(\mathbf{u}\) along \(\mathbf{w}\))
Vector projection of \( \mathbf{u} \) onto \( \mathbf{w} \)\( (\mathbf{u}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{w}})\,\hat{\mathbf{w}} \)   — a vector (the shadow itself)
Perpendicular-component magnitude\( |\mathbf{u}\times\hat{\mathbf{w}}| \)   = \( \sqrt{|\mathbf{u}|^{2} - (\mathbf{u}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{w}})^{2}} \) by Pythagoras
Ratio theorem — \(P\) divides \(AB\) in ratio \(\lambda:\mu\)\( \overrightarrow{OP} = \dfrac{\mu\mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b}}{\lambda + \mu} \)   — weight goes with the OPPOSITE endpoint
External point (extended segment)Ratio theorem does NOT apply directly. Use \( \overrightarrow{AQ} = \overrightarrow{OQ} - \overrightarrow{OA} \) with the given geometric condition, then solve for \(\overrightarrow{OQ}\).
Distinctness of two points \(P, Q\)\( P \neq Q \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \overrightarrow{PQ} \neq \mathbf{0} \). Proof: if \( \overrightarrow{PQ} = \mathbf{q} - \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{0} \) then \(\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{q}\), so they are the same point.
Collinearity of \(A, B, C\)\( \overrightarrow{AB} = k\,\overrightarrow{AC} \) for some scalar \(k\), AND they share the point \(A\). Equivalent: \( \overrightarrow{AB} \times \overrightarrow{AC} = \mathbf{0} \) with both non-zero.
Cross-product self-identity\( \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0} \)   (from \( |\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{a}| = |\mathbf{a}|^{2}\sin 0 = 0 \))
Dot-product self-identity\( \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a} = |\mathbf{a}|^{2}\cos 0 = |\mathbf{a}|^{2} \)
Cross product zero \(\Rightarrow\) parallelIf \( \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0} \) and both are non-zero, then \( \mathbf{a} \parallel \mathbf{b} \).
Two-different-ways method (intersection)Write \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) along one line as \( \mathbf{a} + t(\text{dir}_1) \); along the other as \( \mathbf{b} + s(\text{dir}_2) \). Equate, compare coefficients of independent vectors, solve.

Question 1

Question
Consider the vectors \( \mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} \), \( \mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 0 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} \), \( \mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \).

(i) Find the exact magnitude of each vector. [3]
(ii) Write down the unit vector in the direction of each. [3]
(iii) By computing \( |\hat{\mathbf{u}}|^{2} \), \( |\hat{\mathbf{v}}|^{2} \) and \( |\hat{\mathbf{w}}|^{2} \) directly, verify that each unit vector has magnitude 1. [3]
Solution
Step 1 — (i) Magnitudes
A1 A1 A1
\( |\mathbf{u}| = \sqrt{36 + 4 + 9} = \sqrt{49} = 7 \)
\( |\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{16 + 0 + 9} = \sqrt{25} = 5 \)
\( |\mathbf{w}| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = \sqrt{9} = 3 \)
Step 2 — (ii) Unit vectors
A1 A1 A1
\[ \hat{\mathbf{u}} = \dfrac{1}{7}\begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{v}} = \dfrac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 0 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{w}} = \dfrac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \]
Step 3 — (iii) Verify magnitude = 1
M1 M1 A1
\( |\hat{\mathbf{u}}|^{2} = \dfrac{36 + 4 + 9}{49} = \dfrac{49}{49} = 1 \) ✓
\( |\hat{\mathbf{v}}|^{2} = \dfrac{16 + 0 + 9}{25} = \dfrac{25}{25} = 1 \) ✓
\( |\hat{\mathbf{w}}|^{2} = \dfrac{1 + 4 + 4}{9} = \dfrac{9}{9} = 1 \) ✓
Why the magnitude is exactly 1: when you divide any vector \(\mathbf{v}\) by its own length \(|\mathbf{v}|\), each component is scaled by the same factor \(1/|\mathbf{v}|\). Under \(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}}\), that factor pulls out as \(1/|\mathbf{v}|\), leaving \(|\mathbf{v}|/|\mathbf{v}| = 1\). This is the whole point of a unit vector — it strips length so only direction remains.

You need to know both the mechanic (divide by magnitude) AND the reason (result has magnitude 1, giving pure direction). Every projection / foot-of-perpendicular / line-equation question downstream leans on the unit-vector concept.

Question 2

Question
The point \( A \) has position vector \( \mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \\ 12 \end{pmatrix} \) referred to the origin \( O \).

(i) Find the unit vector in the direction of \( \overrightarrow{OA} \). [2]
(ii) The point \( B \) lies on the ray from \( O \) through \( A \) such that \( |\overrightarrow{OB}| = 26 \) units. Find the position vector of \( B \). [3]
(iii) The point \( C \) lies on the ray from \( O \) through \( A \) such that \( |\overrightarrow{OC}| = 1 \). State the position vector of \( C \). [1]
Solution
Step 1 — (i) Unit vector
M1 A1
\( |\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{16 + 9 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13 \). \[ \hat{\mathbf{a}} = \dfrac{1}{13}\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \\ 12 \end{pmatrix} \]
Step 2 — (ii) Scale unit vector by target length
M1 M1 A1
Since \(B\) is on the ray through \(A\), \(\overrightarrow{OB}\) is in the direction of \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}\). Its magnitude is 26. \[ \overrightarrow{OB} = 26\,\hat{\mathbf{a}} = 26 \cdot \dfrac{1}{13}\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \\ 12 \end{pmatrix} = 2\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \\ 12 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ -6 \\ 24 \end{pmatrix} \]
Step 3 — (iii) Length-1 case
B1
\( \overrightarrow{OC} = 1 \cdot \hat{\mathbf{a}} = \hat{\mathbf{a}} = \dfrac{1}{13}\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \\ 12 \end{pmatrix} \).
What you're using: the unit vector is the "1-unit step" in a given direction. Multiply it by any distance \(d\) to get a point at exactly distance \(d\) from the origin along that direction. This is exactly the mechanic behind the vector equation of a line \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + \lambda\hat{\mathbf{d}}\) — Cluster 2 uses it constantly.
Solution hidden — click Reveal
Archetype revealed
Unit vector as "1-unit step in a direction" — application.

This is why the unit vector matters: it decouples direction from length so you can rebuild any length you want. Locking this is the pre-requisite for lines and planes.

Question 3

Question
Vectors \( \mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \) and \( \mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \).

(i) Find the exact scalar projection of \( \mathbf{u} \) onto \( \mathbf{w} \). [2]
(ii) Show that this scalar projection equals \( |\mathbf{u}| \cos\theta \), where \(\theta\) is the angle between \( \mathbf{u} \) and \( \mathbf{w} \). [3]
(iii) State, in words, what the scalar projection represents geometrically. [1]
Solution
Step 1 — (i) Scalar projection
M1 A1
\( \hat{\mathbf{w}} = \dfrac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \). \[ \mathbf{u} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{w}} = (3)(1) + (4)(0) + (0)(0) = 3 \]
Step 2 — (ii) Show equals \(|\mathbf{u}|\cos\theta\)
M1 M1 A1
\( |\mathbf{u}| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5 \), \( |\mathbf{w}| = 5 \).
\[ \cos\theta = \dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w}}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{w}|} = \dfrac{15}{25} = \dfrac{3}{5} \] Hence \( |\mathbf{u}|\cos\theta = 5 \cdot \dfrac{3}{5} = 3 \), matching the scalar projection from (i). ✓
Step 3 — (iii) Geometric meaning
B1
The scalar projection is the signed length of the shadow of \(\mathbf{u}\) cast onto the line through \(\mathbf{w}\) when light shines perpendicular to \(\mathbf{w}\). It is a signed number, positive when \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\) point in similar directions, negative when they point oppositely.
Why the formula works: In right-angled trigonometry, the adjacent side of a right triangle with hypotenuse \(|\mathbf{u}|\) and included angle \(\theta\) has length \(|\mathbf{u}|\cos\theta\). Since \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w} = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{w}|\cos\theta\), dividing by \(|\mathbf{w}|\) leaves exactly \(|\mathbf{u}|\cos\theta\) — the adjacent side, i.e. the shadow. The unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}\) just does the "divide by \(|\mathbf{w}|\)" cleanly, so \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{w}}\) is the whole recipe in one step.
Solution hidden — click Reveal
Archetype revealed
Scalar projection — formula, derivation, geometric meaning.

Two facts that must be locked: (1) the formula \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{w}}\) is just the right-triangle "adjacent = hypotenuse × cos θ" with the \(|\mathbf{w}|\) division baked in; (2) the value is a signed length, not a vector.

Question 4

Question
Vectors \( \mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \) and \( \mathbf{n} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \).

(i) Find the vector projection \(\mathbf{p}\) of \(\mathbf{u}\) onto \(\mathbf{n}\). [2]
(ii) Let \(\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{p}\). Show that \(\mathbf{q}\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{n}\). [2]
(iii) Verify that \(|\mathbf{u}|^{2} = |\mathbf{p}|^{2} + |\mathbf{q}|^{2}\), and explain briefly what this says geometrically. [3]
Solution
Step 1 — (i) Vector projection
M1 A1
\( |\mathbf{n}| = 1 \), so \( \hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{n} \). Scalar projection: \( \mathbf{u}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}} = 2 \). \[ \mathbf{p} = (\mathbf{u}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})\,\hat{\mathbf{n}} = 2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \]
Step 2 — (ii) Perpendicular component
M1 A1
\( \mathbf{q} = \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \).
\( \mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{n} = (0)(1) + (3)(0) + (6)(0) = 0 \), so \( \mathbf{q} \perp \mathbf{n} \). ✓
Step 3 — (iii) Pythagoras
M1 A1 B1
\( |\mathbf{u}|^{2} = 4 + 9 + 36 = 49 \).   \( |\mathbf{p}|^{2} = 4 \).   \( |\mathbf{q}|^{2} = 0 + 9 + 36 = 45 \).
Sum: \( 4 + 45 = 49 = |\mathbf{u}|^{2} \). ✓
Geometrically: \(\mathbf{u}\) is decomposed into two perpendicular pieces — the shadow \(\mathbf{p}\) along \(\mathbf{n}\), and the perpendicular residue \(\mathbf{q}\). Pythagoras on the right-angled triangle formed by these gives the length identity.
Trap: \(\mathbf{p}\) is a vector (the shadow itself). Scalar projection is just its length \((\mathbf{u}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})\). Reading the question wording carefully is the difference between a scalar and a vector answer.
Solution hidden — click Reveal
Archetype revealed
Vector projection + perpendicular decomposition + Pythagoras.

This is the identity that produces perpendicular distances in every foot-of-perpendicular question: \(|PN|^{2} = |\overrightarrow{OP}|^{2} - |\overrightarrow{ON}|^{2}\). Same shape as here.

Question 5

Question
Points \(A\) and \(B\) have position vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) referred to the origin \(O\). Point \(P\) divides \(AB\) internally in the ratio \(\lambda : \mu\), i.e. \( AP : PB = \lambda : \mu \).

(i) By first expressing \(\overrightarrow{AP}\) as a scalar multiple of \(\overrightarrow{AB}\), show that \[ \overrightarrow{OP} = \dfrac{\mu\mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b}}{\lambda + \mu} \; . \] [4]
(ii) Deduce the midpoint formula \( \overrightarrow{OM} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}) \) as the special case \(\lambda = \mu\). [2]
Solution
Step 1 — (i) \(\overrightarrow{AP}\) as a fraction of \(\overrightarrow{AB}\)
M1
\( AP : PB = \lambda : \mu \), so \(AP = \dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda + \mu} \cdot AB\).
In vectors: \( \overrightarrow{AP} = \dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda + \mu}\,\overrightarrow{AB} \).
Step 2 — Express \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) via \(\overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{AP}\)
M1 M1
\[ \overrightarrow{OP} = \overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{AP} = \mathbf{a} + \dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda + \mu}(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}) \] Put over common denominator \(\lambda + \mu\): \[ = \dfrac{(\lambda + \mu)\mathbf{a} + \lambda(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a})}{\lambda + \mu} = \dfrac{(\lambda + \mu)\mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b} - \lambda\mathbf{a}}{\lambda + \mu} \]
Step 3 — Simplify
A1
\[ = \dfrac{\mu\mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b}}{\lambda + \mu} \quad \text{(shown)} \]
Step 4 — (ii) Midpoint as special case
M1 A1
Setting \(\lambda = \mu\) (so \(AM : MB = 1 : 1\)): \[ \overrightarrow{OM} = \dfrac{\lambda\mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b}}{2\lambda} = \dfrac{\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}}{2} \quad \text{(shown)} \]
Why "weight opposite endpoint": in the final line, the coefficient of \(\mathbf{a}\) is \(\mu\) (the label attached to \(PB\), i.e. the side away from \(A\)). Similarly the coefficient of \(\mathbf{b}\) is \(\lambda\) (the label attached to \(AP\), the side away from \(B\)). Reversing this is the single most common ratio-theorem slip.
Solution hidden — click Reveal
Archetype revealed
Ratio theorem — first-principles derivation.

Deriving it from \( \overrightarrow{OP} = \overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{AP} \) locks the mechanic. If you can rebuild it on demand, you can never forget the weight-swap rule or apply it in the wrong direction.

Question 6

Question
Points \(A\) and \(B\) have position vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) referred to the origin \(O\).

(i) Point \(R\) lies on \(AB\) extended beyond \(B\), such that \( \overrightarrow{AR} = 3\,\overrightarrow{AB} \). Find \(\overrightarrow{OR}\) in terms of \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\). [3]
(ii) Point \(S\) lies on \(BA\) extended beyond \(A\), such that \(A\) is the midpoint of segment \(SB\). Find \(\overrightarrow{OS}\) in terms of \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\). [3]
Solution
Step 1 — (i) Use the given vector relation
M1 M1 A1
\( \overrightarrow{AR} = 3\overrightarrow{AB} \Longrightarrow \overrightarrow{OR} - \overrightarrow{OA} = 3(\overrightarrow{OB} - \overrightarrow{OA}) \) \[ \overrightarrow{OR} = \mathbf{a} + 3(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}) = 3\mathbf{b} - 2\mathbf{a} \]
Step 2 — (ii) Use midpoint definition backwards
M1 M1 A1
\(A\) is midpoint of \(SB\), so \( \overrightarrow{OA} = \dfrac{\overrightarrow{OS} + \overrightarrow{OB}}{2} \). \[ 2\mathbf{a} = \overrightarrow{OS} + \mathbf{b} \;\Longrightarrow\; \overrightarrow{OS} = 2\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \]
Why not ratio theorem here: the ratio theorem in the form \( \overrightarrow{OP} = (\mu\mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b})/(\lambda+\mu) \) applies only when \(P\) lies internally on \(AB\). For an external point, always fall back to \( \overrightarrow{AR} = \overrightarrow{OR} - \overrightarrow{OA} \) or a midpoint relation — first principles.
Solution hidden — click Reveal
Archetype revealed
External points on an extended segment — first principles, not ratio theorem.

Two shapes: "\(AR = k \cdot AB\)" (unwrap via subtraction) and "X is the midpoint of YZ" (unwrap via the midpoint formula). Never force ratio theorem on an external point — you will get sign errors.

Question 7

Question
Referred to the origin \(O\), the points \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\) have position vectors \( \mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \), \( \mathbf{q} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} \), \( \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -4 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \).

(i) Show that \(P\), \(Q\) and \(R\) are all distinct. [2]
(ii) Show that \(P\), \(Q\) and \(R\) are collinear. [3]
Solution
Step 1 — (i) Distinctness via displacement
M1 A1
\( \overrightarrow{PQ} = \mathbf{q} - \mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \neq \mathbf{0} \), so \(P \neq Q\).
\( \overrightarrow{QR} = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{q} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ -4 \\ -4 \end{pmatrix} \neq \mathbf{0} \), so \(Q \neq R\).
\( \overrightarrow{PR} = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} \neq \mathbf{0} \), so \(P \neq R\).
Therefore \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\) are all distinct.
Step 2 — (ii) Collinearity via parallel displacement
M1 M1 A1
Compare \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) and \(\overrightarrow{PR}\): \[ \overrightarrow{PR} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} = -1 \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = -\overrightarrow{PQ} \] So \( \overrightarrow{PR} = -1 \cdot \overrightarrow{PQ} \), i.e. they are parallel (scalar multiple) and share the point \(P\). \[ \therefore P, Q, R \text{ are collinear.} \]
Why \(\overrightarrow{PQ} = \mathbf{0}\) implies same point: \( \overrightarrow{PQ} = \mathbf{q} - \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{0} \) means \(\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{q}\), so \(P\) and \(Q\) have identical coordinates — they are the same point. The contrapositive: \(P \neq Q \Longleftrightarrow \overrightarrow{PQ} \neq \mathbf{0}\).
Solution hidden — click Reveal
Archetype revealed
Distinctness + collinearity in one question.

Distinctness is the "0 marks" precondition for collinearity: you must show the vectors involved are non-zero before you can invoke "parallel + shared point." Skipping distinctness is a corpus-flagged mark loss.

Question 8

Question
Vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) are non-parallel and non-zero.

(i) By using the distributive property and the identity \( \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \), simplify \( (\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) \times (\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}) \). [3]
(ii) Given that \(|\mathbf{a}| = 3\), \(|\mathbf{b}| = 2\), and the angle between \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) is \(60^{\circ}\), find the exact value of \(|(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) \times (\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b})|\). [3]
Solution
Step 1 — (i) Expand using distributivity
M1 M1 A1
\[ (\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) \times (\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{b} \] Using \( \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \) and \( \mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{a} = -\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} \): \[ = \mathbf{0} - \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{0} = -2(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}) \]
Step 2 — (ii) Apply magnitude
M1 M1 A1
\[ |\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}| = |\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\sin\theta = (3)(2)\sin 60^{\circ} = 6 \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 3\sqrt{3} \] \[ |(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) \times (\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b})| = |-2(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})| = 2 \cdot 3\sqrt{3} = 6\sqrt{3} \]
Why \(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\): from the definition \(|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{v}| = |\mathbf{v}|^{2}\sin 0^{\circ} = 0\). A cross product with a zero magnitude is the zero vector. Similarly \(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{v}|^{2}\cos 0^{\circ} = |\mathbf{v}|^{2}\).
Solution hidden — click Reveal
Archetype revealed
Cross-product identity manipulation — leans on \(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\) and anti-commutativity.

These two facts are foundational — a JC paper takes them as memorised, no reminder given. Expand distributively, kill the self-crosses, collect the sign-flipped terms. Same shape appears in every cross-product identity proof.

Question 9

Question
In triangle \(OAB\), \(\overrightarrow{OA} = \mathbf{a}\) and \(\overrightarrow{OB} = \mathbf{b}\). Point \(M\) is the midpoint of \(OA\). Point \(N\) divides \(OB\) internally in the ratio \(ON : NB = 1 : 2\). Lines \(AN\) and \(BM\) intersect at \(P\).

By expressing \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) in two different ways, find \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) in terms of \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\). [6]
Solution
Step 1 — Locate M and N
B1
\( \overrightarrow{OM} = \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{a} \),   \( \overrightarrow{ON} = \tfrac{1}{3}\mathbf{b} \).
Step 2 — \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) along AN
M1
\(P\) lies on \(AN\), so \[ \overrightarrow{OP} = \overrightarrow{OA} + t\,\overrightarrow{AN} = \mathbf{a} + t\!\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}\right) = (1-t)\mathbf{a} + \tfrac{t}{3}\mathbf{b} \quad \text{…(1)} \]
Step 3 — \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) along BM
M1
\(P\) lies on \(BM\), so \[ \overrightarrow{OP} = \overrightarrow{OB} + s\,\overrightarrow{BM} = \mathbf{b} + s\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\right) = \tfrac{s}{2}\mathbf{a} + (1-s)\mathbf{b} \quad \text{…(2)} \]
Step 4 — Equate coefficients
M1
Since \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) are non-parallel, by comparison: \[ 1 - t = \tfrac{s}{2} \quad \text{…(3)}, \qquad \tfrac{t}{3} = 1 - s \quad \text{…(4)} \]
Step 5 — Solve, substitute
M1 A1
From (4): \( s = 1 - \tfrac{t}{3} \). Substitute into (3): \( 1 - t = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(1 - \tfrac{t}{3}\right) = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{t}{6} \).
Multiply by 6: \( 6 - 6t = 3 - t \;\Longrightarrow\; 5t = 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; t = \tfrac{3}{5} \). Then \( s = 1 - \tfrac{1}{5} = \tfrac{4}{5} \).
Substitute into (1): \( \overrightarrow{OP} = \tfrac{2}{5}\mathbf{a} + \tfrac{1}{5}\mathbf{b} = \tfrac{1}{5}(2\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) \).
Why comparison works: \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) are non-parallel, so they form a basis for the plane. Any vector in the plane has a unique decomposition as \(\alpha\mathbf{a} + \beta\mathbf{b}\). Two such decompositions of the same vector must have matching \(\alpha\)'s and matching \(\beta\)'s — that's the "by comparison" step. If \(\mathbf{a}\parallel\mathbf{b}\), this fails.
Solution hidden — click Reveal
Archetype revealed
Two-different-ways intersection method — cevians in a triangle.

The standard method for any "two lines intersect at P, find P" question in position-vector form. Write \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) along each line with its own parameter, equate, compare coefficients, solve.

Question 10

Question
The point \(P\) has position vector \(\mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}\) referred to the origin \(O\). The line \(l\) passes through \(O\) in the direction of the unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{d}} = \dfrac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\).

(i) Let \(N\) be the foot of the perpendicular from \(P\) to \(l\). Show that \(\overrightarrow{ON} = (\mathbf{p} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{d}})\,\hat{\mathbf{d}}\). [3]
(ii) Hence find the exact position vector of \(N\). [2]
(iii) Find the exact perpendicular distance from \(P\) to \(l\). [3]
Solution
Step 1 — (i) Two equations for N
M1 M1 A1
\(N\) lies on \(l\), so \(\overrightarrow{ON} = \lambda\,\hat{\mathbf{d}}\) for some \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\).
\(PN \perp l\), so \(\overrightarrow{NP} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{d}} = 0\): \[ (\mathbf{p} - \lambda\hat{\mathbf{d}}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{d}} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf{p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{d}} - \lambda(\hat{\mathbf{d}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{d}}) = 0 \] Since \(|\hat{\mathbf{d}}| = 1\), \(\hat{\mathbf{d}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{d}} = 1\), so \(\lambda = \mathbf{p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{d}}\). \[ \therefore \overrightarrow{ON} = (\mathbf{p}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{d}})\,\hat{\mathbf{d}} \quad \text{(shown)} \]
Step 2 — (ii) Numerical N
M1 A1
