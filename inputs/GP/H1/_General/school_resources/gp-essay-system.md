## 1. Macro Essay Structure

Every P1 essay follows this exact structure:

1. **Introduction** (approx. 80 words)
2. **Body Paragraph 1** — Stand + Balance (broadest: theory-level)
3. **Body Paragraph 2** — Stand + Balance (societal/global scale)
4. **Body Paragraph 3** — Opposing View + Response (most specific)
5. **Conclusion** (approx. 50–70 words)

Paragraphs must sequence from BROAD to NARROW across the essay. BP1 grounds the argument theoretically. BP2 applies it at societal or global scale. BP3 handles the opposing view and narrows to a specific case or individual scale.

## 2. The 10-Line Paragraph Micro Structure

Every body paragraph follows this line-by-line system:

| Line | Function |
|---|---|
| L1 | Topic sentence: F + C = V1. One stakeholder + one characteristic + one contention link. |
| L2 | Explain WHY. Unpack context. What SPERM factor drives this behaviour or trend? |
| L3 | Explain WHY deeper. Theory, principle, or logical reasoning. Show conceptual depth. |
| L4 | Example. Specific, named, recent. Country, organisation, policy, event, year. |
| L5 | Analysis + Evaluation. Nuanced. Tick/cross. Who benefits, who does not? Key influencing factors. Competing perspectives. |
| L6 | Transition: F + C2 = V2. Shift the SPERM lens OR the scale (P/S/G). New angle, not repetition. |
| L7 | Explain WHY for the second argument. |
| L8 | Example. Different context, country, or scale from L4. |
| L9 | Analysis + Evaluation. Second nuance. Conditions, exceptions, competing perspectives. Tick/cross. |
| L10 | Link back to the question's contention. |

**F + C = V formula:**
- F = Function (what the stakeholder does or what the force is)
- C = Context (specific circumstances, conditions, or domain)
- V = Value/Claim (the contention being made)

## 4. Planning Process

Run this before every essay or plan output.

**Step 1: Question Analysis**
- Define all subjects (terms, concepts, claims) in the question
- List characteristics of each subject: definitions, beliefs, assumptions, scope, what it includes/excludes
- Identify the contention: what is the question actually asking you to argue?

**Step 2: Stakeholder Generation**
- List 8–12 stakeholders ranging from broad (global institutions, societies) to specific (individuals, subgroups)
- For each relevant stakeholder, run the expanded SPERM brainstorm
- Discard weak stakeholder-SPERM combinations; keep only those that produce defensible claims

**Step 3: Scale Mapping**
- Map how the issue plays out at Personal (individual), Societal (national/local), and Global (international/cross-cultural) levels
- Every essay should touch at least 2 of these 3 scales

**Step 4: Stand Declaration**
- Decide: absolute stand (clearly agree or disagree) or qualified stand (agree with conditions/reservations)
- Absolute stand: BP3 rebuts the opposing view
- Qualified stand: BP3 concedes validity but shows why your thesis still holds
- Choose the stand that produces the strongest, most defensible essay

**Step 5: Paragraph Allocation (4 Balls)**
For each of the 3 body paragraphs, specify:
- Which stakeholder
- Which characteristic
- Which contention link
- Which SPERM lens for L2/L3 (first half) and which different lens for L6/L7 (second half)
- Which example for L4 and which different example for L8 (different context or scale)
- Key evaluation points for L5 and L9

Confirm sequencing goes BROAD to NARROW. BP1 is always the most theoretical/universal.

**Step 6: Introduction Strategy**
- Choose one of the 5 effective opening strategies (see Section 5)
- Draft the F + C = V1 thesis statement

**Step 7: Conclusion Direction**
- Identify the key synthesis threads (do not just restate what you said)
- Choose a forward-looking angle: implication, call to reflection, or broader consequence

## 5. Introduction Strategies

### 5 Disastrous Starts (NEVER USE)

1. **Repeating the question** — "The question asks whether..." Wastes words, no intellectual contribution.
2. **Unnecessary definition** — "The Oxford Dictionary defines X as..." Signals a student who cannot start with ideas.
3. **Obvious comment** — "In today's world, technology is everywhere." Generic, adds nothing.
4. **Clichéd description** — "Throughout the ages, mankind has always..." Overused, signals low ambition.
5. **Clumsy anecdote** — A personal story used as the entire opening without analytical purpose.

### 5 Effective Openings (HCI "Hook, Line and Sinker")

1. **Thought-provoking quotation** — A quote from a thinker, leader, or work that frames the debate immediately. Must be relevant and explained.
2. **Perceptive observation** — An insight about a trend, tension, or paradox in the world that the question addresses. Shows independent thinking.
3. **Cultural literacy reference** — A reference to a historical event, philosophical tradition, or cultural phenomenon that contextualizes the question. Signals breadth.
4. **Topically relevant example** — A specific, recent real-world event or case that dramatizes the issue. Grounds abstract claims in reality.
5. **Apposite comparison or analogy** — A comparison between two seemingly different things that reveals an unexpected insight about the question's subject.

### Introduction Structure

Follow this sequence:
1. Opening hook (one of the 5 strategies above)
2. Bridge to the question's terrain (1–2 sentences contextualizing the issue)
3. Define key contested terms naturally within the argument (not as a dictionary entry)
4. Thesis statement using F + C = V1 formula
5. State your stand clearly (qualified or absolute)

## 6. Conclusion Techniques

- **Never** copy language from the introduction
- **Never** introduce a new argument
- Restate the thesis in fresh language that reflects the complexity explored in the essay
- Synthesise the key threads: what the three paragraphs together demonstrate about the question
- End with one of: a forward-looking implication, a broader consequence, a call to reflection, or a conditional statement about when the thesis is most/least true
- Target: 50–70 words
- Tone: measured and nuanced (Band 5 language)

## 7. Evaluation Toolkits

### CARE Framework
Use when the question involves responsibility, civic apathy, or collective action problems.

- **C — Capacity:** Does the actor have the resources, knowledge, or power to act?
- **A — Awareness:** Does the actor know the problem exists and understand its scope?
- **R — Responsibility:** Is there a clear duty, legal or moral, assigned to this actor?
- **E — Effort:** Has the actor made genuine attempts? What has been tried and failed?

Apply to evaluate WHY a stakeholder has or has not done something. Produces tick/cross analysis rather than pure description.

### Effort-Reward Framework
Use for questions about progress, meritocracy, success, achievement, or economic outcomes.

- Examine whether effort actually translates to reward in the context being discussed
- Identify structural factors (wealth, access, discrimination, geography) that break the effort-reward link
- Distinguish between contexts where the link holds (individual skill development) and where it breaks (systemic inequality)

### Past vs Present Comparison Tool
Use for questions about change, relevance, decline, or whether something is "still" true.

- Establish what was true in the past and the mechanisms that made it true
- Identify what has changed (technological, political, economic, cultural) and why
- Evaluate: is the change fundamental (the old claim is now false) or superficial (the old claim holds with modifications)?
- Avoid false nostalgia (assuming the past was better) and false progress (assuming change is always improvement)

## 8. Argumentative Vocabulary Bank

### For building arguments
- "This is primarily because..."
- "What drives this is..."
- "The underlying logic here is..."
- "This operates on the premise that..."
- "The key mechanism is..."

### For evaluation and nuance
- "This holds true insofar as..."
- "The extent to which this applies depends on..."
- "A critical qualification here is..."
- "This argument loses force when..."
- "The picture is more complex when we consider..."

### For transitions within a paragraph (L6)
- "This structural tension is further compounded by..."
- "Shifting the lens to [SPERM category] reveals..."
- "At the [societal/global/personal] level, this dynamic manifests as..."
- "The economic dimension reinforces this claim: ..."

### For the opposing view paragraph
- "Proponents of the contrary position argue that..."
- "There is genuine force to the view that..."
- "One cannot dismiss the claim that..."
- "Yet this reading, however plausible, overlooks..."
- "The concession here is that..., but the stronger case remains that..."

### For conclusions
- "Taken together, these arguments suggest that..."
- "The question ultimately turns on..."
- "What this analysis makes clear is..."
- "The real challenge lies not in [X] but in [Y]..."

### Signposting
- "Central to this argument is..."
- "The crux of the matter is..."
- "What complicates this picture is..."
- "The distinction that matters here is..."

## 9. Final Submission Checklist (9 points)

Before finalising any essay output, verify every item:

1. Introduction uses one of the 5 effective opening strategies. None of the 5 disastrous starts present.
2. Thesis is clear, specific, and states a stand (qualified or absolute).
3. Each body paragraph opens with a topic sentence using F + C = V1.
4. Each paragraph has a L6 transition that shifts SPERM lens or scale.
5. At least 3 different SPERM lenses used across the whole essay.
6. At least 2 different scales (Personal/Societal/Global) used.
7. Every example is specific: named country, organisation, policy, event, or person with a year.
8. L5 and L9 contain genuine tick/cross evaluation, not just description.
9. Conclusion does not copy introduction language, does not introduce new arguments, and ends with a forward-looking statement.

## 10. Cambridge Band Descriptors (P1 Marking)

### Content (30 marks)

| Band | Mark Range | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 5 (A) | 25–30 | Argument is coherent, cogent, well-developed with specific evidence. Clear line of reasoning. Observations convincing, persuasive, and insightful. Nuanced treatment of complexity. Sophisticated and wide knowledge. |
| 4 (B) | 19–24 | Argument is sound, mostly developed. Relevant evidence. Some complexity handled. Plausible and generally relevant. |
| 3 (C) | 13–18 | Argument exists but underdeveloped or partly irrelevant. Observations generalised, assertive, and/or descriptive. Limited evidence or evidence used to show off rather than argue. |
| 2 (D) | 7–12 | Argument is weak, largely descriptive, or tangential. Thin knowledge. |
| 1 (E) | 1–6 | Minimal relevant content. Essay barely engages the question. |

### Language (20 marks)

| Band | Mark Range | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 5 (A) | 17–20 | Fluent, varied, precise. Grammar and syntax consistently accurate. Wide and varied vocabulary used with discrimination. Personal voice. |
| 4 (B) | 13–16 | Mostly accurate. Some variety. Vocabulary adequate. Meaning always clear. |
| 3 (C) | 9–12 | Some errors but meaning generally clear. Limited range. |
| 2 (D) | 5–8 | Frequent errors, some meaning unclear. Basic vocabulary. |
| 1 (E) | 1–4 | Very frequent errors, meaning obscured. |

### Calibration benchmarks (from marked essays in GP/)

- **Pass-level arts essay:** ~25/50 (Content: 12/30, Language: 13/20). Weaknesses: unclear paragraph focus, stand not maintained, examples not tied to argument.
- **B-grade beauty essay:** ~35/50. Stronger argumentation but confusing paragraph structure and drifting from the plan mid-essay.
