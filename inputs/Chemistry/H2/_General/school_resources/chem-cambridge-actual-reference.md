## Quick orientation: what the Cambridge actual paper format looks like

**Paper 1 (9729/01) — Multiple Choice**
- 30 MCQs (post-2017; was 40 MCQs in 9647)
- 1 hr, 30 marks
- Q1-25 are single-answer A/B/C/D. Q26-30 in 9647 were "options 1, 2, 3" type combos; in 9729 the "1, 2, 3 only" style was REMOVED.
- Topics distributed across all syllabus areas; weighting roughly: physical chem (atomic structure, bonding, energetics, kinetics, equilibria) ~12-14 Qs; inorganic (Period 3, Group 2, Group 17, transition metals) ~6-8 Qs; organic ~10-12 Qs.

**Paper 2 (9729/02) — Structured Questions**
- 4-5 long structured questions
- 2 hrs, 75 marks
- Each question carries 12-20 marks split across sub-parts (a)(i), (a)(ii), (b)(i)…
- Mix of all topics, NO planning/practical (planning moved to P4 in 9729).
- Heavy on mechanism drawing, calculations with working, deductions from spectroscopic/qualitative data, structure elucidation.

**Paper 3 (9729/03) — Free Response**
- 4-5 long free-response questions, students choose all (no choice in 9729; was 4 of 5 with choice in 9647).
- 2 hrs, 80 marks
- Multi-stage synthesis problems, equilibrium calculations, thermochemistry cycles, deduction problems with extensive observation tables.
- Most evaluation-level (Bloom 5) questions sit here.

**Paper 4 (9729/04) — Practical (replaces 9647 P5)**
- 2.5 hrs, 55 marks
- 3-4 stations: (a) volumetric/titration analysis (b) qualitative analysis with cation/anion identification (c) planning/design (replaces the 9647 P2 planning Q)
- See 2017 EJC P4 file for layout.

**P3 Q4(a)** [Comparing intermolecular forces — multi-paragraph model answer]:
"Ethane and methane are both non-polar hydrocarbons with instantaneous dipole-induced dipole (id-id) interactions between their molecules. Electrons are constantly moving and at any given moment, the electron density of a molecule can be unsymmetrical, resulting in an instantaneous dipole, which induces a short-lived dipole in a neighbouring molecule. Id-id interactions become stronger when the electron cloud increases in size and becomes more easily distorted. Ethane is a larger molecule with more electrons and thus a larger electron cloud size. As a result, ethane experiences stronger id-id interactions which require a higher energy to overcome and therefore its boiling point is higher. A methanol molecule contains an OH group with H covalently bonded to O. Since O is highly electronegative, it attracts the bonding electrons that it shares with H strongly to itself so that H is partially positive. Since H does not have inner shell electrons, its proton is exposed and interacts strongly with a lone-pair of electrons on O in a nearby methanol molecule." **THIS IS THE CANONICAL "explain BP difference" template. Memorise structure: (1) identify IMFs in each (2) describe nature of forces (3) compare strength (4) link to energy needed (5) link to BP.**

### Mechanism conventions (Cambridge actual)

**SN1:**
- Two-arrow mechanism in slow step: C–X bond breaks heterolytically with both electrons going to X (curly arrow from bond to X).
- Carbocation intermediate explicitly drawn.
- Fast step: nucleophile lone pair attacks C⁺ (curly arrow from lone pair to C). Forms racemic mixture if C is chiral.
- Reagent specificity: usually NaOH(aq), heat, for hydrolysis.

**SN2:**
- Single concerted step. Transition state drawn explicitly with dashed bonds.
- Nucleophile lone pair attacks C from opposite side of leaving group (backside attack).
- Inversion of configuration at chiral C explicitly shown.
- Reagent specificity: usually OH⁻ aq, heat. Primary or methyl halides favoured.

**E1:**
- Rare in actual papers but appears: slow step forms carbocation (same as SN1 slow step).
- Fast step: base (often EtOH solvent) removes β-H, π bond forms.
- Conditions: usually 3° halide, no strong base.

**E2:**
- Single concerted step. Transition state with partial bonds.
- Base removes β-H simultaneously with C-X bond breaking and π bond formation.
- Reagent specificity: ethanolic NaOH, heat. Often anti-periplanar arrangement implied.

**Electrophilic addition (alkene):**
- Slow step: π electrons of C=C attack electrophile (curly arrow from C=C to electrophile). Carbocation forms.
- Fast step: nucleophile lone pair attacks carbocation.
- Markovnikov: H adds to C with more H, X adds to C with fewer H (giving more stable carbocation).
- Mechanism must explicitly justify carbocation stability for major product preference.

**Electrophilic substitution (aromatic):**
- Slow step: 2 of the 6 π electrons of benzene attack electrophile. Carbocation intermediate (cyclohexadienyl cation) drawn with horseshoe arc above 5 sp² carbons.
- Fast step: loss of H⁺ from sp³ carbon to restore aromaticity.
- Catalyst generation: e.g., Cl2 + AlCl3 ⇌ Cl⁺ + [AlCl4]⁻ for chlorination; HNO3 + 2H2SO4 → NO2⁺ + H3O⁺ + 2HSO4⁻ for nitration.

**Nucleophilic addition (carbonyl):**
- Slow step: nucleophile lone pair attacks +δ carbonyl C; C=O π bond breaks, electrons go to O.
- Fast step: protonation of -O⁻ (often by H⁺ from solution or H2O).
- Reagent specificity: HCN with trace NaCN(aq) cold for cyanohydrin; LiAlH4 in dry ether or NaBH4 in methanol for reduction.

**Nucleophilic acyl substitution (acyl chloride/ester/amide hydrolysis):**
- Two-step: addition then elimination.
- Step 1 (slow): nucleophile attacks carbonyl C, forms tetrahedral intermediate with C-O⁻.
- Step 2 (fast): leaving group leaves, C=O reformed.
- Order of reactivity: acyl chloride > ester > amide.

**Free radical substitution (alkane + halogen):**
- Initiation: X2 → 2X• (UV or heat).
- Propagation: X• + RH → R• + HX; R• + X2 → RX + X•.
- Termination: any two radicals combine.
- Statements about stability and rate of secondary > primary radical given for explaining selectivity.

| Cyanide substitution | KCN or NaCN in ethanol, heat |
| Hydration of alkene | conc. H2SO4 then warm H2O (Markovnikov) |
| Amide formation from acyl chloride + amine | Acyl chloride + amine, room temperature |
| Ester formation from acid + alcohol | conc. H2SO4 catalyst, heat, reflux |
| Acidic hydrolysis of ester/amide | dil H2SO4 (or HCl), heat under reflux |
| Alkaline hydrolysis of ester/amide | NaOH(aq), heat under reflux |

### Calculation working conventions

**Faraday/electrolysis:**
- Always show Q = It, then ne = Q/F.
- Then mol of product = ne / (electrons per mol product from half-equation).
- Mass = mol × Mr. Volume at stp = mol × 22.7 dm³ (NOT 22.4 in 9729). At rtp = mol × 24.0 dm³.

**Equilibrium ICE table:**
- Always draw initial / change / equilibrium row.
- Use mole change to derive eqm concentrations.
- Sub into Kc or Kp expression. State units explicitly.

**Henderson-Hasselbalch:**
- pH = pKa + lg ([A⁻]/[HA]).
- Use ratio of moles (not concentrations) if total volume is constant.

**ΔH from bond energies:**
- ΔHr = Σ BE(bonds broken in reactants) − Σ BE(bonds formed in products).
- Watch the sign: this gives ΔH directly (the minus before "formed" matters).
- Use **average** bond energies from Data Booklet; deviation explained by actual molecular environment.

**Born-Haber / Hess cycle:**
- Always draw cycle with arrows labelled with enthalpy values.
- State the route used. Sum with correct signs.
- Lattice energy: negative (exothermic formation of solid from gaseous ions).

**Ksp:**
- For MX2 (e.g. CaF2): Ksp = (s)(2s)² = 4s³.
- Units: mol^(n+1) dm^(-3(n+1)) where n is total ions per formula unit.

**ΔG = ΔH − TΔS:**
- Watch units: ΔH usually kJ mol⁻¹, ΔS usually J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Convert to consistent units.
- ΔG = −RT ln K → R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, so ΔG must be in J mol⁻¹ for this.
- ΔG = −nFE → F = 96500 C mol⁻¹, so ΔG in J mol⁻¹.

### Energy cycle diagram conventions (Cambridge actual)

- Diagonal arrows for enthalpy changes; vertical separations represent state changes.
- Top of cycle: gaseous atoms/ions.
- Always label each arrow with the enthalpy term name and value.
- For lattice energy: arrow from M⁺(g) + X⁻(g) **downward** to MX(s) (exothermic).
- For hydration: arrow from M⁺(g) downward to M⁺(aq) (exothermic).
- For atomisation: arrow from M(s) upward to M(g).
- For ionisation: arrow from M(g) upward to M⁺(g) + e⁻.
- For electron affinity: arrow from X(g) + e⁻ downward to X⁻(g) (exothermic for 1st EA, endothermic for 2nd EA of O).
- Hess's Law summation around any closed cycle = 0; equate routes.

### Curly arrow standards (Cambridge actual)

- Curly arrows show movement of **electron pairs** (full-headed arrows) for heterolytic mechanisms. Half-headed (fishhook) arrows for radical mechanisms (1 electron).
- Tail of arrow: at the bond or lone pair the electrons come from.
- Head of arrow: at the atom or bond they go to.
- Partial charges (+δ, −δ) must be marked on relevant atoms.
- Lone pairs must be drawn on nucleophiles.
- Transition states in SN2 drawn with dashed bonds and a square bracket with ‡.
- Carbocations drawn explicitly with + charge on C.
- For aromatic mechanisms: cyclohexadienyl intermediate drawn with horseshoe arc above sp² carbons + explicit sp³ centre.

### Stereochemistry indication (Cambridge actual)

- Use wedge (solid triangle) for bond coming out of plane, dashed for going behind.
- Always label "*" or specify chiral centres in question.
- For SN1 racemic mixture: draw both enantiomers explicitly.
- For SN2 inversion: show product with reversed configuration vs starting material.
- For cis/trans: label E/Z or cis/trans explicitly when asked.

### Carbocation stability flagging

Cambridge expects this exact reasoning chain:
1. Identify carbocation as 1° / 2° / 3°.
2. Number of electron-donating alkyl groups attached (0/1/2/3).
3. Alkyl groups disperse the positive charge via induction.
4. More alkyl groups = more stable carbocation = forms faster (lower activation energy) = major product preference.
5. If aromatic ring attached: resonance stabilisation (delocalisation of +charge into the ring).

For radical stability: same logic with electron-donating groups stabilising the radical. Plus optional resonance into aromatic π system for benzylic radicals.

## Key extraction summary — what to memorise from Cambridge actual

**Top 5 verbatim mark-scheme phrases:**

1. **Period 3 / IE trend:** "Across Period 3, effective nuclear charge increases due to increasing nuclear charge (increasing no. of protons) and the relatively constant shielding effect (due to same no. of filled inner principal quantum shells of electrons)."

2. **Group 17 oxidising power:** "Down Group 17, the value of E°(X2/X⁻) becomes less positive and the position of equilibrium of the reduction of X2 to X⁻ lies increasingly to the left. Hence, down the group, X2 has less tendency to be reduced and the oxidising power of X2 decreases."

3. **Group 2 carbonate thermal stability:** "Down Group 2, cationic radius increases, resulting in a lower charge density and weaker polarising power of the cations. Consequently, there is decreasing extent of distortion of the electron cloud of the CO3²⁻ anion and hence decreasing extent of weakening of covalent bonds within the CO3²⁻ anion. More heat energy is required to break the covalent bonds, causing the decomposition temperature to increase."

4. **Transition metal colour:** "Due to the presence of ligands, the five 3d orbitals are split into two sets of different energy levels. Since the 3d subshell is partially filled, electrons from the lower-energy d orbitals can absorb energy corresponding to certain wavelengths from the visible spectrum and get promoted to the higher-energy d orbitals (d-d transitions). The colour observed is the complement of the colour absorbed."

5. **Catalyst:** "A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of reaction by providing an alternate reaction pathway of lower activation energy, without itself undergoing any permanent chemical change."

**Top 3 BP/IMF templates:**

A. **Same Mr, different IMFs:** Identify both IMFs → describe nature → compare strength → link to energy → link to BP.

B. **Same IMF type, different Mr/branching:** "Larger Mr means larger electron cloud, more polarisable, stronger id-id interactions, more energy to overcome, higher BP." Branching: less surface area → weaker id-id.

C. **H-bond donor/acceptor comparison:** "[Compound X] has more –OH groups, average more H-bonds per molecule, more extensive H-bonding network, more energy to overcome, higher BP."

**Top 3 acidity templates:**

A. **Conjugate base stability:** "The more stable the conjugate base, the stronger the acid (lower pKa, larger Ka)."

B. **Electron-withdrawing substituent:** "EWG disperses negative charge on conjugate base, stabilises it, acid is stronger."

C. **Electron-donating substituent:** "EDG intensifies negative charge on conjugate base, destabilises it, acid is weaker."

**Top 3 basicity templates:**

A. **Alkyl substituent:** "EDG (alkyl) increases electron density on N atom, lone pair more available for coordination to H⁺, more basic. Ordering: 3° > 2° > 1° > NH3 (in gas phase)."

B. **Aryl group:** "Lone pair on N delocalises into benzene ring via p-orbital overlap with π electron cloud → less available → less basic than aliphatic amines."

C. **Aryl + EWG:** "EWG further reduces electron density via inductive (or further resonance withdrawal), even less basic than phenylamine."

## Practical paper (P4) extraction (from 2017 EJC)

**Standard 9729/04 structure:**
- **Question 1 — Quantitative (thermochemistry or titration):** Plot data, calculate ΔH from temperature change, identify systematic errors (e.g. heat loss), suggest improvements.
- **Question 2 — Reaction kinetics from sampling:** Titrate samples at intervals, plot concentration vs time, determine order from half-life consistency, calculate rate constant.
- **Question 3 — Qualitative analysis:** Identify cations/anions from QA scheme. Standard QA reactions table in Data Booklet. Observation wording matters: "white ppt soluble in excess NaOH(aq)" / "brown gas evolved" / "effervescence of colourless odourless gas, relights glowing splint."
- **Question 4 — Planning (replaces 9647 P2 Q1):** Design an experiment to determine [solute] OR study a reaction's order OR find an equilibrium constant. Pre-calculations, procedure, sketch graph, treatment of data, sources of error.

**Standard observation wordings:**
- "Effervescence observed. Colourless, odourless gas relights glowing splint. Hence, oxygen gas is present."
- "Effervescence observed. Colourless, odourless gas turns limewater milky. Hence, carbon dioxide is present."
- "Brown gas evolved (NO2). Test with damp blue litmus → red. Hence, NO2."
- "White ppt of [substance] forms; insoluble in excess NaOH(aq); soluble in NH3(aq) to give colourless solution. Hence, Ag⁺ confirmed."
- "Orange solution decolourises. Hence, alkene present (electrophilic addition with Br2)."
- "Yellow ppt of CHI3 with characteristic smell. Hence, CH3CO- or CH3CH(OH)- present."
