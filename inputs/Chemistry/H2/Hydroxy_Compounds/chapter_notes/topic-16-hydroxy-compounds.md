## UNIT 1 — Introduction: alcohols & phenols (naming, classification, bp/solubility, prep)

### Definitions & classification (LO 11.1a, 11.3a)
- **Alcohol = R–OH** (–OH on an alkyl/substituted-alkyl C). **Phenol = –OH attached DIRECTLY to a benzene ring.** Phenols differ markedly from alcohols in both physical and chemical properties.
- **Classify by the number of C atoms bonded to the C bearing –OH:** **primary (1°)** = 1 (or 0) C; **secondary (2°)** = 2 C; **tertiary (3°)** = 3 C. *Always state 1°/2°/3° explicitly when naming an alcohol — it determines oxidation behaviour.*
- **Naming:** replace alkane's terminal **-e with -ol**; longest chain must contain the –OH; **number from the end nearer –OH**; substituents alphabetical. –OH outranks halogens (so it's the parent suffix) but as a *substituent* (when a higher-priority group like C=O is present) it's the prefix **hydroxy-**.
- **Ethers (R–O–R) are constitutional isomers of alcohols** but have **no –OH → no H-bonding between molecules → fairly inert, lower bp** than the isomeric alcohol.

### Physical properties (bp & solubility) — all about hydrogen bonding
- **bp(alcohol) > bp(isoelectronic alkane):** dispersion forces are similar (similar electron-cloud size), but alcohols also form **intermolecular hydrogen bonds (stronger than dispersion)** → more energy to overcome → higher bp.
- **bp rises with chain length** (bigger electron cloud → stronger **dispersion forces**; H-bonding stays ~constant). **More –OH groups → more extensive H-bonding → higher bp** (e.g. ethane-1,2-diol > ethanol > ethane).
- **Solubility:** alcohols **more soluble than alkanes** (H-bond with water); **solubility falls as the chain lengthens** — the longer non-polar alkyl chain means H-bonding released (alcohol–water) can no longer overcome the stronger alcohol–alcohol dispersion + water–water H-bonding, and the chain disrupts water's H-bonding. **Phenol: only moderately soluble (large non-polar aryl group), but dissolves fully when warmed.**

### Preparation of alcohols (LO)
- **Electrophilic addition of steam to alkenes** (Topic 11; H₂O(g)/H₃PO₄).
- **Nucleophilic substitution of halogenoalkanes** (Topic 15; NaOH(aq), heat).
- **Reduction of aldehydes/ketones** (Topic 17; LiAlH₄ or H₂/Ni): **aldehyde → 1° alcohol, ketone → 2° alcohol.**
- **Reduction of carboxylic acids** (Topic 18; LiAlH₄) → **1° alcohol only.**

## UNIT 2 — Alcohols & phenols as Brønsted acids (the conceptual spine) ★★

The –OH group lets both act as **Brønsted acids** (lose H⁺ from O–H) or bases (O lone pair protonated). This unit focuses on **acidity**.

### Acidity rule (from Topic 14)
**Acid strength depends on the stability of the conjugate base.** A **more stable** conjugate base is less likely to re-accept H⁺ → greater dissociation → **stronger acid, larger Ka.** Anything that **disperses the negative charge stabilises** the anion (↑ acidity); anything that **intensifies it destabilises** (↓ acidity).

### Acidity ladder: **alcohol < water < phenol** (LO 11.6e) ★
- **Alcohols are WEAKER acids than water.** Alkyl groups are **inductively electron-donating → intensify the −charge on the alkoxide O → destabilise the alkoxide → less dissociation.** (Chain length has no significant effect.) → alcohols **do not turn blue litmus red.**
- **Phenols are STRONGER acids than alcohols AND water** (0.1 mol dm⁻³ phenol has pH ≈ 5.4 → **turns blue litmus red**). Reason: in the **phenoxide ion, a p orbital on the O overlaps the ring π-cloud → the −charge delocalises into the benzene ring → phenoxide is stabilised far more than alkoxide** → less likely to re-accept H⁺.

### Substituent effects on acidity ★
- **Electron-withdrawing groups (–NO₂, –F/–Cl/–Br/–I, –CN, –CO₂H, –COCH₃, –CO₂R) increase acidity** — they **disperse/stabilise** the −charge on the alkoxide/phenoxide.
- **Electron-donating groups (–CH₃, –NH₂, –OH, –OCH₃) decrease acidity** — they intensify the −charge / reduce delocalisation → destabilise the anion.
- **Inductive effect weakens with distance:** the **nearer the EWG is to the −O⁻, the greater the charge dispersal and the stronger the acid** (e.g. CH₃CH₂CHCl–OH > CH₃CHCl–CH₂OH).

### Reactions as acids
- **With sodium (redox, LO 11.6a):** both react with Na/K to give **alkoxide/phenoxide + H₂** (breaks the O–H bond). Mirrors Na + water but: **alcohols react more slowly (weaker acid → slow effervescence); phenol reacts more vigorously (stronger acid → rapid effervescence).** Reagent Na(s), rt.
- **With NaOH (acid-base, LO 11.6d):** **phenol IS acidic enough to react with NaOH** → soluble phenoxide salt + water (cloudy phenol/water mixture → **clear colourless homogeneous solution**). **Alcohols are NOT** (weaker than water). **Neither phenol nor alcohol is acidic enough to react with carbonates to release CO₂** — a useful distinguishing point (separates phenol from a carboxylic acid).

## UNIT 3 — Other reactions of alcohols & phenols; distinguishing tests

### A. Nucleophilic substitution of alcohols → halogenoalkanes (LO 11.6a; mechanism NOT required)
Replaces –OH with halogen (breaks the **C–O bond**). **–OH is a poor leaving group**, so reagents first convert it into a better one:
| Reagent | Product | Conditions / observation |
|---|---|---|
| **PCl₅** | chloroalkane | rt; **dense white fumes of HCl** — good distinguishing test for –OH (if no –CO₂H) |
| **conc HCl** (3°) / **conc HCl + ZnCl₂, heat** (1°,2°) | chloroalkane | solution turns cloudy |
| **conc HBr**, or **NaBr + conc H₂SO₄ (HBr in situ), heat** | bromoalkane | — |
| **conc HI** | iodoalkane | — |
| (PCl₃ rt; or P + Br₂/I₂ heat → PBr₃/PI₃ in situ) | bromo/iodoalkane | FYI |
| (**SOCl₂, warm**) | chloroalkane | SO₂ + HCl gases (easy to separate); FYI |
- HX reactivity: **HI > HBr > HCl** (HF unreactive). The HCl reaction goes via **SN1** (rate ∝ carbocation stability → **3° fastest, 1° very slow**) — which is *why* conc HCl alone works for 3° but 1°/2° need ZnCl₂ + heat.
- **Phenols do NOT undergo this** (extremely harsh conditions needed) — same reasons as halogenoarenes: **O lone pair delocalised into ring → C–O has partial double-bond character → strong/hard to break**, plus steric blocking + π-repulsion.

### B. Dehydration (elimination) → alkene (LO 11.6a)
- **Reagents: concentrated H₃PO₄ catalyst, heat** (or excess conc H₂SO₄/heat, or Al₂O₃/heat). Loses –OH + an H from the **adjacent carbon** → **alkene + H₂O.**
- **H₃PO₄ preferred over H₂SO₄** because H₂SO₄ can act as an oxidising agent → unwanted side products; H₃PO₄ is not an oxidiser.
- **No H on the carbon adjacent to C–OH → no dehydration.** May give a **mixture of alkenes** (constitutional + cis-trans). *(Contrast with halogenoalkane elimination: alkene formed in both, but reagents differ and the by-product is H₂O here vs HX there.)*

### C. Condensation → esters (LO 11.8b, 11.8e, 11.8g)
The –OH (O lone pairs) acts as a **nucleophile** towards acyl carbons.
- **With carboxylic acid (esterification):** alcohol + carboxylic acid **+ a few drops conc H₂SO₄ catalyst, heat → ester + H₂O.** **Slow and reversible.** (Only *a few drops* of H₂SO₄ so it catalyses rather than dehydrates.) Example: ethyl ethanoate.
- **With acyl chloride:** **much more readily — no heat, no catalyst, goes to completion** → ester + **white fumes of HCl.** Works for **both alcohols and phenols.**
- **Phenols do NOT react with carboxylic acids** (O lone pair delocalised into ring → less available/nucleophilic). **The only way to make a phenyl ester is phenol + acyl chloride** (e.g. **phenyl benzoate**). Better yield under **basic conditions** (Na/NaOH first → phenoxide, a stronger nucleophile).

### D. Oxidation of alcohols (LO 11.6a, 11.6b) ★ — the 1°/2°/3° diagnostic
Two oxidant systems: **K₂Cr₂O₇ / dil H₂SO₄ / heat** and **KMnO₄ / dil H₂SO₄ / heat.** **The alcohol must have ≥1 H on the carbon bearing –OH** → so **3° alcohols and phenols cannot be oxidised** by these.
- **1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid:**
  - **To stop at the ALDEHYDE: K₂Cr₂O₇/dil H₂SO₄, heat with IMMEDIATE DISTILLATION** (distil the aldehyde out as it forms — it has no H-bonding so lower bp than the alcohol). **Only K₂Cr₂O₇ is mild/selective enough to stop at the aldehyde; KMnO₄ goes straight to the acid.**
  - **To get the CARBOXYLIC ACID: heat under REFLUX with excess oxidant** (K₂Cr₂O₇ or KMnO₄).
- **2° alcohol → ketone** (resistant to further oxidation).
- **3° alcohol → no reaction.**
- **Observations:** **orange Cr₂O₇²⁻ → green Cr³⁺**; **purple MnO₄⁻ → colourless Mn²⁺.**
- **As a distinguishing test for 1°/2°/3°:** reflux with **excess** acidified K₂Cr₂O₇. **3° → stays orange.** 1° and 2° → **turn green**; to tell those apart, **isolate the product** and test it (carboxylic acid vs ketone — Topics 17/18). *(In simple test-tube tests, no reflux/distillation apparatus.)* Watch for molecules with **both an alkene and a 2° alcohol**: K₂Cr₂O₇ oxidises only the alcohol → ketone; **KMnO₄ also cleaves the C=C.**

### E. Tri-iodomethane (iodoform) test (LO 11.6c) ★
- **Detects a CH₃CH(OH)– group** (a methyl carbinol). **Reagents: I₂(aq) + NaOH(aq), warm. Positive = yellow precipitate of CHI₃ (tri-iodomethane).**
- It is a **step-down reaction (shortens the chain by one C)**: CH₃CH(OH)R → RCO₂⁻ + CHI₃. **Also positive for methyl ketones CH₃CO–** (and ethanol/ethanal) — they get oxidised in situ to the methyl carbonyl first.

### F. Electrophilic substitution of phenol's ring (LO 11.6d) ★ — the benzene contrast
The **O lone pair delocalises into the ring → greatly raises ring electron density → phenol is FAR more reactive to electrophiles than benzene.** Two consequences:
1. **Milder conditions than benzene: no catalyst, no heat.**
2. **–OH is strongly activating and 2,4-directing → easily gives multi-substituted (2,4,6-) products.**
- **Nitration:** **dilute HNO₃(aq), rt → mixture of 2-nitrophenol + 4-nitrophenol** (pale yellow liquid). (Contrast: benzene needs conc HNO₃ + conc H₂SO₄ at 50 °C.) Conc HNO₃ at rt → **2,4,6-trinitrophenol.**
- **Bromination:** **aqueous bromine (bromine water), rt → white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol**, and the **yellow-orange bromine is decolourised — NO catalyst needed.** (Contrast: benzene needs Br₂ + AlBr₃.) For **mono-bromination**, use low temperature + **Br₂(l) or Br₂ in CCl₄, 1:1 ratio.**

### G. FeCl₃ complex test for phenols (LO)
- **Neutral FeCl₃(aq), rt → violet coloration** (colour varies with ring substituents). **Distinguishing test for a phenolic group.**

### Distinguishing tests — quick reference
| To distinguish | Reagent | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| **alcohol/phenol present** (vs no –OH) | **Na(s)** | effervescence (H₂) |
| **phenol vs alcohol** | **NaOH(aq)** / **neutral FeCl₃** / **bromine water** | phenol dissolves / **violet** / **white ppt + decolourised** (alcohol: no change) |
| **1°/2° vs 3° alcohol** | acidified **K₂Cr₂O₇**, heat | 1°/2° → **orange→green**; 3° → stays orange |
| **CH₃CH(OH)– group** | **I₂(aq) + NaOH, warm** | **yellow ppt** (CHI₃) |
| **–OH present** (if no –CO₂H) | **PCl₅** | white fumes (HCl) |

## TOP EXAM TRAPS (Topic 16)
- **State 1°/2°/3°** explicitly when identifying an alcohol — it dictates oxidation product and SN1/SN2 leaning.
- **Acidity ladder alcohol < water < phenol**: alcohols weaker (alkyl *donates* → destabilises alkoxide); phenol stronger (−charge *delocalised into ring* → stabilises phenoxide). Always argue via **conjugate-base stability**, and put the EWG **near** the O⁻ for max effect.
- **Phenol reacts with NaOH but NOT carbonates** (acidic enough for one, not the other) — and **alcohols react with neither.**
- **Oxidation: aldehyde needs K₂Cr₂O₇ + immediate DISTILLATION; carboxylic acid needs REFLUX + excess oxidant.** KMnO₄ overshoots straight to the acid. **3° alcohol & phenol: no oxidation** (no H on the C–OH carbon).
- **Esterification (with acid) is slow/reversible, few drops H₂SO₄;** acyl chloride is fast, complete, no catalyst. **Phenyl esters ONLY from phenol + acyl chloride** (phenol won't react with the acid).
- **Dehydration uses conc H₃PO₄** (not H₂SO₄ — avoids oxidation side-products); needs an H on the adjacent C.
- **Iodoform = CH₃CH(OH)– (or CH₃CO–), yellow ppt** — and it's a *step-down* (−1 C). Don't confuse with the bromine-water white ppt (that's *phenol*).
- **Phenol electrophilic substitution is EASIER than benzene** (activated ring): no catalyst, no heat, gives 2,4,6-trisubstituted product; bromine water alone → white ppt of 2,4,6-tribromophenol.
- **Phenols resist nucleophilic substitution (–OH → halogen) and esterification with acids** — both because the O lone pair is delocalised into the ring.
- **Colour tests to keep straight:** orange→green (Cr₂O₇²⁻, oxidation); purple→colourless (MnO₄⁻); **violet (FeCl₃ → phenol)**; **yellow ppt (iodoform)**; **white ppt + bromine decolourised (phenol + Br₂(aq))**.
