Question 3 — 9 marks

Modelled on N2020 P2 Q2 (strong ionic bonding in \(\ce{Al2O3}\) from high ionic charge + small radius) and the standard 9729 treatment of \(\ce{AlCl3}\) covalent character (\(\ce{Al^3+}\) polarising power) and the \(\ce{Al2Cl6}\) dative-bonded dimer (cf. N2019 P3 Q1, Lewis acid–base / electron-deficient \(\ce{Al}\)).
(a) MgCl₂ vs NaCl melting point — [3]
Lattice energy \(\propto \dfrac{\text{product of the ionic charges}}{\text{sum of the ionic radii}}\). (1)
Product of charges: \(\ce{MgCl2}\) \(= 2\times1 = 2\) vs \(\ce{NaCl}\) \(= 1\times1 = 1\); sum of radii: \(\ce{MgCl2}\) \(= 0.065+0.181 = 0.246\ \text{nm}\) vs \(\ce{NaCl}\) \(= 0.095+0.181 = 0.276\ \text{nm}\) → \(\ce{MgCl2}\) has the larger product of charges and the smaller sum of radii, so a larger-magnitude lattice energy / stronger ionic bonding  (1)
∴ more energy is needed to overcome the stronger electrostatic attraction between ions → higher melting point  (1)
(b) Why AlCl₃ is covalent — [3]
\(\ce{Al^3+}\) has a high charge and small ionic radius → very high charge density → high polarising power  (1); it strongly polarises / distorts the large, polarisable electron cloud of \(\ce{Cl-}\)  (1); electron density is drawn into the region between the nuclei, giving effective overlap of the electron clouds → a (very polar) covalent bond rather than ionic  (1)
(c) Al₂Cl₆ bonding + conductivity — [3]
A lone pair on a Cl of one \(\ce{AlCl3}\) is donated into the empty orbital on the electron-deficient Al of the other \(\ce{AlCl3}\) → a dative (coordinate) covalent bond (two such bonds hold the dimer together)  (1)
Molten \(\ce{AlCl3}\) exists as neutral \(\ce{Al2Cl6}\) molecules with no mobile ions or electrons → does not conduct  (1); molten \(\ce{NaCl}\) has free mobile ions (\(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{Cl-}\)) that move under a potential difference → conducts  (1)
Trap: the dative bond forms because Al in \(\ce{AlCl3}\) has only 6 electrons (electron-deficient / Lewis acidic) and Cl supplies a lone pair. Don't call molten \(\ce{AlCl3}\) "ions" — it is molecular.

Question 6 — 10 marks

Modelled on N2019 P3 Q1 (electron-pair geometry, bond angles and the effect of lone pairs on shape) and the standard 9729 VSEPR treatment of expanded-octet fluorides; polarity + the pd–pd vs id–id boiling-point comparison follow the canonical IMF mark scheme (cf. N2016 P3 Q4(a) / N2018 P2 Q1(b)).
(a) Shapes, angles, lone-pair positions — [5]
\(\ce{SF4}\): 5 electron groups (4 bp + 1 lp) → the lone pair goes equatorial → seesaw, angles \(\approx 120^\circ\)/\(90^\circ\) (slightly depressed)  (1) shape+angle  (1) lp equatorial
\(\ce{BrF3}\): 5 electron groups (3 bp + 2 lp) → both lone pairs equatorial → T-shaped, \(\approx 90^\circ\)  (1) shape+angle  (1) both lp equatorial
Reason (state it): a lone pair prefers the equatorial site because that gives the fewest \(90^\circ\) lone-pair–bond-pair repulsions (2 at \(90^\circ\) equatorially vs 3 if axial), and \(\text{lp–lp} > \text{lp–bp} > \text{bp–bp}\)  (1)
(b) Polarity — [3]
\(\ce{SF4}\) is polar: the seesaw shape is asymmetric (the lone pair breaks the symmetry), so the \(\ce{S-F}\) bond dipoles do not cancel → net dipole moment  (1) polar  (1) reason
\(\ce{SF6}\) is non-polar: octahedral and symmetric, so the six equal \(\ce{S-F}\) dipoles cancel exactly → no net dipole  (1)
(c) Why SF₄ boils higher than SF₆ — [2]
\(\ce{SF4}\) is polar → it has permanent dipole–permanent dipole (pd–pd) interactions in addition to dispersion forces, whereas non-polar \(\ce{SF6}\) has only dispersion forces  (1); more energy is needed to overcome the stronger (pd–pd + dispersion) forces between \(\ce{SF4}\) molecules, so it has the higher boiling point despite its smaller \(M_r\)  (1)
Trap: \(M_r\) alone would predict \(\ce{SF6}\) higher — the polarity (pd–pd) is the deciding factor here. Identify structure → IMF type → compare strength → conclude.

N2013 P1 Q25, \(\ce{KMnO4}\) with a terminal alkene) plus the bromine-water competing-nucleophile case (HCI Alkenes Tutorial Ex 4.3). Observations use the P4 qualitative-analysis wording.
(a) Distinguish hex-1-ene from cyclohexane — [3]
Reagent/conditions: \(\ce{Br2}\) in \(\ce{CCl4}\), in the absence of UV light (to prevent free-radical substitution). (1) reagent+conditions
• hex-1-ene: reddish-brown \(\ce{Br2}\) is decolourised. (1)
• cyclohexane: reddish-brown \(\ce{Br2}\) is not decolourised. (1)
Accept bromine water (yellow-orange → decolourised for the alkene, no change for the alkane). A test answer needs both outcomes.
(b) Distinguish hex-1-ene from hex-2-ene — [3]
Reagent/conditions: \(\ce{KMnO4}\), dilute \(\ce{H2SO4}\), heat.
• hex-1-ene (terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\)): purple \(\ce{KMnO4}\) decolourised, with effervescence of (colourless and odourless) \(\ce{CO2}\) that gives a white precipitate of \(\ce{CaCO3}\) in limewater. (1) conditions  (1) hex-1-ene obs
• hex-2-ene: purple \(\ce{KMnO4}\) decolourised but no gas is evolved. (1)
Why this works: both decolourise \(\ce{Br2}\)/\(\ce{KMnO4}\), so only oxidative cleavage separates them — the terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) of hex-1-ene is cleaved to \(\ce{CO2}\); an internal \(\ce{C=C}\) is not.
(c) Bromine water + NaCl → three products — [3]
The slow step adds \(\ce{Br}\) to C1 and forms the more stable secondary carbocation at C2, which is then attacked by three competing nucleophiles — \(\ce{H2O}\), \(\ce{Br-}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\):
1-bromohexan-2-ol   1,2-dibromohexane   1-bromo-2-chlorohexane  (1)  (1) (all three; Br on C1 each)
No dichloro compound: the only electrophile present is \(\ce{Br2}\) — there is no \(\ce{Cl2}\)/\(\ce{Cl+}\) to start the addition, so the first atom added in the slow step is always \(\ce{Br}\); every product therefore contains at least one \(\ce{Br}\)  (1)
Trap: \(\ce{Cl-}\) is only a nucleophile in the fast step; it can never be the electrophile that opens the double bond, so 1,2-dichlorohexane is impossible.
