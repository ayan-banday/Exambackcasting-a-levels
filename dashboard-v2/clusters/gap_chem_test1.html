redox titration → determine x · Priority 1Question 1
\(6.95\text{ g}\) of a hydrated iron(II) salt \(\ce{FeSO4.xH2O}\) is dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid and made up to \(250\ \text{cm}^3\). \(25.0\ \text{cm}^3\) portions each require \(25.0\ \text{cm}^3\) of \(0.0200\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}\) \(\ce{KMnO4}\). Determine \(x\).
Reveal (marks inline)

Half/overall equation — the \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) is what is oxidised, so build it: \(\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5Fe^2+ -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O + 5Fe^3+}\). (1)

\(n(\ce{MnO4-}) = 0.0250 \times 0.0200 = 5.00\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol}\) per titre. (1)

\(n(\ce{Fe^2+})\) in \(25.0\ \text{cm}^3 = 5 \times 5.00\times10^{-4} = 2.50\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol}\). (1)

\(n(\ce{Fe^2+})\) in \(250\ \text{cm}^3 = 2.50\times10^{-2}\ \text{mol} = n(\text{salt})\). (1)

\(M_r = \dfrac{6.95}{2.50\times10^{-2}} = 277.9\ \text{g mol}^{-1}\). (1)

\(M(\ce{FeSO4}) = 55.8+32.1+64.0 = 151.9\); water \(= 277.9-151.9 = 126.0\); \(x = \dfrac{126.0}{18.0} = \mathbf{7}\). (1)

The trap you fell for. The salt does NOT react with the acid in a fixed ratio — the acid just provides \(\ce{H+}\) and dissolves it. Route to the salt through the species that is titrated (\(\ce{Fe^2+}\)), never through the acid. Whenever "iron / Fe²⁺" appears: (1) write the \(\ce{MnO4-}\) half-equation in acid, (2) write \(\ce{Fe^2+ -> Fe^3+ + e-}\), (3) combine (5:1), then n(MnO₄⁻) → n(Fe²⁺) → n(salt) → \(M_r\) → \(x\).
build the equation from half-equationsQuestion 2
In acidic solution, \(25.0\ \text{cm}^3\) of aqueous sodium ethanedioate \(\ce{Na2C2O4}\) is oxidised by exactly \(20.0\ \text{cm}^3\) of \(0.0200\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}\) \(\ce{KMnO4}\).
(a) Construct the overall ionic equation. [2]
(b) Calculate the concentration of \(\ce{Na2C2O4}\), in mol dm⁻³. [3]
Reveal (marks inline)

(a) Oxidation: \(\ce{C2O4^2- -> 2CO2 + 2e-}\) (1). Combine with \(\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5e- -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O}\) (×2 and ×5 to balance electrons): \(\ce{2MnO4- + 5C2O4^2- + 16H+ -> 2Mn^2+ + 10CO2 + 8H2O}\) (1).

(b) \(n(\ce{MnO4-}) = 0.0200\times0.0200 = 4.00\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol}\) (1). Ratio \(\ce{MnO4-}:\ce{C2O4^2-} = 2:5\), so \(n(\ce{C2O4^2-}) = \tfrac52\times4.00\times10^{-4} = 1.00\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol}\) (1). \([\ce{Na2C2O4}] = \dfrac{1.00\times10^{-3}}{0.0250} = \mathbf{0.0400\ mol\,dm^{-3}}\) (1).

Rule. The mole ratio comes from the balanced equation (here 2:5), never 1:1. Always balance electrons between the two half-equations first.

Recorded theory gaps (no drill — just fix the idea)

cis–trans needs two DIFFERENT groups on each carbon

You thought the two groups had to be the same. They must be different. A \(\ce{C=C}\) shows cis–trans only if each doubly-bonded carbon carries two different groups. That's why pent-2-ene, \(\ce{H3C-CH=CH-CH2CH3}\), works: one \(\ce{C=C}\) carbon has H + CH₃, the other has H + C₂H₅ — every carbon has two different groups, and the π bond locks rotation. (2-methylpropene fails: one carbon has two identical CH₃.)

Symmetric molecule ⇒ non-polar

Bond dipoles cancel when the shape is symmetric (square planar XeF₄, tetrahedral CCl₄, linear CO₂, trigonal planar BF₃). Polar needs an asymmetric shape so the dipoles don't cancel. Check the shape, then decide.

NaCl vs NaI melting point: lattice energy, not covalent character

Same charges (1+/1−), so the discriminator is ionic radius: I⁻ is larger than Cl⁻ → NaI has the smaller lattice energy → lower mp. So NaCl > NaI is a lattice-energy argument. Covalent character (from a small, highly-charged cation polarising a large anion) is a separate effect; where it operates it raises mp (extra covalent attraction on top of the ionic), but it isn't the reason here.

Read the skeleton — free-radical substitution can't change it

Q7 started from 2-methylbutane (branched), not pentane. Radical substitution only swaps an H for a Cl; the carbon skeleton is fixed. Always name the products off the given structure.
