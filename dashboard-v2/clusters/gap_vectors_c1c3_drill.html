Question 1 [9]
A-Level N2020 P1 Q5
(a) Given that \( \mathbf a \) and \( \mathbf b \) are non-zero vectors such that \( \mathbf a\times\mathbf b=\mathbf b\times\mathbf a \), find the relationship between \( \mathbf a \) and \( \mathbf b \). [2] (b) The points \(P\), \(Q\) and \(R\) have position vectors \( \mathbf p \), \( \mathbf q \) and \( \mathbf r \) respectively. The points \(P\) and \(Q\) are fixed and \(R\) varies.   (i) Given that \( \mathbf q \) is non-zero and \( (\mathbf r-\mathbf p)\times\mathbf q=\mathbf 0 \), describe geometrically the set of all possible positions of the point \(R\). [3]   (ii) Given instead that \( \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix} \), \( \mathbf p=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\4\end{pmatrix} \), \( \mathbf q=\begin{pmatrix}3\\-5\\2\end{pmatrix} \) and that \( (\mathbf r-\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf q=0 \), find the relationship between \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\). Describe the set of all possible positions of \(R\) in this case. [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(a) use anti-commutativity to collapse it
\( \mathbf b\times\mathbf a=-(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b) \), so the given equation says \( \mathbf a\times\mathbf b=-(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b) \). \[ 2(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)=\mathbf 0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf a\times\mathbf b=\mathbf 0 \](1) Both are non-zero, so the only remaining possibility is that they are parallel. \[ \mathbf a\ \text{is parallel to}\ \mathbf b,\ \text{i.e.}\ \mathbf a=\lambda\mathbf b\ \text{for some }\lambda\in\mathbb R \](1)
(b)(i) CROSS product zero \( \Rightarrow \) parallel \( \Rightarrow \) a LINE
\( (\mathbf r-\mathbf p)\times\mathbf q=\mathbf 0 \) means \( \overrightarrow{PR} \) is parallel to \( \mathbf q \). Make \( \mathbf r \) the subject, since a geometrical description is wanted. \[ \mathbf r-\mathbf p=\lambda\mathbf q\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf r=\mathbf p+\lambda\mathbf q,\quad\lambda\in\mathbb R \](1)(1) \[ R\ \text{traces the \textbf{line} through }P\text{ parallel to }\mathbf q. \](1)
(b)(ii) DOT product zero \( \Rightarrow \) perpendicular \( \Rightarrow \) a PLANE
Note this zero is the scalar \(0\), not the zero vector — a dot product returns a number. \[ \begin{pmatrix}x+1\\y-2\\z-4\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}3\\-5\\2\end{pmatrix}=0\ \Rightarrow\ 3x+3-5y+10+2z-8=0 \](1)(1) \[ 3x-5y+2z=-5 \](1) \[ R\ \text{traces the \textbf{plane} through }P(-1,2,4)\ \text{with normal}\ \begin{pmatrix}3\\-5\\2\end{pmatrix}. \](1)
This is the question your whole drill needed. Same equation shape, one symbol different, two completely different objects:
\( (\mathbf r-\mathbf p)\boldsymbol\times\mathbf q=\mathbf 0 \)  ⟹  \( \overrightarrow{PR}\ \boldsymbol\parallel\ \mathbf q \)  ⟹  a LINE.
\( (\mathbf r-\mathbf p)\boldsymbol\cdot\mathbf q=0 \)  ⟹  \( \overrightarrow{PR}\ \boldsymbol\perp\ \mathbf q \)  ⟹  a PLANE.
Cross zero = parallel. Dot zero = perpendicular. Also note \( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=d \) written out is the Cartesian equation of a plane — that is why (ii) lands on \(3x-5y+2z=-5\).
Tests: anti-commutativity of the vector product; and the contrast between a zero vector product (parallel, a line) and a zero scalar product (perpendicular, a plane), stated as geometrical descriptions.

Question 2 [6]
MJC 2010 Prelim P2 Q2 · HCI Supplementary Q22
Three non-zero and non-parallel vectors \( \mathbf p \), \( \mathbf q \) and \( \mathbf r \) are such that \( \mathbf p\times\mathbf q=3\,\mathbf p\times\mathbf r \). (i) Show that \( \mathbf q-3\mathbf r=\lambda\mathbf p \), where \( \lambda \) is a scalar. [2] (ii) It is also given that \( \mathbf p \) is a unit vector, \( |\mathbf q|=5 \), \( |\mathbf r|=2 \), and the angle between \( \mathbf q \) and \( \mathbf r \) is \( \cos^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac56\right) \). By considering \( (\mathbf q-3\mathbf r)\cdot(\mathbf q-3\mathbf r) \), find the exact values of \( \lambda \). [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) collect on one side and factor out \( \mathbf p \)
\[ \mathbf p\times\mathbf q-3\,\mathbf p\times\mathbf r=\mathbf 0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf p\times(\mathbf q-3\mathbf r)=\mathbf 0 \](1) A zero vector product means a magnitude is zero or the vectors are parallel. \( \mathbf p\neq\mathbf 0 \), so either \( \mathbf q-3\mathbf r=\mathbf 0 \) or \( \mathbf p\parallel(\mathbf q-3\mathbf r) \) — both are covered by writing it as a scalar multiple. \[ \therefore\ \mathbf q-3\mathbf r=\lambda\mathbf p,\quad\lambda\in\mathbb R\ \text{(shown)} \](1)
(ii) dot the relation with ITSELF to turn vectors into magnitudes
\( \mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r=|\mathbf q||\mathbf r|\cos\theta=(5)(2)\left(\tfrac56\right)=\tfrac{25}{3} \). \[ (\mathbf q-3\mathbf r)\cdot(\mathbf q-3\mathbf r)=|\mathbf q|^2-6\,\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r+9|\mathbf r|^2 \](1) \[ =25-6\left(\tfrac{25}{3}\right)+9(4)=25-50+36=11 \](1) The left side also equals \( (\lambda\mathbf p)\cdot(\lambda\mathbf p)=\lambda^2|\mathbf p|^2=\lambda^2 \), since \( \mathbf p \) is a unit vector. \[ \lambda^2=11\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda=\pm\sqrt{11} \](1)(1)
The move you reached for the wrong way round. In the drill you tried to prove "parallel" by crossing the two sides. The productive direction is the reverse: "parallel" is your licence to write one as a scalar multiple of the other — and once you have \( \mathbf q-3\mathbf r=\lambda\mathbf p \), dotting with itself converts an unusable vector equation into a scalar one. Remember \( \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v=|\mathbf v|^2 \).
Tests: zero vector product implies parallel implies scalar multiple; expanding a self scalar product; extracting an unknown scalar from magnitudes and an angle.

NJC 2009 Prelim P1 Q3 · HCI Supplementary Q4
The points \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) have position vectors \( 3\mathbf i-\mathbf j \), \( 5\mathbf i+2\mathbf j+\mathbf k \) and \( 7\mathbf i+2\mathbf j-\mathbf k \) respectively. (i) Calculate the exact area of the triangle \(ABC\). [2] The point \(N\) is on \(CB\) produced such that \( BN:BC=\lambda:1 \). (ii) Find \( \overrightarrow{AN} \) and determine the value of \( \lambda \) such that \( AN \) is perpendicular to \( BC \). [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) both vectors must start at the SAME vertex of THAT triangle
For triangle \(ABC\), use \( \overrightarrow{AB} \) and \( \overrightarrow{AC} \) — both from \(A\). \[ \overrightarrow{AB}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \overrightarrow{AC}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\3\\-1\end{pmatrix} \] \[ \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}=\begin{pmatrix}-6\\6\\-6\end{pmatrix}=-6\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix} \](1) \[ \text{Area}=\tfrac12\left|\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}\right|=\tfrac12(6\sqrt3)=3\sqrt3\ \text{units}^2 \](1)
(ii) "on \(CB\) produced" fixes the direction of travel
\(N\) lies beyond \(B\) on the ray from \(C\) through \(B\), so step from \(B\) in the direction \( \overrightarrow{CB} \): \( \overrightarrow{BN}=\lambda\,\overrightarrow{CB}=\lambda(-2,0,2) \). \[ \overrightarrow{ON}=\begin{pmatrix}5\\2\\1\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}-2\\0\\2\end{pmatrix};\qquad \overrightarrow{AN}=\begin{pmatrix}2-2\lambda\\3\\1+2\lambda\end{pmatrix} \](1)(1) Perpendicular means the dot product with \( \overrightarrow{BC}=(2,0,-2) \) is zero. \[ 2(2-2\lambda)+0-2(1+2\lambda)=0\ \Rightarrow\ 2-8\lambda=0 \](1) \[ \lambda=\tfrac14 \](1)
Your Q1 slip, fixed. In the drill you started the area of triangle \(OAC\) using a vector involving \(B\) — "there ain't no B there, that's my first mistake." Rule: for triangle \(XYZ\) use \( \overrightarrow{XY} \) and \( \overrightarrow{XZ} \) — only vertices of that triangle, both from the same one. And note the two products doing different jobs in one question: cross for the area, dot for the perpendicularity.

Question 4 [10]
AJC 2010 Prelim P1 Q13 · HCI Supplementary Q21
The plane \( \pi_1 \) and the line \(l\) are given by \( \pi_1:\ \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}=6 \) and \( l:\ \dfrac{x-1}{3}=\dfrac{y+4}{2}=z-5 \). The point \(A\) has position vector \( 3\mathbf i-\mathbf j+4\mathbf k \). (i) Find the distance between the point \(A\) and the plane \( \pi_1 \). [2] (ii) \(B\) is another point such that \( \overrightarrow{AB}=-5\mathbf j-2\mathbf k \). Find the length of projection of \( \overrightarrow{AB} \) onto the plane \( \pi_1 \). [2] (iii) Using your answers to (i) and (ii), find the area of triangle \(ABC\), where \(C\) is the reflection of \(A\) in the plane \( \pi_1 \). [2] (iv) Find the equation of the plane \( \pi_2 \) which contains the line \(l\) and the origin. Hence find the line of intersection of the planes \( \pi_1 \) and \( \pi_2 \). [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) point to plane — DOT with the unit normal
\( \mathbf n=(2,1,-2) \), \( |\mathbf n|=3 \), plane constant \(d=6\). \[ \text{distance}=\frac{\left|\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n-d\right|}{|\mathbf n|}=\frac{|6-1-8-6|}{3}=\frac{9}{3}=3\ \text{units} \](1)(1)
(ii) length of projection onto a PLANE — CROSS with the unit normal
Projecting onto the plane keeps the component perpendicular to \( \mathbf n \), which is exactly \( \left|\overrightarrow{AB}\times\hat{\mathbf n}\right| \). \[ \overrightarrow{AB}\times\mathbf n=\begin{pmatrix}0\\-5\\-2\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}12\\-4\\10\end{pmatrix} \](1) \[ \text{length}=\frac{\left|(12,-4,10)\right|}{3}=\frac{\sqrt{260}}{3}=\frac{2\sqrt{65}}{3}\ \text{units} \](1)
(iii) put the two answers together
\(C\) is the mirror image of \(A\), so \( AC=2\times3=6 \) and \( \overrightarrow{AC} \) is along \( \mathbf n \). Taking \(AC\) as the base, the height is the part of \( \overrightarrow{AB} \) perpendicular to \( \mathbf n \) — which is the answer to (ii). \[ \text{Area}=\tfrac12\times AC\times\left(\text{projection of }\overrightarrow{AB}\text{ on }\pi_1\right)=\tfrac12(6)\left(\tfrac{2\sqrt{65}}{3}\right)=2\sqrt{65}\ \text{units}^2 \](1)(1)
(iv) plane through a line and the origin; then the two planes meet
\(l\) has point \( (1,-4,5) \) and direction \( (3,2,1) \). The plane also contains \(O\), so \( (1,-4,5) \) itself is a second in-plane direction. \[ \mathbf n_2=\begin{pmatrix}3\\2\\1\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}1\\-4\\5\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}14\\-14\\-14\end{pmatrix}=14\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix} \](1) \[ \pi_2:\ \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix}=0\quad\text{(through the origin, so }d=0) \](1) Line of intersection: direction \( =\mathbf n_1\times\mathbf n_2 \); a point satisfying both, by inspection, is \( (2,2,0) \). \[ \mathbf n_1\times\mathbf n_2=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-3\\0\\-3\end{pmatrix}=-3\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} \](1) \[ \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\\0\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb R \](1)
Projection onto a LINE vs onto a PLANE. Onto a line (or a normal) you dot: \( \left|\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\hat{\mathbf d}\right| \). Onto a plane you cross with the normal: \( \left|\overrightarrow{AB}\times\hat{\mathbf n}\right| \) — because you want what survives after removing the normal component. You said in the drill "I forgot the projection factor"; this is the harder half of it.

Question 6 [8]
A-Level 2016 P1 Q10
The plane \(p\) has equation \( \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-3\\2\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\0\end{pmatrix}+\mu\begin{pmatrix}a\\4\\-2\end{pmatrix} \), and the line \(l\) has equation \( \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}a-1\\a\\a+1\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\\2\end{pmatrix} \), where \(a\) is a constant and \( \lambda,\mu,t \) are parameters. (i) In the case \(a=0\), show that \(l\) is perpendicular to \(p\), and find the values of \( \lambda \), \( \mu \) and \(t\) giving the point where \(l\) and \(p\) intersect. [5] (ii) Find the value of \(a\) such that \(l\) and \(p\) do not meet in a unique point. [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) line ⊥ plane means direction is PARALLEL to the normal
With \(a=0\), the plane's two directions are \( (1,2,0) \) and \( (0,4,-2) \). \[ \mathbf n=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\0\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}0\\4\\-2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-4\\2\\4\end{pmatrix}=2\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\\2\end{pmatrix} \](1) The line's direction is \( (-2,1,2) \), which is a scalar multiple of \( \mathbf n \). \[ \mathbf d=\tfrac12\mathbf n\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf d\parallel\mathbf n\ \Rightarrow\ l\perp p\ \text{(shown)} \](1)
then find the intersection
\( p:\ \mathbf r\cdot(-2,1,2)=(1,-3,2)\cdot(-2,1,2)=-1 \), i.e. \( 2x-y-2z=1 \). With \(a=0\), \( l:\ \mathbf r=(-1,0,1)+t(-2,1,2) \). \[ 2(-1-2t)-(t)-2(1+2t)=1\ \Rightarrow\ -4-9t=1\ \Rightarrow\ t=-\tfrac59 \](1) \[ \text{Point}=\left(\tfrac19,\,-\tfrac59,\,-\tfrac19\right) \](1) Matching against the plane's parametric form: \(x\): \(1+\lambda=\tfrac19\Rightarrow\lambda=-\tfrac89\); \(z\): \(2-2\mu=-\tfrac19\Rightarrow\mu=\tfrac{19}{18}\). \[ \lambda=-\tfrac89,\quad \mu=\tfrac{19}{18},\quad t=-\tfrac59 \](1)
(ii) "no unique point" means the line is PARALLEL to the plane
Parallel to the plane means the direction is perpendicular to the normal — the opposite test to (i). \[ \mathbf n(a)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\0\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}a\\4\\-2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-4\\2\\4-2a\end{pmatrix} \](1) \[ \begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\\2\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}-4\\2\\4-2a\end{pmatrix}=8+2+8-4a=18-4a=0 \](1) \[ a=\tfrac92 \](1)

HCI Supplementary Q11
A line \(l\) has equation \( \mathbf r=7\mathbf i+4\mathbf j-3\mathbf k+t(\mathbf i+\mathbf j+\mathbf k) \), where \( t\in\mathbb R \). (i) Find the coordinates of \(N\), the foot of the perpendicular from the point \( B(6,4,4) \) to \(l\). Hence find the shortest distance from \(B\) to \(l\). [4] (ii) Find the coordinates of the points on \(l\) which are at a distance of \( \sqrt{48} \) units from \(N\). [2] (iii) Find the point \(P\) on the line passing through \(N\) and \(B\) which is \( \sqrt{342} \) units from \(l\) and is on the same side of \(l\) as \(B\). [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) foot of perpendicular to a LINE — dot with the direction
\( \overrightarrow{ON}=(7+t,\,4+t,\,-3+t) \), so \( \overrightarrow{BN}=(1+t,\,t,\,-7+t) \). Impose \( \overrightarrow{BN}\cdot\mathbf d=0 \) with \( \mathbf d=(1,1,1) \). \[ (1+t)+t+(-7+t)=0\ \Rightarrow\ 3t-6=0\ \Rightarrow\ t=2 \](1)(1) \[ N=(9,\,6,\,-1) \](1) \[ \text{shortest distance}=\left|\overrightarrow{BN}\right|=\left|(3,2,-5)\right|=\sqrt{38}\ \text{units} \](1)
(ii) travel a set distance along the UNIT direction, both ways
\( \hat{\mathbf d}=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1,1,1) \), and \( \dfrac{\sqrt{48}}{\sqrt3}=\sqrt{16}=4 \), so step \( \pm4(1,1,1) \) from \(N\). \[ (9,6,-1)\pm 4(1,1,1)\ \Rightarrow\ (13,\,10,\,3)\quad\text{and}\quad(5,\,2,\,-5) \](1)(1)
(iii) same idea, now along \( \overrightarrow{NB} \)
\( \overrightarrow{NB}=(-3,-2,5) \) with \( \left|\overrightarrow{NB}\right|=\sqrt{38} \). Distance from \(l\) is measured along \( \overrightarrow{NB} \), so scale it: \( \dfrac{\sqrt{342}}{\sqrt{38}}=\sqrt9=3 \). \[ \overrightarrow{OP}=\overrightarrow{ON}+3\,\overrightarrow{NB}=(9,6,-1)+3(-3,-2,5) \](1)(1) \[ P=(0,\,0,\,14) \](1) The \(+3\) (not \(-3\)) puts \(P\) on the same side as \(B\).
One move, three uses. "Go a distance \(k\) from a known point in a known direction" is always \( \text{point}+k\,\hat{\mathbf v} \). Divide the required distance by \( |\mathbf v| \) to get the multiplier — that is why \( \sqrt{48}/\sqrt3=4 \) and \( \sqrt{342}/\sqrt{38}=3 \). Parts (ii) and (iii) look different but are the same step along different directions.

TJC 2009 Prelim P1 Q6 · HCI Supplementary Q5
Referred to the origin \(O\), the position vectors of \(A\) and \(B\) are \( \mathbf i+3\mathbf j+2\mathbf k \) and \( 3\mathbf i+2\mathbf j-\mathbf k \) respectively. (i) Find the position vector of \(M\), where \(M\) is the mid-point of \(A\) and \(B\). [2] (ii) Find the cosine of angle \(AOB\). [2] (iii) \(OM\) is extended such that \( \overrightarrow{OX}=2\,\overrightarrow{OM} \). Using the result in (ii), find the exact area of \(OAXB\). What is the geometrical shape of \(OAXB\)? [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) midpoint
\[ \overrightarrow{OM}=\frac{\overrightarrow{OA}+\overrightarrow{OB}}{2}=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}4\\5\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\2.5\\0.5\end{pmatrix} \](1)(1)
(ii) angle between two vectors — no modulus
\( \left|\overrightarrow{OA}\right|=\sqrt{1+9+4}=\sqrt{14} \) and \( \left|\overrightarrow{OB}\right|=\sqrt{9+4+1}=\sqrt{14} \). \[ \cos A\hat OB=\frac{(1,3,2)\cdot(3,2,-1)}{\sqrt{14}\sqrt{14}}=\frac{3+6-2}{14}=\frac{7}{14}=\frac12 \](1)(1)

\( \overrightarrow{OX}=2\overrightarrow{OM}=\overrightarrow{OA}+\overrightarrow{OB} \) — the diagonal rule — so \(OAXB\) is a parallelogram. \[ \text{Area}=\left|\overrightarrow{OA}\times\overrightarrow{OB}\right|=\left|(-7,7,-7)\right|=7\sqrt3\ \text{units}^2 \](1)(1) Alternatively from (ii): \( \sin\theta=\sqrt{1-\tfrac14}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \), so area \( =|\overrightarrow{OA}||\overrightarrow{OB}|\sin\theta=14\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}=7\sqrt3 \).(1) Since \( \left|\overrightarrow{OA}\right|=\left|\overrightarrow{OB}\right|=\sqrt{14} \), all four sides are equal. \[ OAXB\ \text{is a \textbf{rhombus}.} \](1)

Here (ii) gives \( \cos\theta \), and \( \sin\theta=\sqrt{1-\cos^2\theta} \) feeds \( |\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\sin\theta \) directly. Also: \( \overrightarrow{OX}=\overrightarrow{OA}+\overrightarrow{OB} \) is the parallelogram signature — and equal side lengths upgrade it to a rhombus.
