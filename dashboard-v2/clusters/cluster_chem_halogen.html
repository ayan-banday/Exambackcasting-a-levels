Question 2 (HCI Tutorial 15A Q1)

Question
(a) The boiling points of chloroethane, bromoethane and iodoethane increase in that order. Explain this trend in terms of intermolecular forces. [2]
(b) Chloroethane and butane have similar relative molecular masses, yet chloroethane has the higher boiling point. Explain why. [2]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a) The halide trend
The size of the electron cloud (or number of electrons) for the molecule increases in the order \(\ce{C2H5Cl}\) < \(\ce{C2H5Br}\) < \(\ce{C2H5I}\), since there is an increase in size of the halogen atom from Cl, Br, to I. Thus, intermolecular dispersion forces increase in strength in the same order (1). Energy needed to overcome intermolecular attractions increases in the same order, thus boiling point increases in the same order (1).
(b) Chloroethane vs butane
Chloroethane is polar; its intermolecular permanent dipole–permanent dipole attractions are stronger than the dispersion forces between non-polar butane molecules (1). More energy is needed to overcome the stronger intermolecular forces, so chloroethane has a higher boiling point (1).
Two different arguments. Between the halides (a) the discriminator is electron-cloud size → dispersion, NOT polarity. Against a non-polar alkane of similar \(M_r\) (b) it flips to pd–pd. Use the right one for the right comparison.
Solution hidden — attempt first
Archetype
Boiling-point reasoning: dispersion (same group, different halogen) vs pd–pd (vs alkane).

Pick the intermolecular-force argument that matches what is being compared.

Question 4 (HCI Tutorial 15A Q5a)

Question
2-chloro-2-methylpropane, \(\ce{(CH3)3CCl}\), reacts with alcoholic sodium cyanide to form a nitrile.
Name the mechanism, and draw it — labelling the slow and fast steps and showing the intermediate. [4]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution
Name of mechanism: unimolecular nucleophilic substitution, SN1 (1).
Mark scheme
Step 1 (slow, rds): curly arrow from the C–Cl bond to Cl; heterolytic fission gives the tertiary carbocation \(\ce{(CH3)3C+}\) + \(\ce{Cl-}\), with δ+/δ− shown on C–Cl (1). Step 2 (fast): the lone pair on the carbon of \(\ce{:CN-}\) attacks the positively charged carbon (1) → nitrile product \(\ce{(CH3)3CCN}\) (1).
The "1" is the order, not the step count — SN1 is a two-step mechanism. Cyanide donates the lone pair on C (not N). Rate = k[RX] only: adding more \(\ce{CN-}\) does not change the rate.
Solution hidden — attempt first
Archetype
Draw an SN1 on a tertiary substrate — two steps via a planar carbocation.

Slow C–X heterolysis (rds) → carbocation → fast nucleophile capture; no transition state required in H2.

Question 5 (HCI Tutorial 15A Q5b)

Question
Chloroethane reacts predominantly by SN2, whereas 2-chloro-2-methylpropane reacts predominantly by SN1.
Explain this difference, referring to both steric hindrance and carbocation stability. [4]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
Chloroethane (primary) → SN2
SN2 is favoured because chloroethane is a primary halogenoalkane. The nucleophile can easily approach the electron-deficient carbon as there is little steric hindrance from only one alkyl group (1). Should the reaction proceed via SN1, the primary carbocation \(\ce{CH3CH2+}\) would be relatively unstable since there is only one electron-donating alkyl group attached to C⁺ (1).
2-chloro-2-methylpropane (tertiary) → SN1
SN1 is favoured because it is a tertiary halogenoalkane. The tertiary carbocation \(\ce{(CH3)3C+}\) is relatively stable since there are three electron-donating alkyl groups attached to C⁺ (1). SN2 is not favoured because of the steric hindrance from the three alkyl groups that obstruct the approach of the nucleophile towards the electron-deficient carbon (1).
You must quote both mechanisms for both substrates. For each, say why its favoured route works and why the other route is shut off — that is what banks all four marks.
Solution hidden — attempt first
Archetype
Justify the mechanism from the substrate class using the two competing factors.

Carbocation stability drives SN1 up 1°→3°; steric hindrance drives SN2 down 1°→3°.

Question

(a)(i) 1-bromopropane + \(\ce{NH3}\)   (ii) bromoethane + \(\ce{CH3NH2}\)   (iii) 4-bromobutan-1-amine, \(\ce{H2N(CH2)4Br}\), intramolecular.
(b)(i) bromoethane + \(\ce{OH-}\)   (ii) bromoethane + \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}\)   (iii) 4-bromobutan-1-ol, \(\ce{HO(CH2)4Br}\), intramolecular.
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution
(a) Nitrogen nucleophiles
(i) \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2NH2}\), propan-1-amine (1) · (ii) \(\ce{CH3CH2NHCH3}\), N-methylethanamine (a secondary amine) (1) · (iii) a 5-membered N-ring, pyrrolidine (1).
(b) Oxygen nucleophiles
(i) \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\), ethanol (1) · (ii) \(\ce{CH3CH2OCH2CH2CH3}\), an ether (ethoxypropane) (1) · (iii) a 5-membered O-ring, oxolane (tetrahydrofuran) (1).

Question 8 (HCI lecture Ex 3.5)

Question
2-bromobutane is heated with hot ethanolic \(\ce{NaOH}\).
Draw the structural formulae of the three alkenes formed, and state which is the major product with a reason. [3]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution
The three alkenes: but-1-ene \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH3}\) (1); cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene, \(\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3}\) — the two cis/trans isomers (1). The major products are the but-2-enes: the \(\ce{C=C}\) carries more alkyl groups (more substituted → more stable alkene), by Saytzeff's rule (1).
Number of alkenes = number of different types of β-hydrogen. 2-bromobutane has two types → but-1-ene (from the terminal \(\ce{CH3}\)) and but-2-ene, which itself splits into cis and trans. Miss the cis/trans and you drop the second mark.

Fluoroethane, chloroethane, bromoethane and iodoethane are each hydrolysed by \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\).

(b) Arrange the four in order of increasing rate of hydrolysis, and explain the order using bond energies. [3]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a) Reagents/conditions
\(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\), heat (1).
(b) Increasing rate + reasoning
Increasing rate of hydrolysis: fluoroethane, chloroethane, bromoethane, iodoethane (1). As the size of the halogen atom increases, the bond lengths increase C–F < C–Cl < C–Br < C–I, and the bond strengths decrease C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I (1). Hence the rate of hydrolysis, which is a nucleophilic substitution requiring the C–X bond to break in the rate-determining step, increases in that order — C–I is weakest so breaks most easily, giving iodoethane the fastest rate (1).

Question 10 (HCI Tutorial 15A Q8)

Question
Chlorobenzene is far less reactive towards nucleophilic substitution than chloroethane.
Give two reasons for this lack of reactivity. [4]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
Reason 1 — partial double-bond character
The lone pair of electrons on Cl delocalises over the benzene ring (1). This gives the C–Cl bond partial double-bond character, making it very strong and not easily broken (1).
Reason 2 — steric + electronic blocking
The nucleophile would be repelled by the delocalised π-electron cloud of the benzene ring (1). The benzene ring itself also causes steric hindrance, "blocking" the nucleophile from approaching the C–Cl carbon (1).
Both reasons required. The same delocalisation makes any halogen on an sp² carbon (aryl or vinyl) unreactive; it also can't go SN1 because the would-be carbocation's empty p-orbital is orthogonal to the ring π system.
Solution hidden — attempt first
Archetype
Why an sp²-C–halogen resists nucleophilic substitution — two-reason answer.

Delocalisation → partial double-bond character (stronger C–X); ring blocks the rear and repels the nucleophile.

Question 11 (HCI Tutorial 15A Q9)

Question
1-bromopropane, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2Br}\), and bromobenzene, \(\ce{C6H5Br}\), are two colourless liquids.
Describe a chemical test, with reagents, conditions and the observation for each compound, that distinguishes them. [5]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
Test procedure
Add \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\) and heat. Then cool, then add excess dilute \(\ce{HNO3}\). Then add \(\ce{AgNO3(aq)}\). (1) (1) (the excess \(\ce{HNO3}\) neutralises unreacted \(\ce{OH-}\), which would otherwise give a brown \(\ce{Ag2O}\) precipitate) (1).
Observations
\(\ce{CH3CH2CH2Br}\) gives a cream precipitate upon addition of \(\ce{AgNO3(aq)}\) (1). \(\ce{C6H5Br}\) does not give a precipitate (1).
Why: 1-bromopropane undergoes nucleophilic substitution — the C–Br breaks to release \(\ce{Br-}\), which reacts with \(\ce{Ag+(aq)}\) to give \(\ce{AgBr(s)}\). Bromobenzene does not undergo nucleophilic substitution (sp² C–Br, partial double-bond character), so no free \(\ce{Br-}\) and no precipitate. (Ethanolic \(\ce{AgNO3}\) with heating is also acceptable.)
A test answer needs both outcomes — the change for one compound AND "no precipitate" for the other. The AgBr colour is cream (AgCl white, AgI yellow); confirm a cream ppt by dissolving it in concentrated ammonia.
Solution hidden — attempt first
Archetype
Silver-halide distinguishing test: hydrolyse → excess HNO₃ → AgNO₃, halogenoalkane vs halogenoarene.

Only an sp³ C–X frees a halide ion to precipitate AgX; the aryl halide gives nothing.

Question 12 (HCI Tutorial 15B Q10 & Q14)

Question
Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) such as \(\ce{CCl2F2}\) were once widely used before being linked to ozone depletion.
(a) State one use of CFCs. [1]
(b) In the upper atmosphere, state which bond in a CFC breaks under UV light, the type of bond fission, and the species formed. [2]
(c) Butane is proposed as a replacement propellant. Explain, in terms of bonds, why butane does not deplete ozone. [2]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a) Use
Refrigerants (in air-conditioners, refrigerators), or aerosol propellants. (1)
(b) Which bond, what fission, what forms
UV light initiates homolytic fission of the C–Cl bond (1), forming \(\ce{Cl\bullet}\) (chlorine) free radicals (1).
(c) Why butane is safe
The C–H and C–C bonds in butane contain no C–Cl (or C–Br, C–I) bond (1). C–H (410) and C–C bonds are too strong to be broken by UV to give halogen radicals, so no \(\ce{Cl\bullet}\)/\(\ce{Br\bullet}\) is produced to destroy ozone (1).
It is the C–Cl bond, not C–F, that does the damage (C–F 485 too strong). The \(\ce{Cl\bullet}\) is regenerated in the chain, so it acts catalytically. C–Br is even weaker than C–Cl, so bromo compounds are worse, not safer.
Solution hidden — attempt first
Archetype
CFCs & ozone: which bond breaks, why, and what makes a safe substitute.

UV homolyses C–Cl → catalytic Cl•; a molecule with no C–Cl/Br/I is safe (though flammability/GWP still matter).
