Question 1 (HCI lecture, Exercise 1.1)

Question
For each compound, give the IUPAC name and classify it as a primary, secondary or tertiary alcohol, or as a phenol.
(a) \(\ce{BrCH2CH(OH)C(CH3)3}\) [2]
(b) \(\ce{C6H5CH2OH}\) — and state clearly why it is not classed as a phenol. [2]
(c) a benzene ring bearing \(\ce{-OH}\), with \(\ce{-Br}\) and \(\ce{-CH3}\) also on the ring (\(\ce{-OH}\) at C1, \(\ce{-CH3}\) at C2, \(\ce{-Br}\) at C5). [2]

Solution
(a)
1-bromo-3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol (1); the C bearing \(\ce{-OH}\) carries two alkyl groups → secondary alcohol (1).
(b)
Phenylmethanol (one H of methanol replaced by a phenyl group) (1). It is a primary alcohol, not a phenol, because the \(\ce{-OH}\) is on an sp³ \(\ce{CH2}\) carbon, not bonded directly to the benzene ring; that carbon bears only one alkyl (phenyl) group → primary (1).

5-bromo-2-methylphenol (number to give the substituents the lowest locants; cite alphabetically, so bromo before methyl) (1). Classed simply as a phenol — the \(\ce{-OH}\) is bonded directly to the ring, so it is not called 1°/2°/3° (1).

Question 2 (HCI lecture, Exercise 2.1)

Question
(a) Arrange ethane, ethanol and ethane-1,2-diol in order of increasing boiling point, and explain the order. [3]
(b) Hexan-1-ol (b.p. 158 °C) and 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol (b.p. 120 °C) are isomers of formula \(\ce{C6H14O}\). Explain the difference in their boiling points. [3]
(c) State and explain how the solubility of alcohols in water changes as the carbon chain lengthens. [1]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a) Increasing b.p.
ethane < ethanol < ethane-1,2-diol (1). Ethane has no \(\ce{-OH}\), so only dispersion forces and cannot hydrogen bond → lowest (1). Ethane-1,2-diol has two \(\ce{-OH}\) groups → a greater probability of forming more extensive AND stronger hydrogen bonds than ethanol (one \(\ce{-OH}\)), so more energy is needed to overcome the intermolecular attractions → highest (1).
(b) Hexan-1-ol vs its branched isomer
They are isomers with the same number of \(\ce{-OH}\) groups → similar hydrogen-bonding strength. The straight-chain hexan-1-ol has a larger surface area than the branched 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol (1), so there is a greater chance of contact / distortion → stronger dispersion forces (1). More energy is needed to overcome them, so hexan-1-ol has the higher boiling point (1).
(c) Solubility falls
Solubility decreases as the chain lengthens: the longer non-polar chain has stronger dispersion forces, and the energy released on forming \(\ce{-OH}\)···water hydrogen bonds is no longer enough to overcome them (and the long chain disrupts water's H-bonding) (1).
Same-number-of-OH check first. In (b) you must state they are isomers with equal H-bonding before attributing the difference to dispersion — otherwise the examiner can't tell you've ruled out H-bonding. Never explain any of these trends by "polarity".
Solution hidden — attempt first
Archetype
Boiling point / solubility by hydrogen bonding: number of –OH, then chain shape (dispersion).

More –OH → stronger H-bonding; equal –OH → decide on surface area/dispersion; solubility falls as the chain grows.

Question 3 (HCI lecture Ex 4.1 + Class Exercise 1, Q1)

Question
(a) Arrange propan-1-ol, 2-chloropropan-1-ol and 1-chloropropan-1-ol in order of increasing acid strength, explaining your order by reference to the conjugate bases. [3]
(b) Explain, in terms of the conjugate bases, why phenol has a lower \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) than propan-1-ol. [3]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a) Increasing acid strength
propan-1-ol < 2-chloropropan-1-ol < 1-chloropropan-1-ol (1). Propan-1-oxide has no electron-withdrawing group; the alkyl group is electron-donating and intensifies the negative charge on the O → least stable conjugate base → weakest acid (1). The Cl is electron-withdrawing and disperses/stabilises the negative charge through the σ (sigma) bonding framework; because the inductive effect weakens with distance, Cl on the α-carbon (1-chloro) withdraws more strongly than Cl on the β-carbon (2-chloro) → the 1-chloro conjugate base is most stabilised → strongest acid (1).
(b) Phenol vs propan-1-ol
Compare phenoxide with propan-1-oxide. In phenoxide, a lone pair on the O is parallel to the p orbitals of the ring, so the negative charge is partially delocalised into the benzene ring (1) — it is only "partially" delocalised because oxygen is still more electronegative than carbon (1). This stabilises phenoxide far more than propan-1-oxide (whose alkyl group only intensifies the charge), so phenol dissociates to a greater extent → stronger acid → lower \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) (1).
Compare like with like. The comparison is phenoxide vs alkoxide, not "phenol vs alkoxide". Use "partially" delocalised, never "fully". Route every acidity argument through conjugate-base stability and name the σ framework for the inductive withdrawal (all carbons sp³, no π system).
Solution hidden — attempt first
Archetype
Acid-strength ranking by conjugate-base stability — EWG distance effect + phenoxide delocalisation.

EDG destabilises / EWG stabilises the anion; the nearer the EWG the stronger; ring delocalisation makes phenol the strongest.

Question 4 (HCI Class Exercise 2, Q2 + acid–base LO)

Question
(a) Arrange 4-nitrophenol, phenol and 4-methoxyphenol in order of increasing \(\mathrm{p}K_a\), explaining your order. [3]
(b) Phenol reacts with aqueous sodium hydroxide but ethanol does not. Explain why, and write an equation for the reaction of phenol with \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\). [2]
(c) State what is observed when \(\ce{Na2CO3(aq)}\) is added separately to phenol and to a carboxylic acid, and why this distinguishes them. [1]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a) Increasing pKa (strongest acid → weakest)
Increasing pKa means decreasing acid strength — the strongest acid comes first.
4-nitrophenol < phenol < 4-methoxyphenol (1). The electron-withdrawing \(\ce{-NO2}\) further delocalises / disperses the negative charge on the O of the phenoxide → more stable conjugate base → stronger acid → lower \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) (1). The electron-donating \(\ce{-OCH3}\) pushes electron density into the ring and prevents the phenoxide charge from delocalising appreciably → less stable conjugate base → weaker acid → higher \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) (1).
(b) Phenol reacts with NaOH; ethanol does not
Phenol is a stronger acid than water, so it is acidic enough to react with \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\); ethanol is a weaker acid than water, so it does not (1).
\(\ce{C6H5OH + NaOH -> C6H5O^{-}Na^{+} + H2O}\) (1).
(c) Carbonate test
The carboxylic acid gives effervescence of \(\ce{CO2}\) (it is a stronger acid than carbonic acid); phenol does NOT react / no effervescence (phenol is a weaker acid than carbonic acid) — so \(\ce{Na2CO3}\) distinguishes them (1).
Direction of "increasing pKa". It runs strongest acid → weakest acid. And keep the ladder straight: phenol reacts with \(\ce{NaOH}\) but not with carbonate; an alcohol reacts with neither; a carboxylic acid reacts with both.

Archetype
Substituent effect on phenol acidity + the NaOH / carbonate acidity ladder.

EWG lowers pKa, EDG raises it; phenol reacts with NaOH only, carboxylic acid with carbonate too — the classic separation.

Question 5 (HCI lecture, Exercise Q2 — deduction)

Question
Four liquids — A, B, C, D — are, in some order, ethanol, propan-1-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol and cyclohexene. The following is observed:
• with \(\ce{Na(s)}\): A, C and D effervesce; B does not.
• with \(\ce{I2(aq)} + \ce{NaOH}\), warm: only C gives a yellow precipitate.
• with acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), heat: A stays orange; C and D turn green.
(a) Write an equation for the reaction of ethanol with sodium, name the reaction type, and explain why it is classed as redox. [2]
(b) Identify A, B, C and D, giving your reasoning. [4]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution
(a) Ethanol + Na
\(\ce{2C2H5OH + 2Na -> 2C2H5O^{-}Na^{+} + H2}\) (1). It is an acid–metal (redox) reaction: Na is oxidised (0 → +1) and the protonic H is reduced (+1 → 0 in \(\ce{H2}\)) (1).
(b) Identify A–D
B = cyclohexene — it has no protonic (O–H) hydrogen, so no effervescence with Na (1). C = ethanol — the only one giving a positive tri-iodomethane test (it carries the \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}\) feature with R = H), and it is oxidised by dichromate (green) (1). A = 2-methylpropan-2-ol — a tertiary alcohol, so it is not oxidised and stays orange (1). D = propan-1-ol by elimination — a primary alcohol, oxidised (green) but iodoform-negative (1).
Read each test as a discriminator. Na separates the alkene (no O–H) from the alcohols; iodoform picks out ethanol; dichromate picks out the tertiary alcohol (stays orange). Propan-1-ol is left by elimination — always name it explicitly.

Question 6 (HCI lecture Ex 4.4 + oxidation LO)

Question
(a) A primary alcohol \(\ce{RCH2OH}\) is oxidised. Give the reagents/conditions and the organic product to obtain (i) the aldehyde and (ii) the carboxylic acid, and state the difference in apparatus and why it is needed. [4]
(b) State the organic product and the colour change (if any) when acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) is heated with (i) a secondary alcohol and (ii) a tertiary alcohol. [2]
(c) A molecule contains both a \(\ce{C=C}\) and a secondary \(\ce{-OH}\). State how acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) and hot acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) would act differently on it. [1]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a) 1° → aldehyde vs → carboxylic acid
(i) Aldehyde \(\ce{RCHO}\): \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), dilute \(\ce{H2SO4}\), heat with immediate distillation — the aldehyde has no hydrogen bonding, so it is more volatile and distils off as it forms, before it can be oxidised further (1) (1). (ii) Carboxylic acid \(\ce{RCOOH}\): \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), dilute \(\ce{H2SO4}\), heat under reflux — reflux keeps the mixture reacting so oxidation goes all the way (1) (1).
(b) 2° and 3°
(i) Secondary → ketone; orange → green (1). (ii) Tertiary → no reaction; stays orange (no H on the carbon bearing \(\ce{-OH}\)) (1).
(c) C=C + 2° alcohol
\(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) oxidises only the alcohol → ketone and does NOT cleave the \(\ce{C=C}\); hot acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) also oxidatively cleaves the \(\ce{C=C}\) (1).
Never write "\(\ce{KMnO4}\), heat with immediate distillation". \(\ce{KMnO4}\) is too strong to stop at the aldehyde — it goes straight to the carboxylic acid. A simple test-tube test must not use reflux/distillation (keep it < 1 min, water bath).

1° stops at aldehyde only by distilling it off; 2° → ketone; 3° → nothing; only KMnO₄ cleaves the alkene.

Question 7 (HCI Class Exercise 1, Q3)

Question
Hexan-3-ol, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH(OH)CH2CH2CH3}\), is warmed with excess concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\).
(a) Name the reaction type and explain why the acid must be excess. [1]
(b) Draw the structural formulae of the four organic products. [3]
(c) Explain why cis and trans isomers count as separate products, and why phenol cannot undergo this reaction. [1]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution
(a) Reaction type
Dehydration (elimination of water; a β-elimination) (1). Excess concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) so it acts as a dehydrating agent and is not diluted by the water produced.
(b) The four alkenes
The –OH is on C3; the two β-carbons (C2 and C4) are different, so two positional alkenes form, each as a cis/trans pair.
hex-2-ene (cis + trans) (1) and hex-3-ene (cis + trans) (1) — four products in total, all four structures correct (1).
(c) Why 4, and why not phenol
cis and trans isomers are different, unique compounds → they count as separate organic products. Phenol cannot dehydrate — it has no β-hydrogen (no H on a carbon adjacent to the C–OH ring carbon) (1).
Number of alkenes = number of different β-hydrogens, then split each internal C=C into cis/trans. Miss the geometric isomers and you drop two products. A symmetrical alcohol (e.g. cyclohexanol) would give only one.

Question 8 (HCI lecture Ex 4.3 + substitution LO)

Question
(a) An alcohol's \(\ce{-OH}\) must be "activated" before a halide can substitute it. Explain why \(\ce{-OH}\) is a poor leaving group. [1]
(b) Give the reagent(s)/conditions and the organic product to convert 2-methylpropan-2-ol into (i) the chloroalkane using a phosphorus compound, and (ii) the bromoalkane using sodium bromide. [2]
(c) Thionyl chloride, \(\ce{SOCl2}\), is warmed with an alcohol. Name the two gases produced and state one advantage of this route. [2]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution
(a) Why –OH is a poor leaving group
If \(\ce{-OH}\) left directly it would leave as hydroxide, \(\ce{OH-}\), which is a strong base and strong nucleophile — so it is reluctant to leave; the \(\ce{-OH}\) must first be converted into a better leaving group (1).
(b) To the halogenoalkane
(i) \(\ce{PCl5}\) (or \(\ce{PCl3}\)), room temperature → 2-chloro-2-methylpropane, \(\ce{(CH3)3CCl}\); dense white fumes of \(\ce{HCl}\) (1). (ii) \(\ce{NaBr}\) + concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) (generating \(\ce{HBr}\) in situ), heat → 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, \(\ce{(CH3)3CBr}\) (1).
(c) SOCl₂
The two gases are \(\ce{HCl}\) and \(\ce{SO2}\) (1). Advantage: both by-products are gases that escape, so the reaction is easily driven to completion (entropy-driven) and the organic product is easy to separate (1).
Match reagent to purpose. \(\ce{PCl5}\)/\(\ce{PCl3}\)/\(\ce{SOCl2}\) give the chloroalkane; for the bromo/iodo you need \(\ce{HBr}\)/\(\ce{HI}\) (in situ from \(\ce{NaBr}\)/\(\ce{NaI}\) + conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\)) or \(\ce{PBr3}\)/\(\ce{PI3}\) made in situ. \(\ce{PCl3}\) is a liquid, \(\ce{PCl5}\) a solid.

Archetype
Convert –OH to a halogenoalkane — why –OH must be activated, and the reagent menu (P halides, HX in situ, SOCl₂).

–OH is a poor leaving group (leaves as strong-base OH⁻); PCl₅/SOCl₂ → chloro, NaBr + conc H₂SO₄ → bromo.

Question 12 (HCI Class Exercise 2, Q2a)

Question
Phenol is nitrated by dilute \(\ce{HNO3}\) at room temperature, whereas methylbenzene requires concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\).
(a) Explain why phenol is so much more reactive towards nitration than methylbenzene. [2]
(b) State the role of the concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) in the nitration of methylbenzene, and name the electrophile it generates. [2]
(c) Write an equation for the reaction of phenol with \(\ce{HNO3}\) (one isomer), and state the by-product. [1]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a) Why phenol is more reactive
In phenol the lone pair of electrons on the oxygen directly overlaps with the π electron cloud of the ring, greatly increasing the ring's electron density — \(\ce{-OH}\) is much more electron-donating than \(\ce{-CH3}\) (1). The electron-rich ring is reactive enough to react with a weak electrophile (nitric acid itself) at room temperature, with no catalyst; methylbenzene's ring is far less activated, so it needs a strong electrophile forced out under harsher conditions (1).
(b) Role of conc H₂SO₄
Concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) protonates the nitric acid to generate the electrophile, the nitronium ion \(\ce{NO2^+}\) (1); it acts as both catalyst and dehydrating agent (1).
(c) Equation
\(\ce{C6H5OH + HNO3 -> C6H4(OH)(NO2) + H2O}\) (e.g. 4-nitrophenol); by-product is \(\ce{H2O}\) (1).
The activation is π-donation, not induction. The O lone pair conjugates into the ring (raising electron density); this is why phenol beats methylbenzene, whose \(\ce{CH3}\) donates only weakly. The nitration by-product is water, not \(\ce{H2}\).
Solution hidden — attempt first
Archetype
Compare aromatic reactivity: phenol (O lone pair → ring) vs methylbenzene; role of conc H₂SO₄ / NO₂⁺.

–OH activates far more than –CH₃, so dilute HNO₃ suffices; H₂SO₄ makes NO₂⁺ and dehydrates for the arene case.

Question 14 (HCI Class Exercise 1, Q2)

Question
Lactic acid (2-hydroxypropanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)COOH}\)) is made from prop-2-en-1-ol, \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2OH}\).
(a) Give the reagents/conditions to oxidise the primary alcohol to prop-2-enoic acid, \(\ce{CH2=CHCOOH}\) (compound X), and state why no aldehyde is obtained. [2]
(b) X is converted to lactic acid by hydrating the \(\ce{C=C}\). Give the two reagents in the correct order, and explain why the way you write the sequence matters for the marks. [2]
(c) Lactic acid is heated under reflux with a few drops of conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\), giving Y, \(\ce{C6H8O4}\). Deduce what has happened and identify the type of product. [2]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution
(a) Oxidise the 1° alcohol → X
\(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), dilute \(\ce{H2SO4}\), heat under reflux → prop-2-enoic acid \(\ce{CH2=CHCOOH}\) (1). No aldehyde is obtained because the setup is reflux (not immediate distillation), so the primary alcohol is completely oxidised to the carboxylic acid; the \(\ce{C=C}\) survives because acidified dichromate does not cleave \(\ce{C=C}\) (1).
(b) Hydration of the C=C — sequencing
Reagents, in order: 1. concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) (cold); then 2. \(\ce{H2O}\) (1). The sequence is itself marked: writing "conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\), cold, \(\ce{H2O}\)" lumped with commas reads as adding everything at once and is wrong — you must number the steps 1 and 2, or write "conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) followed by \(\ce{H2O}\)" (1).
(c) Deduce Y (C₆H₈O₄)
Y has 6 C — double the 3 C of lactic acid — so lactic acid has reacted with itself.
Two lactic acid molecules = \(\ce{2 C3H6O3 = C6H12O6}\); \(\ce{C6H12O6 - C6H8O4 = 2H2O}\), so two water molecules are lost → a double condensation (two ester linkages) (1). Because each lactic acid has both an \(\ce{-OH}\) and a \(\ce{-COOH}\), they cross-esterify twice to give a cyclic diester (a 6-membered ring, "lactide") (1). (An open-chain ester still bearing the alkene is also accepted; "few drops" of acid points to esterification, not dehydration.)
"Few drops" vs "excess". A few drops of conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) signals esterification (catalyst); excess conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) signals dehydration. Reflux → carboxylic acid; immediate distillation → aldehyde. Number sequential reagents — the examiner marks the order.

The four traps buried in this map. (1) Step 7 is not a different reagent from steps 5/6 — the only difference is reflux vs immediate distillation; never write "KMnO₄ with immediate distillation". (2) Step 3 uses aqueous NaOH — ethanolic NaOH would give elimination to ethene instead. (3) Phenol has no route into steps 2 or 9: it cannot be dehydrated (no β-hydrogen) and will not esterify with a carboxylic acid (the O lone pair is delocalised into the ring), which is exactly why step 13 goes via the more nucleophilic phenoxide + acyl chloride. (4) Step 12 dissolving phenol in NaOH is not the test for phenol — that is neutral FeCl₃ → violet/purple complex.
