How to read the tables

Prelims and A-Level are two independent counts of the same thing, kept separate on purpose so you can see where they agree. Marks is the blend of the two, scaled to that paper's mark total — treat it as the expected marks for that topic on a typical paper. In how many papers is the prelim figure: how often the topic shows up at all, which matters more than its average when you are deciding what you cannot afford to skip.

METHOD, AND WHAT NOT TO TRUST

  1. Two independent datasets. 593 questions from 53 prelim papers (2016–2025, across 19 JCs), and 119 questions from 12 A-Level papers (2017–2022). They were counted separately and are reported separately so you can see the agreement rather than take my word for it.
  2. Validation that the count is faithful. Every one of the 53 prelim papers reconciles to between 90 and 110 marks, and the Pure-to-Stats split falls out at 70 : 30 — which is what the syllabus specifies. Nothing was tuned to make that happen; it is what the data did.
  3. Marks, not question counts. A topic's share is its share of marks, which is the thing that decides your grade.
  4. The A-Level source is a school compilation labelled “Modified”, so a few stems have been reworded. Six questions were dropped because the PDF text ran them together and I could not split them cleanly. That is why some A-Level papers below total under 100.
  5. The 9758 syllabus started in 2017. Papers before that are 9740 and test different content, so they are excluded entirely.
  6. Where the two columns disagree by more than about 5 points, believe the prelims — there are four times as many of them. The exception is Section B, where both samples are large enough to trust.
  7. One judgement call worth knowing about. Some Pure questions genuinely straddle two topics (a parametric curve you then integrate, say). Each question is counted once, under the topic doing the most work. That slightly blurs the Graphs / Integration / Differentiation boundary, and it is the main reason those three move around between the two columns.
