Card 1 — Separation: one linear bracket vs partial fractions

The mistake: you tried to split \(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{9.8-2v}\,dv\) into partial fractions. You can't — there's nothing to split. Partial fractions is only for a denominator that is a product of two (or more) different linear factors. A single linear bracket integrates straight to a log: divide by the coefficient of \(v\) and carry the minus sign. Count the brackets in the denominator — one bracket → straight log; two multiplied → partial fractions.
Worked example — shown in full
Part A — one linear bracket (the one you over-thought)
Integrate \(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{9.8-2v}\,dv\).
Let the bracket be \(9.8-2v\); its derivative is \(-2\). Reverse the chain rule — divide by \(-2\):
\(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{9.8-2v}\,dv=-\frac12\ln|9.8-2v|+C\) (1). General rule: \(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{a-bv}\,dv=-\frac1b\ln|a-bv|+C\) (1).

Equivalently, factor the 2 out first: \(9.8-2v=2(4.9-v)\), so \(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{4.9-v}\,dv=-\ln|4.9-v|+C\) — the exact route the parachutist mark scheme takes. Same answer, no partial fractions anywhere.

Part B — product of two linear factors (this one DOES need it)
Integrate \(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{x(20-x)}\,dx\).
Denominator is \(x\) times \((20-x)\): two different linear factors, so split.
\(\displaystyle\frac{1}{x(20-x)}=\frac{1}{20}\!\left(\frac1x+\frac1{20-x}\right)\) (1)
\(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{x(20-x)}\,dx=\frac{1}{20}\big(\ln|x|-\ln|20-x|\big)+C=\frac{1}{20}\ln\left|\frac{x}{20-x}\right|+C\) (1).

Tell them apart: \(9.8-2v\) is one bracket → log. \(x(20-x)\) is two brackets multiplied → partial fractions.

Practice — harder (HCI Supplementary Q9 · SRJC/2005/C2Prelim/P1/Q14b)
The growth of a species of insect is studied. The rate of death is proportional to the number in thousands, \(x\), of insects; the rate of birth is proportional to \(x^2\). When \(x=2\), the number of larvae hatched equals the number that died. Show that \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=ax(x-2)\), where \(a\) is a constant. Given that \(x=a\) initially, find an equation relating \(x\), \(a\) and \(t\). [6]
Answer — verbatim HCI
Form the DE — two DIFFERENT constants
Let birth rate use \(h\) and death rate use \(k\), both positive constants.
\(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=hx^2-kx\), where \(h\) and \(k\) are positive constants. (1)
When \(x=2\), \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=0\): \(\ 4h=2k\Rightarrow k=2h\) (1)
\(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=hx^2-2hx=hx(x-2)=ax(x-2)\), where \(a=h\) (shown)
Separate — two linear factors ⇒ partial fractions
Denominator \(x(x-2)\) is a product of two linear factors, so split it.
\(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{x(x-2)}\,dx=a\int dt\ \Rightarrow\ \int\!\left(\frac{1/2}{x-2}-\frac{1/2}{x}\right)dx=a\int dt\) (1)
\(\displaystyle\int\!\left(\frac{1}{x-2}-\frac{1}{x}\right)dx=2a\int dt\)
Integrate and apply \(x=a\) at \(t=0\)
\(\ln|x-2|-\ln|x|=2at+C\ \Rightarrow\ \ln\left|\dfrac{x-2}{x}\right|=2at+C\) (1)
\(\dfrac{x-2}{x}=Ae^{2at}\), where \(A=\pm e^{C}\) (1)
When \(t=0\), \(x=a\): \(\ A=\dfrac{a-2}{a}\). Hence \(\displaystyle\frac{x-2}{x}=\frac{a-2}{a}\,e^{2at}\) (1)
Why harder: you had to (i) form the DE with two different constants (not one), (ii) recognise \(x(x-2)\) as a product of two linear factors → partial fractions, and (iii) leave the answer implicit (it never asks for \(x\) in terms of \(t\)). The example handed you the integral; here you build it and pick the method.

Card 2 — Forming a DE from a model + pinning the constant

Two moves. (1) Turn the given model into a first-order DE: substitute \(v=\dfrac{dx}{dt}\) so \(\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}=\dfrac{dv}{dt}\). (2) Pin the unknown constant by substituting the given rate and value — don't leave it as a letter. This is the step you fumbled: you had \(\dfrac{dv}{dt}=5\) and \(v=2.4\) sitting there and didn't plug them in.
Worked example — shown in full · N2024/P1/Q11(a)
The motion of a parachutist is modelled by \(\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}+\alpha\dfrac{dx}{dt}=9.8\), where \(\alpha\) is a constant.
(i) Using \(v=\dfrac{dx}{dt}\), write down a differential equation in \(v\) and \(t\). [1]
(ii) Given that \(\dfrac{dv}{dt}=5\) when \(v=2.4\), find the value of \(\alpha\). [1]
(i) Substitute to drop the order
\(v=\dfrac{dx}{dt}\ \Rightarrow\ \dfrac{dv}{dt}=\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}\). Substituting into the model:
\(\dfrac{dv}{dt}+\alpha v=9.8\) (1)
(ii) Substitute the given values to pin \(\alpha\)
Put \(\dfrac{dv}{dt}=5\) and \(v=2.4\) into the DE.
\(5+2.4\alpha=9.8\ \Rightarrow\ 2.4\alpha=4.8\ \Rightarrow\ \alpha=2\) (1)

That is the whole routine — reduce the order by substitution, then plug the given numbers to find the constant. Nothing more.

Practice — harder (HCI Tutorial 9 Q10 · NYJC/2015/C2BT2/1/7)
In a model of an ant colony, the birth rate is inversely proportional to the square of the population and the death rate is proportional to the population. The population (in thousands) is \(n\) at time \(t\) days. When the population was 3000 it stayed constant. Show that \(\dfrac{dn}{dt}=k\!\left(\dfrac{27}{n^2}-n\right)\), \(k\) a positive constant. [2]
(i) Show the population decreases if \(n\) exceeds a value to be found. [1]
(ii) The population was 4000 at the start and decreasing at \(\tfrac{37}{32}\) thousand/day at that instant. Find \(n\) in terms of \(t\). [6]
Answer — verbatim HCI
Form the DE — the two-constant trap
Birth rate and death rate need different constants of proportionality.
\(\dfrac{dn}{dt}=\dfrac{a}{n^2}-bn\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are positive constants. (1)
When \(n=3\), \(\dfrac{dn}{dt}=0\): \(\ \dfrac{a}{9}-3b=0\Rightarrow a=27b\) (1)
\(\dfrac{dn}{dt}=\dfrac{27b}{n^2}-bn=b\!\left(\dfrac{27}{n^2}-n\right)=k\!\left(\dfrac{27}{n^2}-n\right)\), where \(b=k\) (shown)
(i) When does it decrease?
\(\dfrac{dn}{dt}<0:\ k\!\left(\dfrac{27-n^3}{n^2}\right)<0\). Since \(k>0\) and \(n^2>0\), need \(27-n^3<0\Rightarrow n^3>27\Rightarrow n>3\). The population decreases if \(n>3\). (1)
(ii) Pin \(k\) from the given rate, then solve
Substitute \(n=4\), \(\dfrac{dn}{dt}=-\tfrac{37}{32}\) (note \(n=4\), not 4000 — population is in thousands).
\(-\dfrac{37}{32}=k\!\left(\dfrac{27}{16}-4\right)\Rightarrow k=\dfrac12\) (1)
\(\displaystyle\int\frac{n^2}{27-n^3}\,dn=\frac12\int dt\) (1)
\(-\dfrac13\ln|27-n^3|=\dfrac12 t+C\) (1)
\(27-n^3=Ae^{-\frac32 t}\), where \(A=\pm e^{-3C}\) (1)
When \(n=4\), \(t=0\): \(\ 64=27-A\Rightarrow A=-37\). Hence \(n=\big(27+37e^{-\frac32 t}\big)^{1/3}\) (1)
Why harder: the model needs two different constants of proportionality — writing \(\dfrac{dn}{dt}=\dfrac{k}{n^2}-kn\) is the classic error. You pin them from two separate conditions (stationary at \(n=3\), then a given rate at \(n=4\)), and the \(n^2\) fixes the substitution. The example pinned one constant from one condition; this pins two and then solves.

Card 3 — The \(a(1-e^{-bt})\) solution form

This is the shape that comes out of \(\dfrac{dv}{dt}=A-Bv\), and you fumbled every step. Five moves, always the same: separate → integrate (a \(-\ln\)) → exponentiate (the \(\pm\) becomes one constant) → apply \(t=0,\,v=0\) → rearrange to \(v=\dfrac{A}{B}(1-e^{-Bt})\). Read off \(a=\dfrac{A}{B}\) (the limit) and \(b=B\).
Worked example — shown in full · N2024/P1/Q11(b)
With \(\alpha=2\) and initial velocity zero, show that \(v=A(1-e^{Bt})\), where \(A\) and \(B\) are constants to be found. [5]
Separate
\(\dfrac{dv}{dt}+2v=9.8\Rightarrow\dfrac{dv}{dt}=9.8-2v=2(4.9-v)\). One linear bracket → straight to a log:
\(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{4.9-v}\,dv=\int 2\,dt\) (1)
Integrate, then exponentiate
\(-\ln|4.9-v|=2t+C\) (1)
\(4.9-v=\pm e^{-2t-C}=Ae^{-2t}\), where \(A=\pm e^{-C}\) (1)
Apply the initial condition, then rearrange
When \(t=0\), \(v=0\).
\(4.9=A(1)\Rightarrow A=4.9\) (1)
\(4.9-v=4.9e^{-2t}\Rightarrow v=4.9\big(1-e^{-2t}\big)\) (1), so \(A=4.9,\ B=-2\).

Read-off: the limit \(a=\dfrac{\text{constant}}{\text{coeff of }v}=\dfrac{9.8}{2}=4.9\), and \(b=\) the coefficient of \(v\) = 2. That is why the velocity levels off at 4.9.

Practice — harder (HCI Tutorial 9 Q12 · N2017/I/11 parts ii–iii)
For an object falling through the atmosphere the rate of change of velocity is \(\dfrac{dv}{dt}=c-kv\), with \(c=10\) (found earlier) and \(k\) a positive constant. The initial velocity is zero.
(ii) Find \(v\) in terms of \(t\) and \(k\). [5]
The terminal velocity is the value approached after a long time.
(iii) An object has initial velocity zero and terminal velocity \(40\ \text{ms}^{-1}\). Find how long it takes to reach 90% of its terminal velocity. [4]
Answer — verbatim HCI
(ii) Same five-step engine, but \(k\) stays a letter
\(\dfrac{dv}{dt}=10-kv\Rightarrow\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{10-kv}\,dv=\int dt\) (1)
\(-\dfrac1k\ln|10-kv|=t+A\) (1)
\(\ln|10-kv|=-kt-kA\Rightarrow 10-kv=\pm e^{-kt-kA}=Be^{-kt}\), where \(B=e^{-kA}\) (1)
When \(t=0\), \(v=0\): \(\ B=10\) (1)
\(10-kv=10e^{-kt}\Rightarrow v=\dfrac{10}{k}\big(1-e^{-kt}\big)\) (1)
(iii) Use terminal velocity to pin \(k\), then solve for the time
As \(t\to\infty\), \(e^{-kt}\to0\), so \(v\to\dfrac{10}{k}\).
\(\dfrac{10}{k}=40\Rightarrow k=\dfrac14\) (1), hence \(v=40\big(1-e^{-t/4}\big)\).
90% of terminal: \(v=0.9(40)=36\) (1)
\(36=40\big(1-e^{-t/4}\big)\Rightarrow e^{-t/4}=\dfrac{1}{10}\) (1)
\(t=4\ln 10=9.21\ \text{s}\) (1)
Why harder: the constant \(k\) stays a letter the whole way — harder to track than the parachutist's \(\alpha=2\) — and you must use the terminal-velocity idea (the limit as \(t\to\infty\)) to pin \(k\) before you can solve for a time. Same five-step engine, run symbolically.

Card 4 — Linking to displacement: distance \(=\displaystyle\int v\,dt\)

You didn't know how to get from velocity to distance. Velocity is the rate of change of displacement, \(v=\dfrac{dx}{dt}\), so displacement is the integral of velocity: \(x=\displaystyle\int v\,dt\). Integrate \(v(t)\), use the start position (here \(x=0\) at \(t=0\)) to find the constant, then to find the time to fall a set distance, set \(x\) equal to that distance and solve for \(t\) — by GC when it mixes \(t\) and \(e^{-bt}\).
Worked example — shown in full · the integration step of N2024/P1/Q11(c)
Given \(v=4.9\big(1-e^{-2t}\big)\), and the parachutist starts from \(x=0\) at \(t=0\), find \(x\) in terms of \(t\).
Integrate \(v\) to get \(x\)
\(x=\displaystyle\int v\,dt=\int 4.9\big(1-e^{-2t}\big)\,dt\) (1)
\(=4.9\!\left(t-\dfrac{1}{-2}e^{-2t}\right)+d=4.9\!\left(t+\dfrac12 e^{-2t}\right)+d\) (1)
Apply the starting position
When \(t=0\), \(x=0\).
\(0=4.9\!\left(0+\dfrac12\right)+d\Rightarrow d=-2.45\) (1)
\(x=4.9\!\left(t+\dfrac12 e^{-2t}\right)-2.45\)

The step people drop: integrating \(-e^{-2t}\) gives \(+\dfrac12 e^{-2t}\) — divide by the \(-2\). Get the sign wrong and every later value is off.

Practice — harder (HCI Tutorial 9 Q13 · N2024/P1/Q11(c) — the real [5]-mark part)
The helicopter is at a height of 2000 m above the ground. The parachutist falls with velocity \(v=4.9\big(1-e^{-2t}\big)\) from rest. Find the time taken for the parachutist to reach the ground. [5]
Answer — verbatim HCI
Form \(x(t)\) from \(v\)
\(v=\dfrac{dx}{dt}=4.9\big(1-e^{-2t}\big)\Rightarrow x=\displaystyle\int 4.9\big(1-e^{-2t}\big)\,dt=4.9\!\left(t+\dfrac12 e^{-2t}\right)+d\) (1)(1)
Apply \(t=0,\ x=0\)
\(0=4.9\!\left(0+\dfrac12\right)+d\Rightarrow d=-2.45\) (1), so \(x=4.9\!\left(t+\dfrac12 e^{-2t}\right)-2.45\).
Reaching the ground ⇒ \(x=2000\); solve by GC
\(2000=4.9\!\left(t+\dfrac12 e^{-2t}\right)-2.45\) (1)
By GC, \(t=408.66327\ldots\Rightarrow t\approx 409\ \text{s}\) (3 s.f.) (1)
Why harder: the example handed you \(v\) and asked only for \(x(t)\). Here you must (i) form \(x(t)\) yourself, (ii) realise that "reach the ground" from 2000 m means \(x=2000\), and (iii) solve a transcendental equation on the GC.
