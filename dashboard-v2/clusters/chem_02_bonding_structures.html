Toolkit — structure decides everything
The four structures

Giant ionic (electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a lattice) · giant metallic (lattice of cations in a sea of delocalised electrons) · giant covalent (network of covalent bonds, e.g. diamond, graphite, \(\ce{SiO2}\)) · simple molecular (small molecules held by weak intermolecular forces).

Ionic strength / lattice energy

Stronger (higher mp, harder) with higher ionic charge and smaller ionic radius (\(\text{LE} \propto \frac{q_+ q_-}{r_+ + r_-}\)). \(\ce{MgO}\) ≫ \(\ce{NaCl}\) because 2+/2− vs 1+/1− and smaller ions.

Ionic ↔ covalent character (polarisation)

A cation distorts (polarises) an anion's electron cloud → some covalent character. More polarising = small, highly charged cation; more polarisable = large anion. So \(\ce{AlCl3}\) (small 3+ \(\ce{Al}\)) is far more covalent than \(\ce{NaCl}\).

Metallic strength

Stronger with more delocalised electrons per atom and smaller cation (higher charge density): \(\ce{Na} < \ce{Mg} < \ce{Al}\).

Electrical conductivity — needs mobile charge carriers

Metals: delocalised e⁻ (solid + molten). Ionic: mobile ions only when molten or aqueous, never solid. Graphite: one delocalised e⁻ per C → conducts. Diamond / \(\ce{SiO2}\) / simple molecular: no mobile carriers → insulate.

The property chain

Structure → bonding → strength of attraction → property. Never quote a property without naming the structure and the particles being pulled apart. mp/bp breaks the structure's forces (covalent bonds for giant covalent; IMF for simple molecular).

Questions — attempt cold
Question 1[5]
(a) Explain, in terms of the ions present, why \(\ce{MgO}\) (mp 2852 °C) has a much higher melting point than \(\ce{NaCl}\) (mp 801 °C). [3]
(b) \(\ce{NaCl}\) is essentially ionic, but \(\ce{AlCl3}\) has substantial covalent character. Explain why. [2]
Reveal worked solution

(a) Both are giant ionic lattices; melting breaks the electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions. In \(\ce{MgO}\) the ions are \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{O^2-}\) (charge 2+/2−) vs \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\) (1+/1−), and \(\ce{Mg^2+}\)/\(\ce{O^2-}\) are smaller than \(\ce{Na+}\)/\(\ce{Cl-}\). Higher charges and smaller radii → much larger lattice energy → far more energy to separate → higher mp. A1 both giant ionic / electrostatic, A1 higher charge, A1 smaller ionic radius.

(b) \(\ce{Al^3+}\) is small and highly charged, so it is strongly polarising: it distorts (pulls) the electron cloud of the \(\ce{Cl-}\) anion, drawing charge into the region between the ions. This shared electron density is covalent character. \(\ce{Na+}\) (1+, larger) polarises far less. A1 \(\ce{Al^3+}\) small/high charge → polarising, A1 distorts anion's electron cloud → covalent character.

Archetype & trap

Archetype: ionic lattice strength from charge + radius, and polarisation → covalent character (Fajans).

Trigger: "why higher mp / harder" for two ionic solids → compare charge then radius. "Why covalent character in an ionic compound" → small high-charge cation + polarisable anion.

Trap: saying "stronger ionic bonds" without naming charge and radius; forgetting mp breaks electrostatic attractions between ions, the mark-scheme phrase.

Question 2[5]
(a) Describe metallic bonding. [2]
(b) Explain why magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium. [2]
(c) Explain why metals conduct electricity and are malleable. [1]
Reveal worked solution

(a) A lattice of metal cations in a sea of delocalised (valence) electrons; the bonding is the electrostatic attraction between the cations and the delocalised electrons. A1 cations + delocalised e⁻, A1 electrostatic attraction between them.

(b) \(\ce{Mg}\) provides 2 delocalised electrons per atom (vs 1 for \(\ce{Na}\)) and \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) is smaller with higher charge density. Stronger attraction between cations and the electron sea → higher mp. A1 more delocalised e⁻ / higher charge, A1 smaller cation → stronger attraction.

(c) The delocalised electrons are mobile, so they carry charge (conductivity); layers of cations can slide over each other without breaking the bonding, so the metal is malleable. A1 (either mobile e⁻ conduct, or layers slide).

Archetype & trap

Archetype: metallic bonding definition + strength (electrons/atom, cation size) + property mechanism.

Trigger: "why higher mp" for two metals → count delocalised e⁻ per atom + compare cation size. "Conducts / malleable" → mobile electrons / sliding layers.

Trap: writing "sea of electrons" without "delocalised" or without the electrostatic attraction; explaining malleability as "the metallic bonds bend" instead of layers sliding while the bonding stays intact.

Question 3[6]
Diamond and graphite are both giant covalent forms of carbon.
(a) Using their structures, explain why diamond is extremely hard while graphite is soft and used as a lubricant. [3]
(b) Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not. [2]
(c) Both have very high melting points. Explain why. [1]
Reveal worked solution

(a) Diamond: each C is covalently bonded to 4 others in a rigid 3-D tetrahedral network → no weak direction → very hard. Graphite: C bonded to 3 others in flat hexagonal layers, with only weak id–id forces between layers, so layers slide over each other → soft / lubricating. A1 diamond 4 bonds/3-D network, A1 graphite layers, A1 weak forces between layers slide.

(b) In graphite each C uses only 3 of its 4 valence electrons for σ bonds; the 4th is delocalised between the layers and mobile → conducts. In diamond all 4 electrons are localised in σ bonds → no mobile charge carriers → insulator. A1 graphite delocalised mobile e⁻, A1 diamond all e⁻ localised.

(c) Melting requires breaking many strong covalent bonds throughout the giant structure → very high mp. A1.

Archetype & trap

Archetype: giant-covalent structure → hardness, conductivity, mp, contrasting two allotropes.

Trigger: diamond/graphite/\(\ce{SiO2}\) named → map bonds-per-atom and any delocalised/interlayer feature to the property asked.

Trap: saying graphite is soft "because the covalent bonds are weak" — the covalent bonds are strong; it's the weak forces between layers. And melting breaks covalent bonds here, not IMF.

Question 4[5]
Explain the electrical conductivity of each of the following, referring to the charge carriers present:
  1. solid sodium chloride vs molten sodium chloride;
  2. solid magnesium;
  3. solid silicon dioxide.
Reveal worked solution

\(\ce{NaCl}\) (molten)Yeslattice broken → ions free to move and carry charge

The one rule the examiner rewards: conduction needs mobile charge carriers — mobile ions (molten/aqueous ionic) or mobile delocalised electrons (metals, graphite). Fixed ions and localised bonding electrons cannot conduct. A1 per correct explained row (solid vs molten \(\ce{NaCl}\) together = the key contrast).

Archetype & trap

Archetype: conductivity by charge-carrier availability across structure types and states.

Trigger: "does it conduct / explain conductivity" → ask: are there mobile ions (molten/aqueous ionic) or mobile electrons (metal/graphite)? If neither → no.

Trap: saying solid ionic conducts (it does not — ions are fixed), or that \(\ce{SiO2}\)/diamond conduct because they're giant (giant ≠ conducting; it's about mobile carriers).

Question 5[6]
The melting points across Period 3 are: \(\ce{Na}\) 98, \(\ce{Mg}\) 650, \(\ce{Al}\) 660, \(\ce{Si}\) 1410, \(\ce{P4}\) 44, \(\ce{S8}\) 119, \(\ce{Cl2}\) −101 (°C).
(a) Account for the increase from \(\ce{Na}\) to \(\ce{Al}\). [2]
(b) Account for the sharp maximum at \(\ce{Si}\). [2]
(c) Account for the low values of \(\ce{P4}\), \(\ce{S8}\), \(\ce{Cl2}\), and why \(\ce{S8} > \ce{P4} > \ce{Cl2}\). [2]
Reveal worked solution

(a) \(\ce{Na}\), \(\ce{Mg}\), \(\ce{Al}\) are giant metallic. Across the period the number of delocalised electrons per atom rises (1→2→3) and the cation gets smaller/more charged → stronger metallic bonding → rising mp. A1 giant metallic, A1 more delocalised e⁻ / smaller cation.

(b) \(\ce{Si}\) is giant covalent: melting breaks many strong covalent bonds throughout the 3-D network → the highest mp in the period. A1 giant covalent, A1 many strong covalent bonds broken.

(c) \(\ce{P4}\), \(\ce{S8}\), \(\ce{Cl2}\) are simple molecular: melting overcomes only weak id–id forces between molecules (covalent bonds stay intact) → low mp. The size of id–id rises with number of electrons per molecule, so \(\ce{S8}\) (largest) > \(\ce{P4}\) > \(\ce{Cl2}\) (smallest). A1 simple molecular / weak id–id, A1 \(\ce{S8}>\ce{P4}>\ce{Cl2}\) by electron count.

Archetype & trap

Archetype: the Period-3 mp trend — read structure type off the position, then explain each block.

Trigger: any mp/bp trend across a period → split into metallic (rise) / giant covalent (peak) / simple molecular (low, id–id by size).

Trap: explaining the simple-molecular drop as "weaker covalent bonds" — melting breaks id–id between molecules, not the covalent bonds. And \(\ce{S8}>\ce{P4}\) is by electrons/molecule, not \(A_r\).

Gateway: this is a Periodicity question dressed as bonding — the same reasoning returns in Cluster on Periodicity.

Question 7[4]
(a) Explain why sodium chloride dissolves in water but not in hexane. [3]
(b) Explain why silicon dioxide is insoluble in water. [1]
Reveal worked solution

(a) Water is polar: its \(\delta+\) H and \(\delta-\) O attract the \(\ce{Cl-}\) and \(\ce{Na+}\) ions respectively (ion–dipole attraction), and the energy released hydrating the ions is enough to offset the lattice energy, so the lattice breaks and it dissolves. Hexane is non-polar — it can only form weak id–id forces with the ions, not enough to overcome the strong ionic lattice → insoluble. A1 water polar / ion–dipole, A1 hydration energy offsets lattice energy, A1 hexane non-polar can't.

(b) \(\ce{SiO2}\) is a giant covalent network; dissolving would require breaking many strong covalent bonds, and no solvent interaction releases enough energy to do that → insoluble. A1.

Archetype & trap

Archetype: solubility as an energy balance — solvent–solute attraction vs the lattice/bonds being broken ("like dissolves like").

Trigger: "why (in)soluble in water / non-polar solvent" → compare the new solvent–solute forces released against the structure's forces that must break.

Trap: "ionic always dissolves in water" as a bare fact — the mark is the hydration energy vs lattice energy balance and the ion–dipole interaction.

Question 8[5]
Sodium (\(\ce{Na}\)), sodium oxide (\(\ce{Na2O}\)) and phosphorus(V) oxide (\(\ce{P4O10}\)) are all Period-3 element oxides or elements.
(a) State the structure and bonding in each. [3]
(b) Predict and explain which has the lowest melting point. [2]
Reveal worked solution
SubstanceStructure & bonding
\(\ce{Na}\)giant metallic (cations + sea of delocalised electrons)
\(\ce{Na2O}\)giant ionic (\(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{O^2-}\) in a lattice)
\(\ce{P4O10}\)simple molecular (discrete \(\ce{P4O10}\) molecules, covalent bonds within, id–id between)

(b) \(\ce{P4O10}\) has the lowest mp: it is simple molecular, so melting overcomes only weak id–id forces between molecules, whereas \(\ce{Na}\) and \(\ce{Na2O}\) are giant structures needing strong metallic/ionic attractions broken throughout. A1 \(\ce{P4O10}\), A1 simple molecular / only weak id–id vs giant.

Archetype & trap

Archetype: assign structure/bonding to Period-3 species and rank a property from it.

Trigger: a mix of metal / ionic oxide / molecular oxide → simple molecular always has the lowest mp (weak IMF); giant structures are high.

Trap: calling \(\ce{P4O10}\) "giant covalent" because it has covalent bonds — it's simple molecular (discrete molecules), which is why its mp is low. Structure type, not bond type, sets the property.
