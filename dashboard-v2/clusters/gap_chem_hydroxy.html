How many moles of \(\ce{H2}\) gas are produced per mole of polymer? Show the stoichiometry you used. [1]

(a) Counting the hydrogen

Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a)
D. \(\ce{NaHCO3}\) only reacts with acids at least as strong as carbonic acid (i.e. carboxylic acids). Phenol is too weak an acid to liberate \(\ce{CO2}\) from \(\ce{NaHCO3}\), and cyclohexanol is not acidic, so \(\ce{NaHCO3}\) reacts with neither. The ladder both parts are really testing
A proton transfer goes forward only if your acid is a stronger acid than the conjugate acid you are about to make. Read down the ladder: anything ABOVE a base's conjugate acid will react with that base.

Question 3 (a) HCI Prelim/N2023/P3/Q1(c) · (b) HCI lecture Ex 4.1 + Class Exercise 1, Q1 — species printed in scrambled order

Question
(a) Arrange the following in order of increasing Brønsted-Lowry acidity, and suggest an explanation for the order. [3]
  \(\ce{C6H5OH}\)  ·  \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\)  ·  \(\ce{CH3CO2H}\)  ·  \(\ce{H2O}\)

(b) Arrange the following in order of increasing acid strength, explaining your order by reference to the conjugate bases. [3]
  1-chloropropan-1-ol  ·  propan-1-ol  ·  2-chloropropan-1-ol
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a) Increasing acidity — the printed order was NOT the answer order
Correct order: \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\) < \(\ce{H2O}\) < \(\ce{C6H5OH}\) < \(\ce{CH3CO2H}\) (1). Acid strength depends on the stability of the conjugate base (anion) after losing \(\ce{H+}\) — the more stable/delocalised the negative charge, the stronger the acid. \(\ce{CH3CH2O^-}\) (weakest acid): the electron-donating ethyl group (+I effect) intensifies the negative charge on O, destabilising the anion (1). \(\ce{OH^-}\): no electron-donating alkyl group, so the charge is less intensified than in ethoxide → water is more acidic than ethanol. \(\ce{C6H5O^-}\) (phenoxide): the lone pair/negative charge is delocalised into the benzene ring, spreading the charge and stabilising the anion → phenol more acidic than water. \(\ce{CH3CO2^-}\) (ethanoate, strongest): the negative charge is delocalised equally over two electronegative O atoms, giving the greatest stabilisation → carboxylic acid is the most acidic (1). [INFERRED — no official key]
(b) Increasing acid strength — again, not the printed order
Correct order: propan-1-ol < 2-chloropropan-1-ol < 1-chloropropan-1-ol (1). Propan-1-oxide has no electron-withdrawing group; the alkyl group is electron-donating and intensifies the negative charge on the O → least stable conjugate base → weakest acid (1). The Cl is electron-withdrawing and disperses/stabilises the negative charge through the σ (sigma) bonding framework; because the inductive effect weakens with distance, Cl on the α-carbon (1-chloro) withdraws more strongly than Cl on the β-carbon (2-chloro) → the 1-chloro conjugate base is most stabilised → strongest acid (1).
Targets the gap you called "a huge knowledge gap" — you had the alkyl group backwards. An electron-donating alkyl group INTENSIFIES the negative charge on the alkoxide → DEstabilises it → less dissociation → WEAKER acid. It never stabilises an anion. An EWG (Cl, NO₂, C=O) disperses/withdraws the negative charge → stabilises → stronger acid, and that effect weakens with distance, so 2-chloro < 1-chloro. Two wording rules the marker looks for: compare phenoxide with alkoxide, not "phenol with alkoxide"; and say the charge is "partially" delocalised, never "fully". The full-mark phenoxide line is: "the lone pair on O is parallel to the p-orbitals of the ring, so the negative charge is delocalised into the ring → more stable conjugate base → stronger acid → lower pKa." And note the scramble: both parts here were printed out of order on purpose. Never assume the list you are given is the answer.

The role of conc H₂SO₄ in the alcohol route — the bit you did not know
It protonates the carbonyl oxygen, making the carbonyl carbon more electron-deficient / more susceptible to nucleophilic attack by the alcohol. That is why the alcohol route needs a catalyst and heat, while the acyl chloride route needs neither — the acyl carbon is already highly electron-deficient (two electronegative atoms, O and Cl) and \(\ce{Cl^-}\) is a better leaving group than \(\ce{OH^-}\).
Targets the gap ("what the f*** is 9?") — you could not see an ester at all. Writing "phenol + ethanoic acid, conc H₂SO₄" scores zero — phenol's lone pair is tied up in the ring, so it is too weak a nucleophile for the carboxylic acid.

Question 6 (a) HCI Soln/N2022/P3/Q1(b)(ii) · (b) HCI Prelim/N2021/P2/Q2(b)(i)

Question
(a) Explain why phenol undergoes chlorination more readily than methylbenzene — that is, why an \(\ce{-OH}\) group activates the ring more than a \(\ce{-CH3}\) group, and more than benzene itself. [2]

(b) Explain the role of concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) in the formation of the electrophile in the nitration of benzene. Include the equation that generates it. [2]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a)
In phenol, the lone pair of electrons on the O atom delocalises into the benzene ring (1), thereby greatly increasing the electron density of the ring. In methylbenzene, the methyl group is only weakly electron-donating (inductive effect), increasing electron density to a lesser extent (1). Hence the ring in phenol is more activated / more electron rich, more susceptible to electrophilic attack, and undergoes chlorination more readily. (Just stating "OH is electron-donating" is insufficient — the delocalisation must be explained.)
(b)
\(\ce{H2SO4}\) is a stronger Brønsted acid / proton donor than \(\ce{HNO3}\) (1), hence the protonation of \(\ce{HNO3}\) by \(\ce{H2SO4}\) eventually produces the strong electrophile \(\ce{NO2+}\) (1). \(\ce{2H2SO4 + HNO3 -> NO2+ + H3O+ + 2HSO4^-}\). \(\ce{HSO4^-}\) later deprotonates the intermediate and catalyst \(\ce{H2SO4}\) is regenerated.
Targets two gaps you were confidently wrong on in Q12. First: you called \(\ce{-OH}\) electron-WITHDRAWING. It is electron-DONATING — the lone pair on O overlaps with the π-electron cloud of the ring, increasing the ring's electron density, which is why phenol reacts with a weak electrophile and no catalyst (bromine water, dilute \(\ce{HNO3}\)) while methylbenzene needs a halogen carrier or conc \(\ce{HNO3}\)/conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\). Second: you thought \(\ce{HNO3}\) itself attacks. It does not — the electrophile is \(\ce{NO2+}\), generated when conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) protonates \(\ce{HNO3}\) (by-product \(\ce{H2O}\)/\(\ce{H3O+}\)). While you are here, lock the four roles of \(\ce{H2SO4}\): (i) dilute = acidic medium for oxidation; (ii) excess conc = dehydrating agent for elimination; (iii) a few drops conc = protonates the carbonyl O in esterification; (iv) conc = protonates \(\ce{HNO3}\) to make \(\ce{NO2+}\).
