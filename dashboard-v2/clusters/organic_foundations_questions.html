Toolkit — memorise cold
Degrees of unsaturation = (2C + 2 + N − X − H) / 2
What a degree iseach ring or C=C = 1; each C≡C = 2. e.g. C₄H₈ → (8+2−8)/2 = 1 → one C=C or one ring.
Naming — orderlongest chain with the main group = stem → number to give the group the lowest locant → substituents as prefixes in alphabetical order → suffix = principal group.
Nucleophileelectron-pair donor (lone pair / π); attacks an electron-poor δ+ carbon. OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃.
Electrophileelectron-pair acceptor; attacks an electron-rich site (C=C, ring). Br⁺, NO₂⁺, H⁺.
Fissionhomolytic → two radicals (UV, non-polar bonds); heterolytic → two ions (polar bonds / π sites).

C · Evaluative drill

Question 6
A hydrocarbon has the molecular formula C₄H₈.
(a) Draw the displayed formula of every possible structure, and state the type of isomerism relating them. Identify which show cis-trans isomerism and justify with the exact bonding requirement. [6]
(b) For the isomers that react rapidly with bromine, name the reaction type and mechanism, and explain from the bonding why they react while the ring isomer behaves like an alkane. [4]
(c) Predict, with reasoning from intermolecular forces, which isomer has the highest boiling point. [2]
(d) A single molecular formula gives compounds differing in both reactivity and physical properties. State what one structural feature is responsible in each case. [2]
Answer
(a) There is one degree of unsaturation, so every isomer contains either one C=C double bond or one ring. But-1-ene and but-2-ene are positional isomers, because they differ only in where the double bond sits. But-1-ene and 2-methylpropene are chain isomers, because their carbon skeletons are branched differently. The alkenes and the cycloalkanes are functional-group isomers of each other, because one family contains a C=C while the other contains a ring. Only but-2-ene shows cis-trans isomerism, because each doubly-bonded carbon carries an H atom and a CH₃ group, which are two different groups, and the π bond prevents rotation about the double bond. 2-methylpropene cannot show cis-trans isomerism, because one of its doubly-bonded carbons carries two identical CH₃ groups.
(b) The alkenes react rapidly with bromine by electrophilic addition. The C=C π bond is a region of high electron density, so it attracts the electrophile, and the bromine molecule becomes polarised as it approaches. Cyclobutane cannot react in this way, because it contains only σ bonds and is non-polar, so it has no region of high electron density for an electrophile to attack. It therefore behaves like an alkane and undergoes only free-radical substitution under ultraviolet light.
(c) All five isomers are non-polar C₄ molecules, so the only forces between them are instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (dispersion) forces, and their boiling points are quite close. The straight-chain isomers, but-1-ene and but-2-ene, have the largest surface area of contact, so they have the strongest dispersion forces and the highest boiling point. 2-methylpropene is the most branched and therefore the most compact, so it has the smallest surface contact and the lowest boiling point.
(d) The reactivity of each isomer is determined by the presence or absence of the π bond, because an isomer with a C=C undergoes electrophilic addition while a ring isomer behaves like an unreactive alkane. The physical properties are determined instead by the shape and surface area of the molecule, which set the strength of the dispersion forces. A single molecular formula can therefore give several different compounds, because it is the arrangement of the atoms, and not the formula itself, that decides how a molecule behaves.
Question 7
Consider the following three reactions.
(i) ethene + bromine → 1,2-dibromoethane
(ii) bromoethane + hydroxide ion → ethanol + bromide ion
(iii) ethane + chlorine (UV) → chloroethane + hydrogen chloride
(a) For each, classify the reaction type and mechanism, and state whether the attacking species is a nucleophile, electrophile or radical — justifying from the electronic nature of the organic molecule. [6]
(b) Explain why (i) and (ii) proceed by heterolytic pathways but (iii) must proceed homolytically. [3]
(c) State the single general principle linking a molecule's structure to the mechanism it is forced to follow. [1]
Answer
(a) Reaction (i) is an addition reaction that proceeds by an electrophilic mechanism. The C=C π bond is electron-rich, so the bromine molecule acts as the electrophile: it is polarised as it approaches and is then attacked by the π electrons. Reaction (ii) is a substitution reaction that proceeds by a nucleophilic mechanism. The C–Br bond is polar, so that carbon is δ+, and the hydroxide ion acts as the nucleophile by donating its lone pair to the δ+ carbon while the bromide ion leaves. Reaction (iii) is a substitution reaction that proceeds by a free-radical mechanism. Ethane is non-polar and has no δ+ site and no π density, so it cannot be attacked by an ion, and the attacking species is a chlorine radical.
(b) Reactions (i) and (ii) each have a site that an ion can attack, which is an electron-rich π bond in (i) and a polar bond with a δ+ carbon in (ii), so their bonds break heterolytically to give ions that react. Reaction (iii) has neither an electron-rich site nor a polar bond, so no ion can attack it, and the only way to start the reaction is the homolytic breaking of the chlorine molecule by ultraviolet light to form radicals.
(c) The mechanism a molecule follows is determined by its structure: a π system is attacked by electrophiles, a polar σ bond with a δ+ carbon is attacked by nucleophiles, and a non-polar molecule with neither can only be attacked by radicals.
Question 8
A compound X has molecular formula C₃H₆O₃ and is optically active.
(a) Determine the degree of unsaturation and use it to narrow the functional groups X could contain. [2]
(b) X reacts with sodium carbonate to release a gas. Deduce that functional group and the class of X, and account for the remaining oxygen. [2]
(c) Deduce the full structure of X, name it, and explain exactly what makes it chiral. [3]
(d) Describe X's enantiomer and explain why the two are genuinely different compounds despite identical connectivity — give one property that would distinguish them and one that would not. [3]
Answer
(a) The number of degrees of unsaturation is (2×3 + 2 − 6)/2, which equals 1, so X contains either one C=O double bond or one ring. Because X also contains three oxygen atoms, the most likely arrangement is a C=O group together with two further oxygen environments such as O–H or C–O.
(b) The release of a gas with sodium carbonate shows that carbon dioxide is produced, which is the characteristic test for the carboxylic acid group, –COOH, so X is a carboxylic acid. The –COOH group uses one carbon and two of the three oxygen atoms, so the third oxygen must belong to a separate –OH group.
(c) X is therefore 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), CH₃CH(OH)COOH, which has the formula C₃H₆O₃. Its central carbon is chiral, because it is bonded to four different groups: a CH₃ group, an OH group, an H atom and a COOH group.
(d) The enantiomer of X is its non-superimposable mirror image, with the opposite arrangement of groups at the chiral carbon. The two are genuinely different compounds because a chiral centre has no plane of symmetry, so no amount of rotation can make one coincide with the other. The property that would distinguish them is their effect on plane-polarised light, which they rotate by equal amounts but in opposite directions, and they also react at different rates with another chiral molecule. The property that would not distinguish them is any ordinary physical property such as melting point, boiling point or density, and any reaction with an achiral reagent, because these are identical for the two enantiomers.
