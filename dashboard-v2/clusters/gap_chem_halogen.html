Question 1 (HCI Soln/N2022/P3/Q4(f)(i))

Question
1-bromopropane, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2Br}\), reacts with \(\ce{NaCN}\).
State the mechanism, and draw it fully — showing the curly arrows, the transition state (with its overall charge) and the products. [4]
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
Nucleophilic substitution, SN2. 1-bromopropane is a primary halogenoalkane, so it undergoes SN2.
What the mark scheme wants in the drawing
\(\ce{NC^-}\) attacks the δ+ carbon bearing Br from the side opposite to Br (curly arrow from the lone pair on the C of \(\ce{CN^-}\) to that carbon; second curly arrow from the C–Br bond to Br) (1) (1) → a pentacoordinate transition state \([\ce{NC}\cdots\ce{C}\cdots\ce{Br}]^-\) with the three other groups in a plane, drawn in square brackets with the ‡ and the overall negative charge (1) → product \(\ce{NC-CH2CH2CH3}\) (butanenitrile) + \(\ce{:Br^-}\) (1). (SN2 must be stated; the transition state must be included and has a negative charge; include Br⁻ in the product.)
Targets the gap — the kinetics you kept dropping: SN2 is one concerted step, rate = \(k[\ce{RX}][\ce{Nu}]\), second order overall. A primary substrate goes SN2 (not SN1) because a 1° carbocation is too unstable and there is little steric hindrance to backside attack; backside attack gives inversion. Contrast SN1 on a 3° substrate: rate = \(k[\ce{RX}]\), first order, rds = the carbocation-forming step, so [Nu] is absent.

Archetype · gap targeted
Gaps 1–2 — draw an SN2 on a primary substrate, plus why 1° → SN2 and the SN1/SN2 rate laws.

Anionic nucleophile on a 1° halide: backside attack, bracketed TS with ‡ and charge, leaving group written; kinetics = k[RX][Nu] second order.

Question 2 (HCI Soln/N2019/P3/Q5(c)(ii))

Question
In a synthesis, 1,2-dibromoethane, \(\ce{BrCH2CH2Br}\), is reacted with a limited amount of ethanolic \(\ce{NH3}\), heated, to make a bromoamine.
State a possible side product formed in this step, and state how to minimise it. [2]
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
In step 1, \(\ce{H2NCH2CH2NH2}\) may be formed (1). Use a limited amount of \(\ce{NH3}\) (1). (Since there are two electron-deficient C atoms in 1,2-dibromoethane for NH₃ to attack, a possible side product is the diamine; avoid suggesting a side product whose formation is hard to minimise.)
Targets the gap — the R–X + NH₃ pattern in full: the \(\ce{NH3}\) lone pair does nucleophilic substitution on the δ+ C–Br to give \(\ce{R-NH3^+}\), then a second \(\ce{NH3}\) deprotonates it to the primary amine \(\ce{R-NH2}\). Because that amine is itself a nucleophile, it attacks more R–X → secondary → tertiary → finally a quaternary ammonium salt \([\ce{R4N}]^+\) (over-alkylation). Use excess \(\ce{NH3}\) to favour the primary amine; here, where the target is only the mono-bromoamine, limited \(\ce{NH3}\) keeps the diamine down.

Archetype · gap targeted
Gap 3 — R–X + NH₃ → amine: substitution, deprotonation, and controlling over-substitution.

Ammonia substitutes then is deprotonated to the 1° amine; every amine formed is a fresh nucleophile, so excess NH₃ is needed or you climb to the quaternary salt.

Question 3 (HCI Prelim/N2024/P1/Q23)

Question
Which reaction will give the product indicated? [1]
A  \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\) —(ethanolic KCN, heat)→ \(\ce{CH3CH2CN}\)
B  phenol —(conc. \(\ce{HNO3}\), r.t.)→ 4-nitrophenol
C  \(\ce{C6H5CHBrC(CH3)3}\) —(ethanolic KOH, heat)→ \(\ce{C6H5CH(OH)C(CH3)3}\)
D  \(\ce{CH3CH2Cl}\) —(excess \(\ce{CH3NH2}\), heat)→ \([\ce{(CH3)3N-CH2CH3}]^+\ \ce{Cl^-}\)
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
C. In \(\ce{C6H5CHBrC(CH3)3}\) the carbon bearing Br has no β-hydrogen (its neighbours are the phenyl ring and the quaternary C of tert-butyl), so elimination is impossible; the benzylic C–Br instead undergoes nucleophilic substitution (SN1 via a phenyl-stabilised carbocation) to give the alcohol. A fails (the −OH of an alcohol is not displaced by \(\ce{CN^-}\)). B fails (conc. \(\ce{HNO3}\) over-nitrates phenol to 2,4,6-trinitrophenol). D fails (methylamine + chloroethane cannot place three methyl groups on N). [INFERRED — no official key]
Targets the gap — the rule this MCQ hides: for a normal substrate the solvent decides. Aqueous \(\ce{NaOH}\), heat → \(\ce{OH^-}\) acts as a nucleophile → substitution → the alcohol. Hot ethanolic \(\ce{NaOH}\)/\(\ce{KOH}\) → \(\ce{OH^-}\) is less solvated in ethanol, so it is a stronger base → it abstracts a β-hydrogen → elimination → the alkene. Option C is the exception: with no β-H, even ethanolic KOH can only substitute.

Archetype · gap targeted
Gap 4 — substitution vs elimination: aqueous OH⁻ → alcohol, ethanolic OH⁻ → alkene, and the no-β-H case where only substitution is possible.

Solvent flips OH⁻ between nucleophile and base; elimination needs a β-hydrogen, so a substrate without one substitutes regardless.

Question 4 (HCI Soln/N2022/P3/Q4(f)(iii))

Question
Three primary halogenoalkanes M, N and O each react with \(\ce{NaOH}\). N contains a C–I bond; M and O both contain C–Br bonds; O has a highly branched, bulky alkyl group.
Arrange M, N and O in order of increasing rate of hydrolysis and explain the order. [3]
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
The relative rate increases from O to M to N (1). All three are primary halogenoalkanes undergoing nucleophilic substitution via SN2. The C–I bond in N is weaker than the C–Br bond in M and O, so N has a lower activation energy and the fastest rate (1). The highly branched alkyl group in O is bulkier than in M, causing greater steric hindrance for nucleophilic attack and the slowest rate. Hence O < M < N (1). (In SN2, rate depends on the strength of the C–X bond and the steric effect of the alkyl group; O's bulky chain may even cause it to prefer SN1, like a tertiary halogenoalkane.)

Targets the gap you had backwards: rate of hydrolysis follows C–X bond strength, because the C–X bond breaks in the rate-determining step. Weaker/longer bond → faster. C–I is the weakest → fastest; C–F the strongest → essentially inert. It is NOT set by electronegativity or how δ+ the carbon is — if it were, the very polar C–F would be fastest, but it is the slowest. Quote the bond energies, not polarity.

Archetype · gap targeted
Gap 5 — rate of hydrolysis is set by C–X bond energy (C–I weakest → fastest), not electronegativity; steric bulk slows SN2.

Rank by bond strength first (I < Br < Cl < F breaks hardest); then let steric hindrance separate two substrates sharing the same halogen.

Question 5 (HCI Soln/N2020/P1/Q21)

Question
Four compounds are warmed with ethanolic silver nitrate. One of them — chlorobenzene, \(\ce{C6H5Cl}\) — will NOT react.
Explain why chlorobenzene (an aryl halide) is unreactive towards nucleophilic substitution. [2]
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
Halogenoarenes are not reactive towards nucleophilic substitution due to steric hindrance and delocalisation of the lone pair on X into the benzene ring, giving partial double-bond character to the C–X bond (1) (1); hence chlorobenzene does not react. (An acyl chloride reacts immediately to give a white ppt of AgCl; halogenoalkanes react but less readily, usually on warming.)
Targets the gap: the same delocalisation makes any halogen on an sp² carbon (aryl or vinyl) unreactive. It also blocks SN1 — the would-be carbocation's empty p-orbital is orthogonal to the ring π system, so it gains no stabilisation. Contrast a benzylic C–X (on the sp³ carbon next to the ring), which reacts readily.
Solution hidden — attempt first
Archetype · gap targeted
Gap 6 — why an sp²/aryl C–X resists nucleophilic substitution: lone-pair delocalisation → partial double-bond character.

Delocalisation strengthens and shortens the C–X bond and the ring repels/blocks the nucleophile; SN1 is shut off too.

Question 7 (HCI Soln/N2019/P3/Q5(c)(i))

Question
1,2-dibromoethane, \(\ce{BrCH2CH2Br}\), is converted through L → M → K, where L is a bromo-amine, M is a dinitrile, and K is a dicarboxylic acid.
Draw the structures of L, M and K, and state the reagents and conditions for each step. [6]
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
step 1: limited ethanolic \(\ce{NH3}\), heat → L = \(\ce{BrCH2CH2-NH-CH2CH2Br}\) (a secondary bromo-amine). step 2: ethanolic KCN, heat → M = \(\ce{NC-CH2CH2-NH-CH2CH2-CN}\) (the dinitrile). step 3: dilute \(\ce{H2SO4}\), heat → K = \(\ce{HO2C-CH2CH2-NH-CH2CH2-CO2H}\) (the dicarboxylic acid). (React 1,2-dibromoethane with NH₃ — use excess dibromoethane or limited NH₃ to ensure further substitution — then nucleophilic substitution with KCN, then hydrolysis of the nitrile groups.)
Bundled gaps — the chain-lengthening logic, naming, and cyclisation
The nitrile route adds a carbon each time \(\ce{CN^-}\) replaces \(\ce{Br}\); hydrolysing \(\ce{-CN}\) with dilute acid gives \(\ce{-CO2H}\). Name the backbone off the longest chain including the \(\ce{-CO2H}\)/\(\ce{-NH2}\) locant.
Naming + cyclisation drill: 4-bromobutan-1-amine, \(\ce{H2N(CH2)4Br}\), has a nucleophile (N lone pair) and a leaving group (\(\ce{Br}\)) in the same molecule. The N attacks the δ+ C–Br intramolecularly (nucleophilic substitution), closing a 5-membered ring → pyrrolidine, after loss of \(\ce{H+}\).
Targets the gaps: reagent + condition on every arrow — "ethanolic," "dilute," "heat" are all mark-bearing. Count carbons first: each \(\ce{CN^-}\) step is +1 C. For cyclisation, a nucleophile and leaving group in one molecule ring-close; a 5- or 6-membered ring forms most readily.

Archetype · gap targeted
Gaps 8–10 — multi-step synthesis via the nitrile route (chain-lengthening to acids/amines), systematic naming, and intramolecular cyclisation to a 5-membered N-ring.

Deduce L/M/K by tracking C-count and functional-group change; CN⁻ adds a carbon; an internal nucleophile + leaving group cyclise.
