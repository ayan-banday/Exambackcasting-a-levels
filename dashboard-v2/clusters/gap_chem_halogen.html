Question 1 (HCI Class Exercise 1)

Question
A single enantiomer of 2-bromobutane is warmed with \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\).
(a) A parallel experiment replaces bromine with iodine, using 2-iodobutane under identical conditions. State which of the two reacts faster, and explain using bond energies. [2]
(b) The 2-bromobutane reacts by both SN2 and SN1. Draw both mechanisms, showing the stereochemistry of the product in each. [5]
(c) Assume the substrate reacts 50% by SN2 and 50% by SN1, and goes to completion. Deduce whether the butan-2-ol produced is optically active, showing the proportions of each enantiomer. [3]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution
(a) Which reacts faster
The rate-determining step of both mechanisms breaks the C–halogen bond. From the Data Booklet, C–Br ≈ 280 kJ mol⁻¹ is stronger than C–I ≈ 240 kJ mol⁻¹ (1), so C–I is easier to break → lower \(E_a\) → 2-iodobutane reacts faster than 2-bromobutane (1).
(b) Both mechanisms with stereochemistry
SN2: \(\ce{OH-}\) attacks the δ+ carbon from the side opposite Br, in one step, through a bracketed transition state → inversion of configuration → a single enantiomer (A) (1) (1). SN1: C–Br breaks first (slow) to give a trigonal planar carbocation; \(\ce{OH-}\) then attacks the top and bottom faces with equal probability → a racemic mixture (A + B) (1) (1) (1).
(c) Optical activity of the product mixture
50% via SN2 → 100% inversion → all one enantiomer → 50% A (1). 50% via SN1 → racemic → 25% A + 25% B (1). Total = 75% A + 25% B: because A is in excess (not a 50:50 racemate), the mixture is optically active (1).
The fatal drawing error is accidentally cancelling the SN2 inversion by swapping two groups. And "no optical activity" is not automatic for SN1 here — the SN2 half tips the balance so the overall product still rotates plane-polarised light.

Bond-energy rate comparison + both mechanisms + book-keeping the enantiomer proportions to decide net optical activity.

Question 2 (Prelim NJC 2017 / II / 4a)

Question
A halogenoalkane P is hydrolysed by aqueous sodium hydroxide; its substitution product is a chiral alcohol. Two experiments are run and the concentration of hydroxide is followed with time.
• In experiment 1, a graph of \(\ce{[OH-]}\) against time is a straight line of constant gradient.
• In experiment 2, the initial concentration of P is doubled, and the initial rate doubles (from \(1.538\times10^{-4}\) to \(3.077\times10^{-4}\) mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹).
(a) State the reagents and conditions for the hydrolysis. [1]
(b) Deduce the order of reaction with respect to \(\ce{OH-}\) and with respect to P, and hence write the rate equation. [3]
(c) Name the mechanism, and explain why the product shows no optical activity. [4]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a) Reagents / conditions
\(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\), heat (1).
(b) Orders and rate equation
Since \(\ce{[OH-]}\) decreases linearly with time at a fixed gradient, the rate stays constant as \(\ce{[OH-]}\) falls, so the order with respect to \(\ce{OH-}\) is 0 (1). Comparing the two experiments, when \(\ce{[P]}\) is doubled the rate also doubles, so the order with respect to P is 1 (1). Rate equation: rate = k[P] (1).
(c) Mechanism + no optical activity
Draw the planar cation attacked from both faces.
Mechanism: unimolecular nucleophilic substitution, SN1 (1). The trigonal planar carbocation allows the nucleophile to approach from the top and bottom of the plane at equal probability, producing equimolar enantiomers — a racemic mixture (1) (1). The rotation of plane-polarised light by one enantiomer is completely cancelled by the other, resulting in a lack of optical activity (1).
Read the graph, don't guess the order. A straight \(\ce{[OH-]}\)–time line at constant gradient = zero order in \(\ce{OH-}\) (rate independent of \(\ce{[OH-]}\)). Zero order in the nucleophile is the fingerprint of SN1 — the nucleophile is not in the rate-determining step.

Question 3 (HCI Class Exercise 2, Q2)

Question
An optically active compound R, \(\ce{C10H11Br}\), is refluxed with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Two organic products form: P, \(\ce{C10H12O}\), and Q, \(\ce{C10H10}\). R is a tertiary, benzylic bromide.
(a) By comparing each product's molecular formula with R, deduce the reaction type that gives P and the reaction type that gives Q. [2]
(b) Neither P nor Q is optically active, but for different reasons. Explain the reason in each case. [4]
(c) Describe a chemical test, with observations, to confirm that R is a bromo compound. [3]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution
(a) Deduce the reaction types from the formulae
R → P: \(\ce{C10H11Br -> C10H12O}\) is a net "−Br, +OH" → P is a nucleophilic substitution product (nucleophile \(\ce{OH-}\)) (1). R → Q: \(\ce{C10H11Br -> C10H10}\) is a net loss of HBr → Q is an elimination product (1). (P is the tertiary benzylic alcohol; Q is a conjugated diene.)
(b) Why each is optically inactive — different reasons
Draw the planar benzylic cation being attacked from both faces.
P — inactive because it is a racemate formed via SN1. R is tertiary, so it reacts by SN1 through a trigonal planar carbocation, stabilised by delocalisation into the benzene ring (benzylic) (1). The \(\ce{OH-}\) attacks the top and bottom faces with equal probability → equal amounts of the two enantiomers → racemic mixture → optically inactive (1).

Q — inactive because it has no chiral centre. Q has no carbon bonded to four different groups; its mirror image is superimposable on itself, so it cannot rotate plane-polarised light (1) (1).
(c) Confirming R is a bromo compound
Add ethanolic \(\ce{AgNO3}\) to R and warm → a cream precipitate of \(\ce{AgBr}\) forms (1) (1). To confirm the cream colour, add excess concentrated ammonia → the precipitate dissolves (\(\ce{AgBr}\) is soluble in concentrated \(\ce{NH3}\)) (1). (Alternatively: \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\)/warm, then excess dilute \(\ce{HNO3}\), then \(\ce{AgNO3(aq)}\) → cream ppt.)
"No optical activity" has two distinct causes — a racemate (P, formed via SN1) vs no chiral centre at all (Q). Naming the mechanism is not enough for P; you must draw the planar cation attacked from both faces to score.

Archetype
Structural elucidation by atom book-keeping + two-cause optical inactivity + confirmatory test.

Compare each product's formula to the parent to classify substitution vs elimination; separate racemate from achiral; confirm the halide.

Question 4 (HCI Class Exercise 1, Q2)

Question
In 1-bromocyclohexene the bromine is bonded to a carbon of the \(\ce{C=C}\) double bond (an sp²-hybridised carbon). In 3-bromocyclohexene the bromine is on an adjacent sp³ carbon, next to the \(\ce{C=C}\).
(a) Explain why 1-bromocyclohexene is unreactive towards nucleophiles, addressing why it resists both SN2 and SN1. [4]
(b) State, with a reason, whether 3-bromocyclohexene is more or less reactive than a typical primary halogenoalkane towards SN1. [2]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution
(a) Why 1-bromocyclohexene resists nucleophiles
The bromine lone pair (in a p orbital) delocalises side-on into the \(\ce{C=C}\) π system, giving the C–Br bond partial double-bond character → the bond is strengthened → hard to break (1). Resists SN2: the nucleophile must attack from the rear, but that side is blocked by the ring and the approaching nucleophile is repelled by the π-electron cloud (1); and the strengthened C–Br raises \(E_a\) (1). Resists SN1: the carbocation that would form has its empty p orbital orthogonal (perpendicular) to the \(\ce{C=C}\) π system, so it cannot be stabilised by it → very unstable → C–Br will not break (1).
(b) 3-bromocyclohexene (allylic)
More reactive towards SN1 than a typical primary halogenoalkane (1). When Br leaves, the empty p orbital is aligned to overlap with the \(\ce{C=C}\) π system, delocalising and reducing the positive charge of the (allylic) carbocation → it forms more readily (1).
Position is everything. A halogen on the sp² alkene carbon is inert (partial double-bond character, orthogonal cation). A halogen next to the \(\ce{C=C}\), on an sp³ carbon, is allylic and unusually SN1-reactive (the cation is stabilised by the very same π system).
Solution hidden — attempt first
Archetype
sp²/vinyl unreactivity vs sp³/allylic hyper-reactivity — the same π system, opposite effect.

On the C=C carbon → inert (both mechanisms shut off). Adjacent sp³ carbon → allylic → SN1 favoured by delocalisation.

Question 5 (Prelim PJC 2017 / III / 1e, modified)

Question
Compound A is a cyclohexane ring bearing, on one carbon, both an iodo group and a methyl group, and on the neighbouring carbon a chloro group.
(a) Iodoalkanes are hydrolysed faster than chloroalkanes. Give two reasons why the C–I group reacts before the C–Cl group. [2]
(b) A is warmed with 1 mol of \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\) to give B, and B is then heated with ammonia in ethanol under pressure to give C. Draw B and C, and identify which halogen is replaced at each stage. [4]
Before revealing — commit out loud: Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Solution
(a) Why C–I reacts before C–Cl
1. The bond energy of C–I (240) is lower than C–Cl (340 kJ mol⁻¹), so the C–I bond is weaker and more easily broken in the rate-determining step (1). 2. \(\ce{I-}\) is a weaker base / better leaving group than \(\ce{Cl-}\) (\(\ce{Cl-}\) is a stronger base), so it leaves more readily (1).
(b) Selective substitution A → B → C
1 mol NaOH substitutes only the more reactive C–I; ammonia then substitutes the remaining C–Cl.
A → B: 1 mol \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\), heat substitutes the more reactive C–I (weaker bond), so only the iodo group is replaced by \(\ce{-OH}\) — B is the chloro-alcohol (1) (1). B → C: \(\ce{NH3}\) in ethanol, heated under pressure substitutes the remaining C–Cl by \(\ce{-NH2}\) — C is the amino-alcohol (1) (1).
Selectivity comes from bond strength. With only 1 mol of base, the weaker C–I goes first; the C–Cl survives to the next step. Aqueous \(\ce{NaOH}\) gives substitution (not elimination) — and note \(\ce{NH3}\) is used in ethanol under pressure, the standard amine condition.

Archetype
Reagent/leaving-group selectivity threaded through a multi-step synthesis + deduction.

Bond strength and leaving-group ability decide which halogen reacts first; limited reagent isolates one substitution before the next.
