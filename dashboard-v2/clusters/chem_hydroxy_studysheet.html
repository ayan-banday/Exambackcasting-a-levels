Chemistry — Hydroxy Compounds

H2 9476 · Topic 16 · Organic · STUDY SHEET (read this first, then drill). Alcohols & phenols. Built from the HCI lecture notes, HCI Class Exercises 1 & 2, and the HCI "Organic Reactions by Reagents" reference. Green dashed boxes are verbatim mark-scheme wording — reproduce them word-for-word. Mechanisms are NOT required in this chapter (you may be guided to draw one, but you don't need them cold).

THE SPINE (Layer 1) — one functional group, \(\ce{-OH}\), on two very different carbons. On an alkyl carbon → an alcohol; bonded directly to a benzene ring → a phenol. Three ideas run through everything: (1) hydrogen bonding sets the physical properties (bp, solubility); (2) acidity is compared only through conjugate-base stability, giving the ladder alcohol < water < phenol; (3) the reaction map — alcohols are the second great synthesis hub (→ halogenoalkane, alkene, aldehyde/ketone, carboxylic acid, ester), while phenol behaves oppositely (won't dehydrate, won't esterify with a carboxylic acid, resists nucleophilic substitution) yet its ring is strongly activated toward electrophilic substitution. Two diagnostics recur: oxidation is the 1°/2°/3° test, and four colour/ppt tests identify what you hold (orange→green dichromate · yellow ppt iodoform · violet FeCl₃ · white ppt + decolourised bromine water).

1 Nomenclature & classification Layer 1

Alcohol = an \(\ce{-OH}\) group bonded to an alkyl (sp³) carbon, \(\ce{R-OH}\). Phenol = an \(\ce{-OH}\) group bonded directly to a benzene ring, \(\ce{C6H5OH}\). Name an alcohol by replacing the alkane's terminal -e with -ol; the longest chain must contain the \(\ce{-OH}\); number from the end nearer the \(\ce{-OH}\) so it takes the lowest locant (propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol). "Hydroxy" alone is a group, not a functional group.
Layer 2 — how to classify 1° / 2° / 3°
Look only at the carbon that bears the \(\ce{-OH}\), then use either test (they agree):
ClassAlkyl (C) groups on that carbonH atoms on that carbonExample
Primary (1°)1 (or 0)2ethanol \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\), propan-1-ol
Secondary (2°)21propan-2-ol \(\ce{(CH3)2CHOH}\)
Tertiary (3°)302-methylpropan-2-ol \(\ce{(CH3)3COH}\)
Always state 1°/2°/3° explicitly when you identify an alcohol — it decides the oxidation product. A phenol is generally NOT classed 1°/2°/3° — call it a phenol.
The \(\ce{-OH}\) outranks alkyl and halogen for the lowest locant, but when a higher-priority group (e.g. \(\ce{-COOH}\), \(\ce{C=O}\)) is present the \(\ce{-OH}\) becomes a substituent prefix, "hydroxy-" (e.g. 3-hydroxybutanoic acid, not "3-ol butanoic acid"). Cite substituents alphabetically: 5-bromo-2-methylphenol, not "2-methyl-5-bromophenol".
Traps. (1) \(\ce{C6H5CH2OH}\) is phenylmethanol — a primary ALCOHOL, not a phenol: the \(\ce{-OH}\) sits on an sp³ \(\ce{CH2}\), not on the ring. (2) "Hydroxy" is a group; a carboxylic acid is a carbonyl group + a hydroxy group together. (3) Modern term is constitutional isomerism (old: "structural"); an ether \(\ce{R-O-R}\) is a constitutional isomer of an alcohol — no protonic H, so no H-bonding → lower bp.

2 Physical properties — it's all hydrogen bonding

Layer 2 — boiling point
Compared with the isoelectronic alkane, an alcohol boils much higher (ethane \(-89\,°\mathrm{C}\) vs ethanol \(78\,°\mathrm{C}\)) even though their electron clouds — and so their dispersion forces — are similar.
The alcohol also forms intermolecular hydrogen bonds (through the \(\ce{O-H}\)), which are stronger than dispersion forces, so more energy is needed to overcome the intermolecular attractions and the boiling point is higher. More \(\ce{-OH}\) groups → more extensive and stronger H-bonding → higher bp (ethane < ethanol < ethane-1,2-diol). bp also rises with chain length (bigger electron cloud → stronger dispersion forces; H-bonding stays roughly constant).
Layer 2 — solubility in water
Short-chain alcohols are miscible: the H-bonds formed with water release enough energy to overcome both the alcohol–alcohol H-bonding and the dispersion forces between chains. As the chain lengthens, solubility falls — the dispersion forces between the growing non-polar chains are no longer repaid by the alcohol–water H-bonding, and the long chain disrupts water's own H-bonding. Phenol is only moderately soluble (large non-polar ring; strong ring–ring dispersion) — cloudy in cold water, but dissolves fully when warmed.
Two exam-useful subtleties. (1) A straight chain has a larger surface area than its branched isomer → stronger dispersion → higher bp (hexan-1-ol 158 °C > 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol 120 °C). (2) A rigid ring (phenyl) gives stronger, better-defined dispersion forces than a flexible chain of similar \(M_r\) (methylbenzene 111 °C > hexane 69 °C). Never explain the halide/alcohol bp trends by "polarity" — it's electron-cloud size + H-bonding.

3 Acidity — the conceptual spine Layer 1

Layer 1 — the one rule
To compare acid strength you compare the stability of the CONJUGATE BASE — the anion left after \(\ce{H+}\) is lost. A more stable conjugate base is less likely to re-accept \(\ce{H+}\) → greater dissociation → stronger acid, larger \(K_a\), lower \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) (\(\mathrm{p}K_a=-\log_{10}K_a\)). Anything that disperses the negative charge stabilises the anion (↑ acidity); anything that intensifies it destabilises (↓ acidity). Never compare the neutral acids directly.
The acidity ladder: alcohol < water < phenol.
• Alcohols are weaker acids than water: the alkyl group is electron-donating → intensifies the negative charge on the alkoxide O → destabilises it → less dissociation. Alcohols are essentially neutral — they do not turn blue litmus red. (Chain length has no significant effect.)
• Phenol is a much stronger acid (0.1 mol dm⁻³ phenol has pH ≈ 5.4 → turns blue litmus red): in the phenoxide ion a p orbital on the O overlaps the ring π-cloud, so the negative charge is partially delocalised into the benzene ring → phenoxide is far more stabilised than an alkoxide.
Ethoxide (charge intensified by alkyl → weak acid) vs phenoxide (charge delocalised into the ring → strong acid). The bulk of the charge stays on O.
\(\mathrm{p}K_a\) benchmarks to memorise (anchor any comparison to these):
Phenol\(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 10\)
Ethanol (alcohol)\(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 16\)
Ethanoic acid (carboxylic acid)\(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 4.76 \approx 5\)
Ethanoic acid ≫ phenol ≫ ethanol. A carboxylic acid beats phenol because the ethanoate charge is spread equally over two equivalent oxygens, far more effective than phenol's mostly-on-one-O delocalisation.
Substituent effects (same rule, both classes). Electron-withdrawing groups (\(\ce{-NO2}\), \(\ce{-Cl/-Br/-I}\), \(\ce{-CF3}\), \(\ce{-CN}\)) disperse/stabilise the negative charge → stronger acid; the nearer the EWG sits to the \(\ce{-O^-}\), the stronger the effect (inductive withdrawal weakens with distance — 1-chloropropan-1-ol > 2-chloropropan-1-ol). Electron-donating groups (\(\ce{-CH3}\), \(\ce{-OCH3}\), \(\ce{-NH2}\)) intensify the charge / reduce delocalisation → weaker acid. So a nitrophenol > phenol > a methylphenol in acidity.
Traps. (1) Alkyl chain length does NOT set acidity — "a longer chain is a stronger donor" is false; a bulkier alkoxide (tert-butoxide) is actually a weaker acid because it H-bonds less with the solvent. (2) Phenoxide's charge is mostly on O, NOT shared 1/7 equally over the ring carbons. (3) Say which species you mean — compare phenoxide with alkoxide, not "phenol with alkoxide". (4) Use "partially" delocalised, never "fully".

4 Reactions as acids — Na, NaOH, carbonate Layer 1

Layer 1 keywords
With sodium (redox): both alcohols and phenol react with \(\ce{Na(s)}\), room temperature → alkoxide / phenoxide + \(\ce{1/2 H2}\) (the \(\ce{O-H}\) breaks). With \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\): phenol reacts (it is acidic enough), alcohols do NOT. With carbonate: neither reacts — a key point that separates phenol from a carboxylic acid.
Na (acid–metal, redox): Na is oxidised \(0\to+1\); H is reduced \(+1\to0\) in \(\ce{H2}\). Observation: effervescence of \(\ce{H2}\) (test with a lit splint → "pop"). Because phenol is a stronger acid than an alcohol, phenol gives rapid effervescence while an alcohol gives slow effervescence.
\(\ce{2C2H5OH + 2Na -> 2C2H5O^{-}Na^{+} + H2}\)  ·  \(\ce{2C6H5OH + 2Na -> 2C6H5O^{-}Na^{+} + H2}\)
NaOH (acid–base): phenol + \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\) → sodium phenoxide + water. A cloudy phenol/water mixture turns into a clear, colourless, homogeneous solution (the ionic phenoxide has strong ion–dipole attraction to water). \(\ce{C6H5OH + NaOH -> C6H5O^{-}Na^{+} + H2O}\). Alcohols are weaker than water, so they do not react with \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\).
Traps. (1) The Na reaction is a single electron transfer to the protonic H — the alkoxy group itself is neither oxidised nor reduced. (2) NaOH dissolving phenol is NOT the test for phenol — the dedicated test is neutral \(\ce{FeCl3}\) (§10/11). (3) "Alcohols don't react with NaOH" needs the word aqueous — neat ethanol + solid NaOH/KOH pellets can give the ethoxide. (4) Neither phenol nor alcohol reacts with \(\ce{Na2CO3}\) (no \(\ce{CO2}\)) — a carboxylic acid does (effervescence of \(\ce{CO2}\)), so carbonate distinguishes phenol from a carboxylic acid.

Reagents conc \(\ce{HCl} + \ce{ZnCl2}\): 3° → cloudy immediately; 2° → cloudy within ~5 min at room temp; 1° → no cloudiness unless warmed. (Rate tracks carbocation stability: 3° > 2° > 1°.)
Trap. Phenol does NOT undergo this substitution — same reason as halogenoarenes: the O lone pair is delocalised into the ring → the C–O bond has partial double-bond character → strong and hard to break, and the ring π-cloud repels the nucleophile.

6 Dehydration (elimination) → alkene Layer 1

Layer 1 keywords
Reagents (know all three): excess conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\), heat · conc \(\ce{H3PO4}\), heat · \(\ce{Al2O3}\), heat (dry). Loses \(\ce{-OH}\) plus an H from the adjacent (β) carbon → alkene + \(\ce{H2O}\). This is a β-elimination.
\(\ce{H3PO4}\) is often preferred over \(\ce{H2SO4}\) because conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) can act as an oxidising agent and give side-products. No H on the β-carbon → no dehydration — so phenol cannot be dehydrated (no β-H). A dehydration may give a mixture of alkenes; the more-substituted alkene is the more stable (thermodynamic) major product (Saytzeff).
Traps. (1) You must write "excess" conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) for dehydration (excess so it acts as the dehydrating agent, not diluted by the water made). (2) cis and trans isomers are distinct organic products — count both. hexan-3-ol → 4 products (hex-2-ene cis+trans, hex-3-ene cis+trans). (3) A symmetrical ring alcohol (cyclohexanol) gives one product; a trans-cycloalkene is impossible for a 7-membered ring or smaller (ring strain), so only the cis forms.

7 Esterification — two routes, and why phenol is different Layer 1

Layer 1 keywords
A condensation (a small molecule is lost). Route A — carboxylic acid: alcohol + carboxylic acid, a few drops conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) catalyst, heat → ester + \(\ce{H2O}\) (slow, reversible). Route B — acyl chloride \(\ce{RCOCl}\): room temp, no catalyst, goes to completion → ester + \(\ce{HCl}\) (white fumes). Route B works for both alcohols and phenols.
Alcohols take either route; phenols take only the acyl-chloride route.
Role of conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) in Route A: it protonates the carbonyl oxygen, making the carbonyl carbon more electron-deficient and so more susceptible to nucleophilic attack by the weak nucleophile (the alcohol). Only a few drops — enough to catalyse, not to dehydrate.
The classic trap. Phenol does NOT react with a carboxylic acid — the O lone pair is delocalised into the ring, so phenol is a weak nucleophile and the carboxylic acid is not reactive enough. To make a phenyl ester you must use an acyl chloride (and converting phenol to phenoxide first, a stronger nucleophile, improves the yield). Do not confuse which \(\ce{-OH}\) partner works with which acyl reagent.

8 Oxidation — the 1°/2°/3° diagnostic (the most important section) Layer 1

Layer 1 keywords
Two oxidant systems: \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) / dil \(\ce{H2SO4}\) / heat (milder) and \(\ce{KMnO4}\) / dil \(\ce{H2SO4}\) / heat (stronger). Oxidation needs an H on the carbon bearing \(\ce{-OH}\) (an α-H), so 3° alcohols and phenols are NOT oxidised. 1° → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; 2° → ketone; 3° → no reaction.
1° oxidises twice (stop at the aldehyde by distilling it off; go to the acid under reflux); 2° → ketone; 3° → nothing.
To stop at the ALDEHYDE: \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\)/dil \(\ce{H2SO4}\), heat with IMMEDIATE DISTILLATION — the aldehyde has no H-bonding, so it is more volatile and distils off before it oxidises further. To get the CARBOXYLIC ACID: heat under REFLUX with excess oxidant. Only \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) is mild enough to stop at the aldehyde; \(\ce{KMnO4}\) always overshoots straight to the acid.
Colour changes: \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\): orange \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\) → green \(\ce{Cr^3+}\). \(\ce{KMnO4}\): purple \(\ce{MnO4^-}\) → colourless \(\ce{Mn^2+}\). As a distinguishing test for 1°/2°/3°, reflux with acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\): 3° stays orange; 1° and 2° turn green.
Traps. (1) There is no such thing as "\(\ce{KMnO4}\), heat with immediate distillation" — \(\ce{KMnO4}\) can't stop at the aldehyde. (2) A simple test-tube test must NOT use reflux or distillation (keep it < 1 min; use a water bath if heating). (3) \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) does NOT cleave a \(\ce{C=C}\); hot \(\ce{KMnO4}\) does (oxidative cleavage) — so a molecule with both a 2° alcohol and an alkene gives a ketone with \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) but is also cleaved by \(\ce{KMnO4}\).

9 Tri-iodomethane (iodoform) test Layer 1

Detects the \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}\) group (a "methyl carbinol"), or a methyl ketone \(\ce{CH3CO-}\). Reagents: \(\ce{I2(aq)} + \ce{NaOH(aq)}\), warm (below 60 °C). Positive result: a yellow precipitate of \(\ce{CHI3}\) (tri-iodomethane), plus a carboxylate \(\ce{RCOO^-}\). It is a step-down reaction (the chain shortens by one carbon).
Only the \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)R}\) / \(\ce{CH3CO-}\) pattern gives the yellow \(\ce{CHI3}\) precipitate.
Balanced equation — derive it, don't rote-learn "4 6 5 5":
\(\ce{CH3CH(OH)R + 4I2 + 6OH^{-} -> CHI3 + RCOO^{-} + 5I^{-} + 5H2O}\)
(the α-C goes \(0\to+3\), losing 3 e⁻; the methyl C goes \(-3\to+2\), losing 5 e⁻; 8 e⁻ total reduce 4 \(\ce{I2}\); 3 of the 8 \(\ce{I^-}\) end up in \(\ce{CHI3}\), 5 remain; balance charge with 6 \(\ce{OH^-}\), then O/H with 5 \(\ce{H2O}\)).
Traps. (1) Among primary alcohols only ethanol gives a positive test (it is the only 1° alcohol carrying \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}\) with R = H). Every other iodoform-positive alcohol is secondary. (2) Tertiary alcohols never give it. (3) It's a step-down (−1 C) — contrast the KCN step-up (+1 C).

10 Phenol — electrophilic substitution on the activated ring Layer 1

The O lone pair partially delocalises into the ring → greatly raises the ring's electron density, so phenol is far more reactive to electrophiles than benzene. Two consequences: (1) milder conditions — no catalyst, no heat; (2) \(\ce{-OH}\) is strongly activating and 2,4-directing → easily gives 2,4,6-trisubstituted products. (Net effect: the π-donation outweighs the σ-withdrawal, so \(\ce{-OH}\) is overall electron-donating — same logic as \(\ce{-NH2}\).)
Bromination needs no catalyst; the by-product is HBr (the ring H is substituted), not H₂.
Bromination: aqueous \(\ce{Br2}\) (bromine water), room temp → white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol, and the yellow-orange bromine is decolourised — no catalyst needed. For mono-bromination: low temperature + a non-polar solvent (\(\ce{Br2(l)}\) or \(\ce{Br2}\) in \(\ce{CCl4}\)). Nitration: dilute \(\ce{HNO3(aq)}\), room temp → 2-nitrophenol + 4-nitrophenol (pale yellow); concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) → 2,4,6-trinitrophenol. (Contrast benzene: bromination needs \(\ce{Br2}\)/\(\ce{AlBr3}\); nitration needs conc \(\ce{HNO3}\) + conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) at ~50 °C.)
Why phenol nitrates far more easily than methylbenzene: the O lone pair delocalises directly into the ring, so \(\ce{-OH}\) is much more electron-donating than \(\ce{-CH3}\); the electron-rich ring reacts even with the weak electrophile \(\ce{HNO3}\) itself, no catalyst. Methylbenzene needs conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) to protonate \(\ce{HNO3}\) and generate the strong electrophile \(\ce{NO2^+}\) (nitronium ion); conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) acts as both catalyst and dehydrating agent.
Traps. (1) The bromination by-product is HBr (the ring H leaves as \(\ce{H+}\); one Br becomes \(\ce{Br-}\)) — never \(\ce{H2}\). (2) "White precipitate" is only reliable for 2,4,6-tribromophenol; for a substituted phenol the mono-bromo product may be an oil, so quote decolourisation + white fumes of HBr as the safer observations. (3) Don't guess halogen colours — \(\ce{Br2(aq)}\) is orange / yellow-orange (data booklet).

13 Active recall — cover, answer aloud, then reveal

Do not read these — generate the answer from memory first, then reveal to mark yourself. If you hesitate on any, that node isn't encoded yet.

Q1 · Classify and name \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3}\) and \(\ce{(CH3)3COH}\). Why must you state 1°/2°/3°?
Butan-2-ol (2°) — the C–OH carbon carries two alkyl groups. 2-methylpropan-2-ol (3°) — three alkyl groups. You state the class because it decides the oxidation product (2° → ketone; 3° → no reaction).
Hidden — answer first
Q2 · Why does ethanol boil higher than ethane but lower than ethane-1,2-diol? Why does alcohol solubility fall with chain length?
Ethanol has H-bonding (stronger than the dispersion in ethane) → more energy to overcome → higher bp; ethane-1,2-diol has two \(\ce{-OH}\) → more extensive/stronger H-bonding → higher still. Solubility falls because the growing non-polar chain's dispersion forces are no longer repaid by alcohol–water H-bonding, and the chain disrupts water's H-bonding.
Hidden — answer first
Q3 · Rank ethanol, water, phenol by acid strength and justify each via conjugate-base stability.
ethanol < water < phenol. Ethoxide: alkyl donates → intensifies the charge on O → less stable → weakest acid. Phenoxide: charge partially delocalised into the ring → most stable → strongest. \(\mathrm{p}K_a\): phenol ≈ 10, ethanol ≈ 16.
Hidden — answer first
Q4 · What do alcohols and phenol each do with (a) Na, (b) NaOH(aq), (c) Na₂CO₃? Use it to distinguish phenol from an alcohol AND from a carboxylic acid.
(a) Na: both effervesce \(\ce{H2}\) (phenol faster). (b) NaOH(aq): phenol reacts (cloudy→clear); alcohol doesn't → separates phenol from alcohol. (c) Na₂CO₃: neither reacts, but a carboxylic acid gives \(\ce{CO2}\) → separates phenol from a carboxylic acid.
Hidden — answer first
Q5 · Reagents/conditions to oxidise a 1° alcohol to (a) the aldehyde, (b) the carboxylic acid. What apparatus differs and why? What about 2° and 3°?
(a) \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\)/\(\ce{H2SO4}\), heat + immediate distillation (the volatile aldehyde distils off before further oxidation). (b) heat under reflux with excess oxidant. 2° → ketone; 3° → no reaction (no α-H). Never \(\ce{KMnO4}\) for the aldehyde — it overshoots to the acid.
Hidden — answer first
Q6 · Give reagents to convert ethanol → chloroethane (two ways), → bromoethane, → ethene. Which catalyst for dehydration, and why not H₂SO₄?
Chloroethane: \(\ce{PCl5}\) (r.t.) or conc \(\ce{HCl} + \ce{ZnCl2}\). Bromoethane: conc \(\ce{HBr}\) or \(\ce{NaBr}\) + conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\). Ethene: excess conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\), heat (or conc \(\ce{H3PO4}\) / \(\ce{Al2O3}\)). Prefer \(\ce{H3PO4}\) because \(\ce{H2SO4}\) can oxidise and give side-products.
Hidden — answer first
Q7 · Why can't phenol undergo nucleophilic substitution of \(\ce{-OH}\), nor esterify with a carboxylic acid? How then do you make a phenyl ester?
Both fail because the O lone pair is delocalised into the ring: the C–O bond gains partial double-bond character (hard to break) and phenol is a weak nucleophile. Make a phenyl ester with an acyl chloride (convert phenol to phenoxide first for better yield).

Q8 · Which compounds give a positive iodoform test, what are the reagents and observation, and is it a step-up or step-down?
Anything with \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}\) or a methyl ketone \(\ce{CH3CO-}\). Reagents \(\ce{I2(aq)} + \ce{NaOH}\), warm → yellow ppt of \(\ce{CHI3}\). It is a step-down (−1 C). Among 1° alcohols only ethanol works.

Q9 · Compare the bromination and nitration of phenol with benzene (reagents, conditions, products). Why is phenol so much more reactive?
Phenol: \(\ce{Br2(aq)}\), no catalyst → 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white ppt); dilute \(\ce{HNO3}\) → 2-/4-nitrophenol. Benzene needs \(\ce{Br2}\)/\(\ce{AlBr3}\) and conc \(\ce{HNO3}\)+conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\)/50 °C. Phenol's O lone pair delocalises into the ring → higher electron density → activated.

Q10 · Name three distinguishing tests for a phenol and the positive observation of each.
Neutral \(\ce{FeCl3}\) → violet. Bromine water → decolourised + white ppt (of 2,4,6-tribromophenol). \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\) → cloudy mixture dissolves to a clear solution (weaker evidence). Also: phenol does not effervesce with \(\ce{Na2CO3}\) (unlike a carboxylic acid).
