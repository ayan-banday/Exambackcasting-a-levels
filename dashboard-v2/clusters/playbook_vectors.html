TRIAGE — reading the stem in ten seconds

Almost every Vectors question is decided by three questions asked in order.

AskWhat it settles
1. How many objects, and of what kind?
points / vectors / lines / planes		Points-and-vectors only → Part A/B. One line → 13–20. One plane → 21–26. Two objects → 16, 27, 31.
2. What relationship word appears?
parallel · perpendicular · contains · lies in · meets · reflects · nearest		This is the single highest-yield word in the stem. Parallel → scalar multiple or cross = 0. Perpendicular → dot = 0. Nearest / shortest → distance formula. Contains / lies in → substitute the general point.
3. Is the answer a scalar or a vector?Decides whether a modulus goes on. Length of projection, distance and angle are scalars and take a modulus. Projection vector, position vector and normal are vectors and do not.
The one-line summary of your two Priority-1 gaps. Dot = 0 means perpendicular. Cross = 0 means parallel. And a line perpendicular to a plane has its direction parallel to the normal, so you test it with a scalar multiple, never with a dot product.

THREE DECISION TREES

Which product?

Do I want a number (angle, length, a test for perpendicularity)? → dot.
Do I want a new vector perpendicular to what I have (a normal, an area, a distance to a line)? → cross.
Am I testing parallel? → scalar multiple, or cross \( = \mathbf 0 \).
Am I testing perpendicular? → dot \( = 0 \).

Which distance formula? (there are only two in 9758)

Point to a LINE → \( |\overrightarrow{AP}\times\hat{\mathbf d}| \)   (A is any point on the line)
Point to a PLANE → \( |\overrightarrow{AP}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}| \)   (A is any point on the plane)
Line to line (skew) is NOT in 9758. The syllabus excludes both the shortest distance between skew lines and the common perpendicular, and it excludes triple products \( \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) \), which that formula is. If a stem looks like a skew-distance question, the real ask is a point-to-line minimisation.
Do not assume they are the same distance. Your notes state it flatly on p97: "the ⊥ distance from A to plane is different from the ⊥ distance from A to line, though the line is on the plane." Dropping perpendicular to a line inside a plane lands you somewhere further away than dropping perpendicular to the plane.

Foot of perpendicular — line or plane?

To a LINE: you already know \( F \) is on the line, so write \( \overrightarrow{OF}=\mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf d \) and impose \( \overrightarrow{PF}\cdot\mathbf d=0 \). The unknown is where along the line.
To a PLANE: you already know the direction you travel is \( \mathbf n \), so write \( \mathbf r=\overrightarrow{OP}+t\mathbf n \) and substitute into \( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=D \). The unknown is how far you travel.
That is the whole distinction: for a line you know the road and solve for the position; for a plane you know the heading and solve for the distance.

PART A — VECTOR FUNDAMENTALS

1 Unit vector, or a vector of stated length in a stated direction
Trigger "find a unit vector in the direction of…", "find the vector of magnitude 12 in the direction of…", "\(\overrightarrow{ST}\) has length k and is parallel to…"
  1. Compute \( |\mathbf a|=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2} \).
  2. \( \hat{\mathbf a}=\dfrac{\mathbf a}{|\mathbf a|} \).
  3. For a stated length: (required vector) = (magnitude) × (unit vector).
Micro-example
\( \mathbf a=(2,-1,2) \Rightarrow |\mathbf a|=3 \), \( \hat{\mathbf a}=\tfrac13(2,-1,2) \). Length 12 in that direction: \( 12\cdot\tfrac13(2,-1,2)=(8,-4,8) \).
Trap Two vectors of the same length may point opposite ways. If the stem only says "parallel to" and not "in the direction of," there are two answers, \( \pm \).
2 Show two vectors are equal, or a quadrilateral is a parallelogram
Trigger "show that ABCD is a parallelogram", "show that \(\mathbf p=\mathbf q\)"
  1. To show two non-zero vectors are equal you need both: \( \mathbf p \parallel \mathbf q \) (i.e. \( \mathbf p=k\mathbf q \)) and \( |\mathbf p|=|\mathbf q| \). Equivalently just show the column vectors are identical.
  2. For a parallelogram \( ABCD \), show \( \overrightarrow{AB}=\overrightarrow{DC} \).
Trap Your notes flag this explicitly: "the vertices in a polygon must be labelled in either clockwise or anticlockwise manner." So in \(ABCD\) the side opposite \(AB\) is \(DC\), not \(CD\). Getting this backwards gives you \( \overrightarrow{AB}=-\overrightarrow{CD} \) and a sign error.
3 Ratio theorem — position vector of a dividing point
Trigger "P divides AB in the ratio λ : μ", "AN : NB = 1 : 2", "M is the midpoint of…", "BP is produced to…"
  1. Draw the segment and mark the two ratio numbers on it.
  2. Apply \( \overrightarrow{OP}=\dfrac{\mu\,\mathbf a+\lambda\,\mathbf b}{\lambda+\mu} \) where \( AP:PB=\lambda:\mu \). Each weight sits on the OPPOSITE endpoint. The far end gets the near number.
  3. Midpoint is the special case \( \overrightarrow{OM}=\tfrac12(\mathbf a+\mathbf b) \).
Micro-example
\( A(1,2,3) \), \( B(4,-1,0) \), \( AP:PB=2:1 \). Then \( \overrightarrow{OP}=\dfrac{1\cdot\mathbf a+2\cdot\mathbf b}{3}=\dfrac{(1,2,3)+(8,-2,0)}{3}=(3,0,1) \). The 2 from \(AP\) lands on \(\mathbf b\).
The generalisation the notes give and most people miss. "When using the ratio theorem, all three vectors must be pointing outwards (or inwards) from a common point. The common point need not necessarily be the origin O." So \( \overrightarrow{CP}=\tfrac16(5\overrightarrow{CB}+\overrightarrow{CA}) \) is just as legal as the \(O\) version. Anchoring at a vertex instead of dragging everything back to the origin shortens a lot of the harder ratio questions.
Trap \( \overrightarrow{OM}\neq\tfrac12\overrightarrow{BD} \) for a midpoint \(M\) of \(BD\). What is true is \( \overrightarrow{BM}=\tfrac12\overrightarrow{BD} \). The half applies to the displacement along the segment, not to the position vector.

For a produced line (P beyond C), convert the ratio first: \( BC:BP=1:\lambda \) becomes \( BC:CP=1:(\lambda-1) \).
4 Prove three points are collinear
Trigger "show that A, B and C are collinear", "show that P lies on AB"
  1. Form two displacement vectors that share a point: \( \overrightarrow{AB} \) and \( \overrightarrow{AC} \).
  2. Show one is a scalar multiple of the other: \( \overrightarrow{AB}=k\,\overrightarrow{AC} \).
  3. State the common point in words. "Since \( \overrightarrow{AB}=k\overrightarrow{AC} \), \(AB\) is parallel to \(AC\), and they share the common point \(A\); therefore \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) are collinear."
Trap Parallel alone is not collinear. Two parallel lines a metre apart are still parallel. The common point is a mark, so write it down.

And the notes' standing warning: "There is no division in vectors. Hence it is incorrect to write \( k=\mathbf u/\mathbf v \)." If you want a ratio of lengths, write \( \dfrac{1}{1-\lambda}=\dfrac{AB}{AP} \) using unbolded lengths, never a quotient of vectors.
5 Prove a geometric result by expanding a dot product
Trigger "prove that the diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular", "deduce the cosine rule", "show that the median…"
  1. Name the two independent sides \( \mathbf p \) and \( \mathbf q \). Everything else is built from these.
  2. Write the objects of interest in terms of \( \mathbf p,\mathbf q \) (diagonals of a parallelogram are \( \mathbf p+\mathbf q \) and \( \mathbf p-\mathbf q \)).
  3. Expand the dot product using distributivity, and use \( \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a=|\mathbf a|^2 \).
  4. Apply the special condition (rhombus ⇒ \( |\mathbf p|=|\mathbf q| \)) and read off the conclusion.
Micro-example
\( (\mathbf p+\mathbf q)\cdot(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)=|\mathbf p|^2-|\mathbf q|^2 \). In a rhombus \( |\mathbf p|=|\mathbf q| \), so this is \(0\), so the diagonals are perpendicular.
Trap \( (3\mathbf b-2\mathbf c)\cdot(3\mathbf b-2\mathbf c)=|3\mathbf b-2\mathbf c|^2 \), and that is not \( (3\mathbf b-2\mathbf c)^2 \) — squaring a vector is meaningless notation. Also: \( \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c \) does not give \( \mathbf b=\mathbf c \). There is no cancellation law.

PART B — PRODUCTS AND PROJECTIONS

6 Angle between two vectors
Trigger "find angle AOB", "find the acute angle between…", "find the angle \(\mathbf p\) makes with the positive x-axis"
  1. Make sure both vectors diverge from (or both converge to) the shared vertex. For \( \angle ABO \) the vectors are \( \overrightarrow{BA} \) and \( \overrightarrow{BO} \), not \( \overrightarrow{AB} \).
  2. \( \cos\theta=\dfrac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|} \) — no modulus, because the answer is allowed to be obtuse.
  3. For an axis, dot with \( \mathbf i=(1,0,0) \), \( \mathbf j=(0,1,0) \) or \( \mathbf k=(0,0,1) \).
Micro-example
\( (1,2,2)\cdot(2,-1,2)=4 \), both have magnitude 3, so \( \cos\theta=\tfrac49 \) and \( \theta=63.6^\circ \).
Trap The sign of the dot product is information: positive means acute, negative means obtuse. Slapping a modulus on out of habit destroys it. Bars go on only when the stem says "acute" or when the objects are lines or planes (which have no direction).
7 Show perpendicular / find the unknown that makes it perpendicular
Trigger "show that OA is perpendicular to OB", "find the value of p for which … are perpendicular", "show that l is perpendicular to plane π"
  1. First decide what kind of objects they are. This is the step that has cost you marks twice.
    1. vector ⊥ vector, or line ⊥ line → \( \mathbf d_1\cdot\mathbf d_2=0 \).
    2. line ⊥ plane → the direction is parallel to the normal. Show \( \mathbf d=k\mathbf n \). Do not dot them.
    3. plane ⊥ plane → \( \mathbf n_1\cdot\mathbf n_2=0 \).
    4. line lies in plane → \( \mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n=0 \) and a point of the line satisfies the plane.
  2. Set the relevant expression to zero and solve for the unknown.
Micro-example
\( (1,2,p)\cdot(3,-1,2)=3-2+2p=1+2p=0 \Rightarrow p=-\tfrac12 \).

8 Find a vector perpendicular to two given vectors
Trigger "find a vector perpendicular to both…", "find a normal to the plane containing…"
  1. Cross them: \( \mathbf a\times\mathbf b=(a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\ -(a_1b_3-a_3b_1),\ a_1b_2-a_2b_1) \). The middle entry carries a leading minus.
  2. Simplify by factoring out any common scalar. \( (-2,-2,-2)=-2(1,1,1) \), so quote \( (1,1,1) \).
  3. Check. Dot your answer with each original vector; both must give zero.
Micro-example
\( (1,1,-2)\times(3,1,-4)=(-2,-2,-2)=-2(1,1,1) \). Check: \( (1,1,1)\cdot(1,1,-2)=0 \) and \( (1,1,1)\cdot(3,1,-4)=0 \). ✓
Your words Simplifying the normal is a logged gap: on \( (-3,3,3)=-3(1,-1,-1) \) you said "how do you get inverse 1 −1 −1? what's an inverse? that actually got me good." It is not an inverse. You are dividing every component by the common factor \(-3\), which gives a shorter vector pointing the same way along the same line, and any such vector is an equally valid normal.
Trap The answer is not unique — any non-zero multiple works, in either direction. That means a "wrong-looking" normal can still be right. But it also means you must use the same normal consistently through the rest of the question, especially in a distance-between-planes calculation.
9 Length of projection vs projection vector
Trigger "find the length of projection of a on b", "find the projection vector of OA onto BC", "find \(\overrightarrow{AN}\) where N is the foot of perpendicular from A to OB"
  1. Build \( \hat{\mathbf b}=\mathbf b/|\mathbf b| \).
  2. Compute the signed scalar \( s=\mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf b} \).
  3. Length of projection \( =|s| \). Take the modulus.
  4. Projection vector \( =s\,\hat{\mathbf b} \). Keep the sign.
Micro-example
\( \mathbf a=(1,2,-2) \), \( \mathbf b=(2,-1,2) \), \( |\mathbf b|=3 \). \( s=-\tfrac43 \). Length of projection \( =\tfrac43 \). Projection vector \( =-\tfrac43\cdot\tfrac13(2,-1,2)=\left(-\tfrac89,\tfrac49,-\tfrac89\right) \), which points opposite to \( \mathbf b \) — and the negative sign is what tells you so.
Your words "I forgot the projection factor. I don't even know what the projection…" Rebuild it from the picture, not from memory: drop a perpendicular from the tip of \( \mathbf a \) onto the line of \( \mathbf b \). The distance along \( \mathbf b \) is \( \mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf b} \); to turn that distance into a vector you re-attach the direction by multiplying by \( \hat{\mathbf b} \) again. That is the whole formula.
Trap Answering the wrong species. If the question says "length," a vector answer scores nothing, and vice versa. Read the last word of the stem.
10 Area of a triangle or parallelogram
Trigger "find the area of triangle ABC", "find the area of parallelogram OPQR", "find the exact area of…"
  1. Pick a single vertex and build both vectors from it. For triangle \(XYZ\), use \( \overrightarrow{XY} \) and \( \overrightarrow{XZ} \).
  2. Triangle: \( \tfrac12|\overrightarrow{XY}\times\overrightarrow{XZ}| \). Parallelogram: \( |\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AD}| \).
  3. For a parallelogram you may also use one side and one diagonal: \( |\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}| \) gives the same area.
Micro-example
\( A(1,0,0),B(0,2,0),C(0,0,3) \). \( \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}=(6,3,2) \), magnitude \(7\), so the triangle has area \( \tfrac72 \).
Your words On triangle \(OAC\) you reached for a vector involving \(B\), then caught it: "there ain't no B there, that's my first mistake." Fix: before you cross anything, write down the three letters in the triangle's name and check that both of your vectors are built only from those letters, starting at the same one.
Trap Using \( \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{BC} \) instead of \( \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} \). It happens to give the same area here, but the same slip in a normal-to-a-plane question changes the normal and wrecks everything downstream. Always start both vectors at the same vertex.
11 "Give the geometrical meaning of…"
Trigger "state the geometrical meaning of \(|\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c|\)", "give the geometrical meaning of \(|\mathbf a\times\hat{\mathbf p}|\)"
  1. Check whether the second vector is a unit vector. If it is, the phrasing is clean; if not, you must divide by its magnitude in your description.
  2. Dot form \( |\mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}| \) → "the length of projection of \( \mathbf a \) onto \( \mathbf b \)."
  3. Cross form \( |\mathbf a\times\hat{\mathbf b}| \) → "the perpendicular distance from the point \(A\) to the line through \(O\) with direction \( \mathbf b \)."
  4. Name the actual points from the question, not the letters from the formula.
Trap These are two marks for a sentence, and the sentence has to be specific. "It's the projection" is not enough — say length of projection of what onto what. Likewise for the cross form, name the point and name the line.
12 Cross-product algebra — expand and simplify
Trigger "show that \(\mathbf m\times\mathbf n=(3\lambda^2-4\lambda+2)(\mathbf b\times\mathbf a)\)", "given \(\mathbf a\times3\mathbf b=2\mathbf a\times\mathbf c\), show that \(3\mathbf b-2\mathbf c=\lambda\mathbf a\)"
  1. Expand immediately. The cross product distributes: \( \mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\pm\mathbf c)=\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\pm\mathbf a\times\mathbf c \), and scalars pull straight out.
  2. Kill every self-term: \( \mathbf q\times\mathbf q=\mathbf 0 \).
  3. Use anticommutativity to line up the remaining terms: \( \mathbf b\times\mathbf a=-(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b) \).
  4. If the result is \( \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\mathbf 0 \), conclude \( \mathbf u \parallel \mathbf v \), so \( \mathbf u=\lambda\mathbf v \).
Micro-example
\( \big((1-\lambda)\mathbf p+\lambda\mathbf q\big)\times\mathbf q=(1-\lambda)(\mathbf p\times\mathbf q)+\lambda(\mathbf q\times\mathbf q)=(1-\lambda)(\mathbf p\times\mathbf q) \). Verified symbolically.
Your words "they just expanded it. So next time when you see A, it will just immediately expand." Exactly right, and it is worth making it reflexive: any bracket crossed with something goes straight to expansion, and then you look for the self-term to delete.
Trap Order matters. \( \mathbf a\times\mathbf b=-(\mathbf b\times\mathbf a) \), so if the target answer is written in terms of \( \mathbf b\times\mathbf a \) and you produced \( \mathbf a\times\mathbf b \), you owe a sign flip. In a "show that" this is the difference between full marks and none.

PART C — LINES

13 Find the equation of a line
Trigger "find a vector equation of the line through A and B", "find the equation of the line through P parallel to…"
  1. Get a direction: \( \mathbf d=\overrightarrow{AB}=\mathbf b-\mathbf a \), or copy it from whatever the line is parallel to.
  2. Factor out any common scalar to keep the numbers small.
  3. Get a point: either given point works.
  4. Write \( l:\ \mathbf r=\mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf d,\ \lambda\in\mathbb R \). The "\( \lambda\in\mathbb R \)" is part of the answer.
Micro-example
Through \( A(1,2,3) \) and \( B(3,5,4) \): \( \mathbf d=(2,3,1) \), so \( \mathbf r=(1,2,3)+\lambda(2,3,1),\ \lambda\in\mathbb R \).
Trap The equation is not unique. Using \(B\) as the point, or \(-\mathbf d\) as the direction, gives a different-looking but equally correct answer. Do not panic when your answer does not match the printed one — check instead that the printed point satisfies your equation and the directions are parallel.

A line through the origin has \( \mathbf a=\mathbf 0 \), so \( \mathbf r=\lambda\mathbf d \). The axes are \( \mathbf r=\lambda(1,0,0) \), \( \lambda(0,1,0) \), \( \lambda(0,0,1) \).
14 Convert between vector, parametric and Cartesian form
Trigger "express l in Cartesian form", "the line \(\frac{x-1}{3}=\frac{y+2}{4}=z\)", any line handed to you as equations rather than vectors
  1. Parametric is the bridge. Never jump straight from vector to Cartesian; go through parametric.
  2. Vector → parametric: read off \( x=a_1+\lambda d_1 \), \( y=a_2+\lambda d_2 \), \( z=a_3+\lambda d_3 \).
  3. Parametric → Cartesian: make \( \lambda \) the subject of each row and equate: \( \dfrac{x-a_1}{d_1}=\dfrac{y-a_2}{d_2}=\dfrac{z-a_3}{d_3} \).
  4. Cartesian → vector: force every term into the shape \( \dfrac{x-a}{d} \) first, then read \( \mathbf a \) from the numerators and \( \mathbf d \) from the denominators.
  5. If a component is constant, it has no denominator and is written separately: \( \dfrac{x-1}{3}=\dfrac{y-2}{4},\ z=5 \).
Micro-example (the exact trap you hit)
\( 10-x=\dfrac{y}{2}=-z-1 \). Rewrite: \( 10-x=\dfrac{x-10}{-1} \) and \( -z-1=\dfrac{z+1}{-1} \). So \( \dfrac{x-10}{-1}=\dfrac{y-0}{2}=\dfrac{z-(-1)}{-1} \), giving \( \mathbf r=(10,0,-1)+\lambda(-1,2,-1) \). Verified: this reproduces \( x=10-\lambda \), \( y=2\lambda \), \( z=-1-\lambda \).
Your words "you always have to make sure that there's a denominator… always have to read the standard form." That is the fix, and it is mechanical: before reading anything off, rewrite every term as (variable minus number) over (number). A bare \( 10-x \) is \( \dfrac{x-10}{-1} \), and a bare \( y \) is \( \dfrac{y-0}{1} \).
Trap Reading \( 10-x \) as \( x-10 \) flips the sign of that component of the direction vector. Everything downstream — the angle, the intersection, the foot of perpendicular — comes out wrong, and there is no internal check to catch it.

Also: a line parallel to a coordinate plane has a zero in the matching component of \( \mathbf d \), which means that variable becomes a constant with no denominator. Parallel to the \(y\)–\(z\) plane looks like \( x=a_1,\ \dfrac{y-a_2}{d_2}=\dfrac{z-a_3}{d_3} \).
15 Does a point lie on a line?
Trigger "determine whether the point (…) lies on l", "show that C is on the line AB"
  1. Set the point equal to the general point: \( \mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf d=\overrightarrow{OC} \).
  2. Equate components to get three equations, each giving a value of \( \lambda \).
  3. The point lies on the line only if all three give the SAME \( \lambda \).
  4. If they differ, write the conclusion in words: "since the values of \( \lambda \) obtained from each equation are different, there is no \( \lambda \) satisfying the equation, so the point does not lie on \(l\)."
Trap Checking only two components. Two out of three will often agree by coincidence. All three, or nothing.
16 Relationship between two lines — parallel, intersecting or skew
Trigger "determine whether l₁ and l₂ intersect", "show that the lines are skew", "are the lines coplanar?"
  1. Test parallel FIRST. Is \( \mathbf d_1=k\mathbf d_2 \)? If yes, they are parallel — and then check whether a point of one lies on the other to tell "same line" from "distinct parallel lines." Stop here.
  2. If not parallel, equate the two vector equations: \( \mathbf a_1+\lambda\mathbf d_1=\mathbf a_2+\mu\mathbf d_2 \).
  3. That is three equations in two unknowns. Solve two of them for \( \lambda,\mu \).
  4. Substitute into the third to check consistency. This step is the whole question.
    1. Consistent → the lines intersect. Substitute back into either line to get the point.
    2. Inconsistent → the lines are skew.
  5. On the GC: APPS → 5:PlySmlt2 → 2:SIMULT EQN SOLVER, set 3 equations and 2 unknowns. "NO SOLUTION FOUND" means skew.
Micro-example
\( l_1:\mathbf r=(1,0,0)+\lambda(1,1,0) \), \( l_2:\mathbf r=(0,1,1)+\mu(1,0,1) \). Directions are not parallel. From \(y\): \( \lambda=1 \). From \(z\): \( \mu=-1 \). Check in \(x\): \( 1+1=2 \) but \( 0+\mu=-1 \). Inconsistent, so the lines are skew. Verified: no solution exists.
The vocabulary mark. Lines on the same plane are coplanar. Parallel lines are coplanar; intersecting lines are coplanar; skew lines are non-coplanar. If a stem asks "are they coplanar," it is asking "are they anything other than skew."
Your words "I forgot how to do this already" — then you rebuilt both halves correctly. The half that goes missing is always step 4, the consistency check. Without it you have solved two equations and proved nothing.
Trap Declaring "skew" from an inconsistent system without first ruling out parallel. Two parallel distinct lines also fail to intersect, and they are not skew — they are coplanar. Skew needs both conditions: directions not parallel, and no solution. That is why the parallel test comes first.

Watch the wording too. The notes define intersection by the existence of unique values of \( \lambda \) and \( \mu \). A "show that" answer should say "there are no values of \( \lambda \) and \( \mu \) satisfying all three equations," not just "the equations don't work."
17 Acute angle between two lines
Trigger "find the acute angle between l₁ and l₂", "find the angle l makes with the y-axis"
  1. Take the two direction vectors only. Points are irrelevant.
  2. \( \cos\theta=\dfrac{|\mathbf d_1\cdot\mathbf d_2|}{|\mathbf d_1||\mathbf d_2|} \) — modulus present.

  2. "Parallel not just… it just means a scalar multiple itself." When a stem says parallel, reach for \( \mathbf u=k\mathbf v \) first. A cross product is the slower route to the same conclusion, and on a "show that" it invites algebra errors.
  3. "You always have to make sure that there's a denominator… always have to read the standard form." Every Cartesian line gets rewritten as \( \dfrac{x-a}{d} \) before you read anything off it.
  4. "There ain't no B there, that's my first mistake." Before crossing anything for an area or a normal, check that both vectors are built from the letters actually named in the object, and that both start at the same vertex.
  5. "They just expanded it. So next time when you see A, it will just immediately expand." Brackets in a cross product get expanded on sight, then the self-term is deleted.
  6. "I didn't need to solve for lambda." To show something lies in something, substitute the general point and read off the identity. Solving for the parameter first is wasted time.
  7. Check every normal and every cross product. Dot the result with both inputs; both must be zero. Your notes make the same point: "The accuracy of the answer can be checked by performing scalar product." It costs ten seconds and catches nearly every arithmetic slip in the chapter.
  8. Nothing here is unique. Line equations, plane equations and normals all have infinitely many correct forms. Verify your answer against the conditions; never against the printed answer's appearance.

MARK-LOSER CHECKLIST — read before you hand in

  1. Did I write \( \lambda\in\mathbb R \)? Line and plane equations are incomplete without the parameter's domain.
  2. Modulus check. Is every angle-between-lines, angle-between-planes, line-plane angle, length and distance wearing its bars? Is every angle-between-vectors and every projection vector free of them?
  3. Sine, not cosine, for the line-and-plane angle. The only sine in the chapter.
  4. Did I check consistency in the third equation? Two equations solved is not a proof of intersection.
  5. Did I normalise? \( \hat{\mathbf d} \) and \( \hat{\mathbf n} \), not \( \mathbf d \) and \( \mathbf n \), inside every distance formula.
  6. Two answers or one? Fixed-distance points, parallel planes at a given distance, and "parallel to" directions all come in \( \pm \) pairs.
  7. Did I use the same normal throughout? Especially in same-side tests and distance-between-planes.
  8. Did I answer the species asked? Length or vector. Position vector or coordinates. Exact or 3 s.f.
  9. Did I state the geometrical conclusion in words? "Therefore the lines are skew." "Therefore \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) are collinear, since they share the common point \(A\)." The sentence is often the mark.
  10. Excluded content check. If I have written a triple product \( \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) \) or a skew-line distance, I have gone outside 9758 and misread the question.
