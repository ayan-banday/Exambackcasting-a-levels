Question 1[1]  ·  running total 1 / 80

2-methylbutane, \(\ce{(CH3)2CHCH2CH3}\), is treated with a limited amount of bromine under ultraviolet light. A mixture of mono-brominated products is obtained. Which row gives the correct combination of products? Ignore stereoisomers.

A 5 products, in the ratio 3 : 3 : 3 : 2 : 1
B 4 products, in the ratio 3 : 3 : 3 : 2
C 4 products, in the ratio 6 : 3 : 2 : 1
D 3 products, in the ratio 9 : 2 : 1
[1]

Question 1 — [1]

HCI Prelim / N2025 / P1 / Q19
Answer: C

C — 4 products, in the ratio 6 : 3 : 2 : 1 1

2-methylbutane has four distinct hydrogen environments: the two equivalent \(\ce{CH3}\) groups on C2 (6 H → 1-bromo-2-methylbutane); the terminal \(\ce{CH3}\) on C3 (3 H → 1-bromo-3-methylbutane); the \(\ce{CH2}\) (2 H → 2-bromo-3-methylbutane); and the tertiary \(\ce{C-H}\) (1 H → 2-bromo-2-methylbutane). The statistical ratio by number of equivalent H is therefore 6 : 3 : 2 : 1.

Why each of the other options fails

OptionWhy it is wrong
ACounts the two methyl groups on C2 as giving two different products. They are equivalent, so substitution in either gives the same compound — there are 4 products, not 5.
BSame error as A in the ratio: it treats the two C2 methyls as separate 3 H environments instead of one 6 H environment, and it drops the tertiary C–H product entirely.
CCorrect — 4 products, statistical ratio 6 : 3 : 2 : 1.
D9 primary hydrogens would require three equivalent methyl groups (as in 2,2-dimethylpropane or 2-methylpropane); 2-methylbutane has only three distinct \(\ce{CH3}\) environments totalling 9 H but they are not all equivalent, so they cannot merge into one 9 H product. Only 3 products is also too few.
Trap. Without further information, prediction of proportions in free-radical substitution is based solely on the probability of substituting different H atoms — count equivalent hydrogens first, and only bring in radical stability when the question gives you an observed ratio to explain.

C +CH2–CH(OH)CH3
D CH2(Br)–CH(O−)CH3

HCI Prelim / N2024 / P1 / Q20
Answer: A

A 1 — In electrophilic addition, \(\ce{Br^+}\) adds to the terminal \(\ce{CH2}\) so that the positive charge sits on the more substituted (secondary) carbon, giving the more stable secondary carbocation CH2(Br)–+CHCH3 (Markovnikov). This is then attacked by water or \(\ce{Br^-}\) to give the major product.

OptionWhy it is wrong
ACorrect — the secondary carbocation, stabilised by two electron-donating alkyl groups.
BBr has added to the middle carbon, leaving the charge on the terminal carbon — this is the less stable primary carbocation, which leads to the minor product.
CPuts \(\ce{-OH}\) on before the bromine. Water is the nucleophile in the fast step, never the electrophile that opens the \(\ce{C=C}\); the first species to add in the slow step is always the \(\ce{Br}\) from the polarised \(\ce{Br2}\).
DThis is an alkoxide anion, not a carbocation. No negatively charged carbon-containing intermediate is formed in electrophilic addition.

This question is about the shapes of small molecules and the forces between them.

(a) By considering the electron pairs around the central atom, explain the shapes and the bond angles of \(\ce{BF3}\), \(\ce{NH3}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\). [4]
(b) Draw diagrams showing the polarity of the bonds in \(\ce{BF3}\) and in \(\ce{H2O}\), and hence deduce which of these two molecules has a net dipole moment. [2]
(c) Phosgene, \(\ce{COCl2}\), is a planar molecule. Identify and explain the intermolecular forces that exist between phosgene molecules. [2]
(d) \(\ce{MgCl2}\) melts at 714 °C, whereas \(\ce{SiCl4}\) melts at −68 °C. Explain this difference. [2]

Question 4 — [10]

HCI Soln / N2019 / P3 / Q1(a) & (b) · HCI Soln / N2018 / P2 / Q1(b)(ii) · HCI Soln / N2017 / P3 / Q1(b)(i)
(a) Shapes and bond angles — [4]

The electron pairs arrange themselves as far apart as possible in space, to minimise their mutual repulsion, and lone pair–lone pair repulsion > lone pair–bond pair repulsion > bond pair–bond pair repulsion. 1

The 3 bond pairs and no lone pairs of \(\ce{BF3}\) arrange themselves to minimise electronic repulsion, adopting a trigonal planar shape with a bond angle of 120°. 1

The 4 regions of electron density of \(\ce{NH3}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\) adopt a tetrahedral electron-pair geometry. \(\ce{NH3}\), with 3 bond pairs and 1 lone pair, is trigonal pyramidal with a bond angle of 107° — smaller than 109.5° because lone pair–bond pair repulsion is stronger than bond pair–bond pair repulsion. 1

\(\ce{H2O}\), with 2 bond pairs and 2 lone pairs, has additional lone pair–lone pair repulsion, leading to a bent shape with a smaller angle of 105°. 1

(b) Bond polarity and net dipole — [2]

\(\ce{BF3}\) (trigonal planar, three \(\ce{B->F}\) dipoles) does not have a net dipole moment as the individual dipoles cancel off. 1

\(\ce{H2O}\) (bent) has a net dipole moment as the individual dipoles do not cancel off (there are lone pairs on the central atom). 1

(c) Intermolecular forces in phosgene — [2]

Phosgene is a polar molecule (the electronegativities of O and Cl differ), so it has a permanent dipole; the electrostatic attraction between the oppositely-charged ends of two phosgene molecules gives permanent dipole–permanent dipole (pd-pd) interactions. 1

Temporary fluctuations in electron density give an instantaneous dipole, which induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule, giving id-id (dispersion) forces. 1

Both forces must be named. Dispersion forces exist between all molecules; the pd-pd is the extra one you earn by first proving the molecule is polar.
(d) MgCl₂ vs SiCl₄ — [2]

\(\ce{MgCl2}\) has a giant ionic lattice structure, held together by strong ionic bonding between oppositely charged \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{Cl^-}\) ions. 1

\(\ce{SiCl4}\) has a simple covalent structure, held by weak dispersion forces. A much greater amount of energy is required to overcome the strong ionic bonds in \(\ce{MgCl2}\) than the weak dispersion forces in \(\ce{SiCl4}\), so \(\ce{MgCl2}\) has the higher melting point. 1

(a) Deductions from the four tests — [5]

Comparing \(\ce{C6H8O2}\) with \(\ce{C6H14}\), degree of unsaturation = (14 − 8)/2 = 3. It is likely that I contains \(\ce{C=C}\) and/or \(\ce{C=O}\) and/or a ring. 1

(b) Stereoisomerism in maleic acid — [2]

cis–trans (geometric) isomerism. 1

It arises because there is restricted rotation about the \(\ce{C=C}\) double bond, and each of the two doubly-bonded carbon atoms carries two different groups (a hydrogen atom and a \(\ce{-CO2H}\) group), so the \(\ce{-CO2H}\) groups can be fixed on the same side (cis, maleic) or on opposite sides (trans, fumaric). 1

(c) Why a racemic mixture is optically inactive — [2]

A racemic mixture contains equal amounts (50:50) of the two enantiomers. 1

One enantiomer rotates the plane of plane-polarised light by a given angle clockwise, and the other rotates it by the same angle anticlockwise; the two equal and opposite rotations exactly cancel, so the mixture produces no net rotation and is optically inactive. 1

(d) Homolytic fission and curly arrows — [2]

Homolytic fission is the breaking of a covalent bond such that one electron goes to each of the atoms, forming free radicals (a free radical is a chemical species which contains an unpaired electron). 1

Homolytic fission is represented by half (single-barbed / fishhook) curved arrows, which should always occur in pairs; heterolytic fission is represented by a single full curved arrow, which points towards the more electronegative atom. 1

(b) Why bromine needs a longer wavelength — [2]

In the free radical substitution mechanism, the light provides energy to cleave the halogen–halogen bond. The \(\ce{Br-Br}\) bond, being weaker than the \(\ce{Cl-Cl}\) bond, requires less energy to cleave. 1

Hence cleaving the \(\ce{Br-Br}\) bond only requires lower energy, longer wavelength light. 1

(c) The 1 : 6 : 9 product ratio — [3]

L is produced when the one and only tertiary hydrogen of 3-ethylpentane is substituted. M is produced when one of the 6 secondary hydrogens is substituted. N is produced when one of the 9 primary hydrogens is substituted. 1

The overall ratio is dependent on the probability of substitution and the stability of the radical formed. The probability of substitution is determined by considering the number of equivalent hydrogens available to be substituted. 1

The tertiary free radical produced when the tertiary hydrogen is substituted to form L is significantly more stable than the primary free radical formed to give N. This results in the overall ratio, which forms L preferentially over N despite the probability of substitution. 1

Question 9 — [10]

N2021 / P3 / Q4(b) = HCI Soln / N2021 / P3 / Q4(b) · N2019 / P3 / Q3(c)(iii) · HCI Soln / N2021 / P1 / Q16
(a) The mechanism — [4]

Name of mechanism: electrophilic addition; the first step must be labelled slow and the second fast. 1

Slow step: partial charges shown on Hδ+–Brδ−; one curly arrow from the \(\ce{C=C}\) π bond to the δ+ H, and a second curly arrow from the \(\ce{H-Br}\) bond to Br. 1

Intermediate drawn correctly: the benzylic secondary carbocation C6H5–+CH–CH3, together with \(\ce{:Br^-}\) (lone pairs shown). 1

Fast step: a curly arrow from a lone pair on \(\ce{Br^-}\) to the positively charged carbon (to the carbon, not to the "+"), giving \(\ce{C6H5CHBrCH3}\). 1

(b) Why this is the major product — [3]

A benzylic cation is more stable as the positive charge can delocalise into the benzene ring. 1

In the slow step, the empty p-orbital of the positively charged carbon overlaps with the π electron cloud of benzene, allowing the π electrons of benzene to delocalise onto the empty p orbital of the positively charged carbon, dispersing the positive charge. 1

Hence the more stable 2° carbocation is formed instead of the less stable 1° carbocation. The 2° carbocation then reacts with \(\ce{Br^-}\) in the fast step to form the major product. 1

(c) Why a 50 : 50 mixture of enantiomers — [2]

In the slow step of the electrophilic addition of phenylethene, a planar carbocation is formed. 1

In the fast step, the bromide ions can attack the planar carbocation centre from above and below the plane with equal probability, forming a 50:50 mixture of two enantiomers. 1

The structure of the carbocation should be shown in your answer — the examiner wants to see that you know which carbon is planar, not just the phrase "attacked from both sides".
(d) π bond vs σ bond — [1]

The carbon–carbon π bond is donated to the electrophile because the π bond is weaker than the σ bond (due to less effective side-on orbital overlap) and is more easily broken. The electrons are donated from the \(\ce{C=C}\), which makes the \(\ce{C=C}\) the nucleophile. 1

(a) Order of increasing rate of hydrolysis — [3]

The relative rate increases from O to M to N. 1

All three are primary halogenoalkanes undergoing nucleophilic substitution via SN2. The \(\ce{C-I}\) bond in N is weaker than the \(\ce{C-Br}\) bond in M and O, so N has a lower activation energy and the fastest rate. 1

The highly branched alkyl group in O is bulkier than in M, causing greater steric hindrance for nucleophilic attack, and hence the slowest rate. Hence O < M < N. 1

Whether the mechanism is SN1 or SN2, breaking of the \(\ce{C-X}\) bond occurs in the rate-determining step — so the stronger the \(\ce{C-X}\) bond, the slower the rate. Quote the bond energies from the data if you want the point beyond doubt: \(\ce{C-I}\) +238 < \(\ce{C-Br}\) +280 < \(\ce{C-Cl}\) +340 kJ mol\(^{-1}\).

(c) Why iodobenzene does not react — [2]

Halogenoarenes are not reactive towards nucleophilic substitution because of the delocalisation of the lone pair on X into the benzene ring, giving partial double-bond character to the \(\ce{C-X}\) bond, which is therefore stronger and harder to break. 1

There is also steric hindrance — the ring blocks the approach of the nucleophile to the carbon bearing the halogen. 1

Two traps sit next to this one. A compound giving no precipitate is not necessarily a halogenoarene — a fluoroalkane will also not react because the \(\ce{C-F}\) bond is very strong. And an acyl chloride reacts immediately at room temperature, without needing NaOH first.

(a) Why phenols are more acidic than alcohols — [2]

When phenol loses its \(\ce{H+}\), the resulting phenoxide ion's negative charge on oxygen is delocalised into the benzene ring (the O lone pair overlaps with the ring π system), spreading and stabilising the charge, so the phenoxide ion is more stable and the equilibrium of dissociation lies further to the right. 1

In ethoxide the negative charge is localised on oxygen and the ethyl group is electron-donating, destabilising it. Hence phenol is a stronger acid than ethanol. 1

Compare like with like — the comparison is phenoxide vs alkoxide, never "phenol vs alkoxide". And the alkyl group is electron-donating: it pushes charge onto the O and makes the anion worse, which is why alcohols are the weaker acids.
(b) Position of R — [2]

\(\Delta M_r\) = 335.8 − 178.0 = 157.8 = 2 × 78.9, so two bromine atoms are substituted into the ring. 1

Phenol directs bromination to positions ortho and para to \(\ce{-OH}\) (positions 2, 4 and 6). Since only two (not three) Br are introduced, one of these activated positions must already be occupied by R; therefore R is at position 2 or 4 (ortho or para to \(\ce{-OH}\)), not position 3. 1

Aqueous bromine, room temperature, no catalyst is the phenol reagent — the ring is activated because the O lone pair is donated into it. That is a different reagent from \(\ce{Br2}\) in \(\ce{CCl4}\) in the dark, which is the test for a \(\ce{C=C}\) by electrophilic addition. Substituting \(\ce{Br}\) costs \(M_r\) 78.9 each (79.9 in, 1.0 H out); adding \(\ce{Br2}\) across a \(\ce{C=C}\) would cost 159.8.
(c) Structure of Q and the yellow precipitate — [2]

Q = \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\) (ethanol) — it contains the \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}\) unit required for the tri-iodomethane test, and the remaining fragment appears as methanoate, giving \(\ce{HCO2H}\) on acidification. 1

It gives a positive tri-iodomethane (iodoform) test — the yellow precipitate is \(\ce{CHI3}\), tri-iodomethane. 1

(d) Comparing acidity with sodium — [1]

Record the time taken for a fixed volume (e.g. 10 cm\(^3\)) of \(\ce{H2}\) gas to be collected for each compound when Na metal is added. Since acidity increases from ethanol to benzene-1,4-diol to phenol, the time taken is shortest for phenol and longest for ethanol (rate fastest for phenol, slowest for ethanol). 1

You cannot differentiate them by the volume of gas collected — equal amounts of each give the same volume of \(\ce{H2}\). The measurement has to be a rate.
