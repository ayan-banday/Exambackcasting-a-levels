(a) Shapes and bond angles of BF₃, NH₃ and H₂O — [3]
By considering the electron pairs around the central atom, explain the shapes and bond angles of \(\ce{BF3}\), \(\ce{NH3}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\).

The 3 bond pairs (and no lone pairs) of \(\ce{BF3}\) arrange themselves to minimise electronic repulsion, adopting a trigonal planar shape with a bond angle of 120°. 1

The 4 regions of electron density of \(\ce{NH3}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\) adopt a tetrahedral electron-pair geometry. \(\ce{NH3}\), with 3 bond pairs and 1 lone pair, has a bond angle of 107° (smaller than 109.5°) due to lone pair–bond pair repulsion being stronger than bond pair–bond pair repulsion. 1

\(\ce{H2O}\), with 2 bond pairs and 2 lone pairs, has additional lone pair–lone pair repulsion (which is stronger than lone pair–bond pair, which is stronger than bond pair–bond pair), leading to a smaller angle of 105°. 1

(b) Bond polarity and net dipole moment — [3]
Draw diagrams showing the polarity of the bonds in each of the three molecules, and deduce whether each has a net dipole moment.

\(\ce{BF3}\) is trigonal planar with three \(\ce{B->F}\) dipoles. It does not have a net dipole moment as the individual dipoles cancel off. 1

\(\ce{NH3}\) is trigonal pyramidal and has a net dipole moment, as the individual dipoles do not cancel off. 1

\(\ce{H2O}\) is bent and has a net dipole moment, as the individual dipoles do not cancel off. In each case the lone pair(s) on the central atom prevent the symmetrical cancellation seen in \(\ce{BF3}\). 1

Draw the dipole arrows on the actual shape. A dipole argument that never shows the geometry cannot earn the cancellation mark, because cancellation is a statement about the arrangement in space, not about the bonds alone.

\(\ce{NH3}\) is the Brønsted base (\(\ce{H+}\) acceptor) and \(\ce{H2O}\) is the Brønsted acid (\(\ce{H+}\) donor). 1

Question 2 — [9]

(a) HCI Soln / N2018 / P3 / Q3(d) · (b) HCI Prelim / N2021 / P2 / Q2(c) · (c) HCI Soln / N2021 / P2 / Q2(b) = RI TYS / N2021 / P2 / Q2
(a) Order of melting point — [4]
Arrange propanoic acid, glycine, pentane and butylamine in order of increasing melting point and explain the order.

The order of increasing melting point is pentane < butylamine < propanoic acid < glycine. 1

Pentane is a non-polar molecule with only weak dispersion forces, so the least energy is required to break its intermolecular forces and it has the lowest melting point. 1

Glycine exists as zwitterions in the solid state; the strong ionic bonding between zwitterions requires the most energy to break, so it has the highest melting point. 1

Both butylamine and propanoic acid have hydrogen bonding, which is stronger than dispersion but weaker than ionic bonding. The hydrogen bonding in propanoic acid is stronger than in butylamine because O is more electronegative than N, so more energy is needed for propanoic acid. 1

(b) Nitrobenzene against water — [2]
Explain why nitrobenzene has a higher boiling point than water.

Nitrobenzene has a larger electron cloud / more electrons than water (each nitrobenzene molecule has 64 electrons against 10 for water). 1

More energy is required to break the stronger dispersion forces between nitrobenzene molecules than the hydrogen bonding between water molecules. 1

Hydrogen bonding does not automatically win. Nitrobenzene forms no hydrogen bonds at all, yet still boils higher, because a sixfold difference in electron count outweighs the hydrogen bonding in water. Compare the electron clouds first, then the type of force.
(c) The two lattice-energy factors and which dominates — [3]
State the two factors that affect the numerical magnitude of a lattice energy, and use the interionic distances of \(\ce{NaCl}\) (0.276 nm), \(\ce{NaF}\) (0.231 nm) and \(\ce{MgO}\) (0.205 nm) to deduce which factor has the greater effect.

The two factors are the interionic distance and the product of the cationic and anionic charge. 1

The interionic distances of \(\ce{NaCl}\), \(\ce{NaF}\) and \(\ce{MgO}\) are 0.276, 0.231 and 0.205 nm respectively, so the magnitude of lattice energy increases in this order. However, the increase is greatest from \(\ce{NaF}\) to \(\ce{MgO}\), where the ionic charges of \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{O^2-}\) double compared to \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{F-}\), and the magnitude more than quadruples, while only a smaller increase is seen from \(\ce{NaCl}\) to \(\ce{NaF}\). 1

This shows the product of the ionic charges has the greater effect on the numerical magnitude of lattice energy. 1

(a) Hybridisation and bonding in ketene — [3]
State the hybridisation of \(\ce{C_a}\), \(\ce{C_b}\) and \(\ce{O}\) in \(\ce{H2C_a=C_b=O}\), and describe how each \(\sigma\) and each \(\pi\) bond is formed in terms of orbital overlap.

\(\ce{C_a}\) and \(\ce{O}\) are sp\(^2\) hybridised while \(\ce{C_b}\) is sp hybridised (\(\ce{C_a}\) has 3 electron groups, \(\ce{C_b}\) has 2). 1

Each \(\ce{C_a-H}\) \(\sigma\) bond is formed from the head-on overlap of the sp\(^2\) hybrid orbital of \(\ce{C_a}\) with the 1s orbital of H. The \(\ce{C_a-C_b}\) \(\sigma\) bond is formed from head-on overlap of the sp\(^2\) hybrid orbital of \(\ce{C_a}\) with the sp hybrid orbital of \(\ce{C_b}\), and the \(\ce{C_b-O}\) \(\sigma\) bond from head-on overlap of the sp hybrid orbital of \(\ce{C_b}\) with the sp\(^2\) hybrid orbital of O. 1

The \(\ce{C_a-C_b}\) and \(\ce{C_b-O}\) \(\pi\) bonds are formed from side-on overlap of the unhybridised p orbitals of \(\ce{C_a}\) and \(\ce{C_b}\), and of \(\ce{C_b}\) and O respectively. 1

(b) Isomerism and bond angle in diazene — [3]
Explain why \(\ce{HN=NH}\) can exist as two isomers, state how the two isomers differ, and predict the \(\ce{H-N=N}\) bond angle.

Each N of the \(\ce{N=N}\) double bond possesses one lone pair and one hydrogen atom. Diazene exists as cis-trans isomers due to the restricted rotation around the \(\ce{N=N}\) bond and each N bearing 2 different groups (a H atom and a lone pair of electrons). 1

Two non-superimposable isomers arise as the two H atoms can be on the same side (cis) or on opposite sides (trans) of the \(\ce{N=N}\). 1

With 3 regions of electron density (one \(\ce{N-H}\) single bond, one \(\ce{N=N}\) double bond and 1 lone pair), the \(\ce{H-N=N}\) bond angle is just under 120° (quoted as 117–118° due to bond pair – lone pair repulsion). 1

A lone pair counts as one of the "two different groups". Diazene is the standard trap because neither nitrogen carries two visible substituents. If you only look at atoms, you will wrongly conclude that no cis-trans isomerism is possible.
(c) Enantiomerism in penta-2,3-diene — [1]
Explain why penta-2,3-diene, \(\ce{CH3CH=C=CHCH3}\), can exhibit enantiomerism.

In the \(\ce{C=C=C}\) allene unit the two \(\pi\) bonds are mutually perpendicular, so the two end (sp\(^2\)) carbons and their substituents lie in two perpendicular planes. Each end carbon carries two different groups (H and \(\ce{CH3}\)), so the rigid molecule has no plane of symmetry and is non-superimposable on its mirror image — hence it exists as a pair of enantiomers, despite having no chiral carbon. 1 INFERRED — no official key

(d) Rotation in intermediate X, and the racemic outcome — [2]
Explain why it is easier to rotate the \(\ce{C_y-C_z}\) bond in intermediate X than in enantiomer 1, and why step 2 is likely to form a racemic mixture.

In enantiomer 1 the \(\ce{C_y-C_z}\) bond is a full \(\ce{C=C}\) double bond (\(\sigma\) + \(\pi\)); rotation is restricted because it would require breaking the strong \(\pi\) bond. In intermediate X the two remaining \(\pi\) electrons are delocalised over \(\ce{C_x}\), \(\ce{C_y}\) and \(\ce{C_z}\), so the \(\ce{C_y-C_z}\) bond has only partial double-bond character (lower bond order, more single-bond-like) and can rotate much more easily. 1

In step 2, after rotation the \(\ce{CH3}\) on \(\ce{C_z}\) can re-form the allene pointing to either side with equal probability, so enantiomer 1 and enantiomer 2 are produced in equal (50:50) amounts — a racemic mixture. 1 INFERRED — no official key

(a) Mechanism for ICl and 1-phenylpropene — [3]
Name the mechanism and draw it, showing curly arrows, partial charges, lone pairs and the intermediate, with the slow and fast steps labelled.

The mechanism is electrophilic addition. 1

Slow step: a curly arrow runs from the \(\ce{C=C}\) \(\pi\) bond of 1-phenylpropene to the \(\delta+\) I of I–Cl (drawn δ+I–Clδ−), and a second curly arrow runs from the I–Cl bond to Cl. This forms the benzylic carbocation \(\ce{C6H5-C^+(H)-CH(I)-CH3}\) together with \(\ce{Cl^-}\), and the step is labelled "slow". 1

Fast step: a curly arrow runs from a lone pair on \(\ce{Cl-}\) to the positively charged carbon, giving the product with Cl and I on adjacent carbons. 1

The arrow must start on a bond or a lone pair and end on the atom, not on the "+" sign. Marks are also routinely lost for omitting the induced partial charges on I–Cl and for not writing "slow" against the first step.
(b) Which carbocation is preferred — [2]
Two carbocation intermediates are possible. State which is preferred and explain.

The benzylic carbocation, with the positive charge on the carbon next to the ring, is more stable than the alternative and hence is the preferred carbocation. 1

The empty p orbital of the carbocation centre overlaps with the delocalised \(\pi\) electron cloud of the benzene ring, so electrons from the benzene ring delocalise to the carbocation centre. Hence the positive charge can be dispersed into the benzene ring, stabilising it further. 1

(c) Reaction carried out in water — [1]
Explain what additional product forms if the reaction is carried out in water.

The lone pairs of electrons on O in \(\ce{H2O}\) act as a competing nucleophile in the second step to attack the carbocation intermediate. A proton is lost subsequently to form the compound with an \(\ce{-OH}\) group, with \(\ce{H3O+}\) as the by-product. 1

(d) Oxidative cleavage of L and M — [4]
State the observations when L and M are treated with \(\ce{KMnO4}\), \(\ce{H2SO4}\)(aq), heat, and give the organic products.

L: the purple \(\ce{KMnO4}\) decolourises, and effervescence is observed; the gas forms a white precipitate in limewater. 1

L + 7[O] \(\rightarrow\) 2\(\ce{(CH3)2C=O}\) (propanone) + 2\(\ce{CO2}\) + \(\ce{H2O}\) 1

M: the purple \(\ce{KMnO4}\) decolourises, but no gas is evolved, because M has no terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\). 1

M + 3[O] \(\rightarrow\) a ketone + a carboxylic acid, with no \(\ce{CO2}\). 1

Effervescence is the diagnostic, not the decolourisation. Every alkene decolourises hot acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\). Only a terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) is cleaved all the way to \(\ce{CO2}\), so the limewater test is what separates L from M.

(a) Why the two bromoalkanes react by different mechanisms — [2]
Explain why 1-bromobutane and 2-bromo-2-methylpropane react with \(\ce{OH-}\) by different mechanisms.

1-bromobutane undergoes SN2: it has fewer alkyl groups attached to the electron-deficient carbon, so the attack by \(\ce{OH-}\) is less sterically hindered. It does not undergo SN1 as a primary carbocation is unstable. 1

2-bromo-2-methylpropane undergoes SN1: the alkyl groups are electron-donating, so it forms a more stable tertiary carbocation. In SN1 the \(\ce{C-Br}\) bond breaks in the slow step to form a carbocation, and the electron-donating alkyl groups disperse the positive charge. 1

(b) Deducing E from the kinetics — [2]
When E reacts with \(\ce{NaOH}\)(aq), the rate is independent of \(\ce{[OH^-]}\). Deduce whether E is the primary or the secondary chloroalkane, and explain.

Since the rate is independent of \(\ce{[OH^-]}\), the nucleophilic substitution occurs via an SN1 mechanism, in which the rate-determining step involves only the halogenoalkane. 1

Hence E is the secondary chloroalkane, \(\ce{C6H5CHClCH3}\), because the secondary (benzylic) carbocation is more stable than a primary carbocation. F is then \(\ce{C6H5CH(OH)CH3}\). 1

Rate law first, mechanism second, structure third. SN1 gives rate = \(k\ce{[RX]}\) (first order overall); SN2 gives rate = \(k\ce{[RX][Nu]}\) (second order overall). Never name the mechanism without naming the order it implies.
(c) Order of increasing rate of hydrolysis — [3]
Arrange M, N and O in order of increasing rate of hydrolysis and explain the order.

The relative rate increases from O to M to N, that is O < M < N. All three are primary halogenoalkanes undergoing nucleophilic substitution via SN2. 1

The \(\ce{C-I}\) bond in N is weaker than the \(\ce{C-Br}\) bond in M and O, so N has a lower activation energy and the fastest rate. 1

The highly branched alkyl group in O is bulkier than in M, causing greater steric hindrance for nucleophilic attack, so O has the slowest rate. 1

Bond energy, not electronegativity. The rate of hydrolysis follows the \(\ce{C-X}\) bond energy because that bond breaks in the rate-determining step, so \(\ce{R-I > R-Br > R-Cl}\). Arguing from electronegativity gives the order backwards.

Record the time taken for a fixed volume (for example 10 cm\(^3\)) of \(\ce{H2}\) gas to be collected for each compound when Na metal is added. 1

Since acidity increases from ethanol to hydroquinone to phenol, the time taken is shortest for phenol and longest for ethanol — that is, the rate is fastest for phenol and slowest for ethanol. 1

The electron-donating alkyl group intensifies the negative charge on the oxygen of the ethoxide ion, destabilising the conjugate base, so ethanol is the weakest acid. 1

Both phenoxides have resonance stabilisation due to delocalisation of the negative charge into the benzene ring, so phenol and hydroquinone are stronger acids than ethanol. 1

However, hydroquinone has an additional electron-donating \(\ce{OH}\) group on the ring, which intensifies the negative charge on the O and destabilises its conjugate base; hence phenol is a stronger acid than hydroquinone and gives the greatest rate of \(\ce{H2}\) production. 1

You cannot differentiate ethanol from phenol by the volume of gas collected, as both give the same volume: \(\tfrac{0.010}{2} \times 24000 = 120\) cm\(^3\), so collecting 10 cm\(^3\) is feasible.

Judge the conjugate base, never the acid. An electron-donating group intensifies the negative charge on the alkoxide or phenoxide, destabilises it, and makes the parent a weaker acid. An electron-withdrawing group disperses the charge, stabilises the base, and makes the parent a stronger acid.
