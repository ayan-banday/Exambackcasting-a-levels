(a) The two lattice-energy factors — [3]

The two factors are the interionic distance and the product of the cationic and anionic charge. Lattice energy magnitude \(\propto |q^+ \times q^-| / (r^+ + r^-)\). 1

\(\ce{Mg^2+}\) has a higher charge (+2 against +1) and a smaller ionic radius (0.065 nm against 0.095 nm) than \(\ce{Na+}\). Substituting into the expression, \(\ce{MgCl2}\) therefore has the larger magnitude of lattice energy. 1

More energy is required to overcome the stronger electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely charged ions in the giant ionic lattice of \(\ce{MgCl2}\). 1

The wording that costs the mark. Say "larger magnitude of lattice energy", never "larger lattice energy" — lattice energy is negative, so "larger" is ambiguous and examiners penalise it. And quote the actual charges and the Data Booklet radii and put them into the expression. Asserting "bigger charge, smaller radius" without the numbers is a partial answer.
(b) Why AlCl₃ is covalent — [3]

\(\ce{Al^3+}\) has a very high charge density (high charge, +3, and a small ionic radius, 0.050 nm), and therefore a high polarising power. 1

It distorts / polarises the electron cloud of the polarisable \(\ce{Cl-}\) anion. The electron density is drawn into the region between the two nuclei, giving effective overlap of the electron clouds — that is, a covalent bond. 1

\(\ce{AlBr3}\) has stronger covalent character than \(\ce{AlCl3}\), because \(\ce{Br-}\) is larger (0.195 nm against 0.181 nm) and its electron cloud is therefore more easily polarised by the \(\ce{Al^3+}\) cation. 1

Say "overlap of electron clouds", not "overlap of orbitals". Polarisation is a distortion of a charge cloud, not orbital hybridisation.
(c) Al₂Cl₆ and electrical conductivity — [3]

Two \(\ce{AlCl3}\) units joined by two chlorine bridges. Each Al is bonded to two terminal Cl by normal covalent bonds and to two bridging Cl; of each pair of bridging bonds, one is a normal covalent bond and the other is a dative (co-ordinate) bond. Every Cl shows its three non-bonding lone pairs and all atoms attain an octet. 1

The dative bond forms because a lone pair on a bridging Cl of one \(\ce{AlCl3}\) is donated into the empty (vacant) orbital of the electron-deficient Al of the other \(\ce{AlCl3}\) — Al has only six electrons in its valence shell and can accept two more. 1

Molten \(\ce{MgCl2}\) has free, mobile ions that act as mobile charge carriers, so it conducts. \(\ce{AlCl3}\) exists as discrete, neutral \(\ce{Al2Cl6}\) molecules with no mobile ions, so it cannot conduct. 1

(a) Melting-point order — [4]

Order of increasing melting point: pentane < butylamine < propanoic acid < glycine. 1

Pentane is a non-polar molecule with only weak dispersion forces, so the least energy is required to break its intermolecular forces — lowest melting point. 1

Both butylamine and propanoic acid have hydrogen bonding, which is stronger than dispersion but weaker than ionic bonding. The hydrogen bonding in propanoic acid is stronger than in butylamine because O is more electronegative than N, so more energy is needed for propanoic acid. 1

Glycine exists as zwitterions in the solid state; the strong ionic bonding between the zwitterions requires the most energy to break — highest melting point. 1

(b) Pentane vs 2,2-dimethylpropane — [2]

Both are simple non-polar molecules with the same number of electrons, so only instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (dispersion) forces act between them and the electron cloud size is the same. 1

The straight-chain pentane has a greater surface area of contact between adjacent molecules and therefore more extensive dispersion forces than the branched \(\ce{C(CH3)4}\), so pentane has the higher boiling point. 1

The direction you have got backwards before. More branching makes the molecule more compact, so less surface is in contact with a neighbour, so the dispersion forces are weaker and the boiling point is lower. The quantity that matters is the surface area of contact between adjacent molecules, not surface area in isolation. Verbatim line to reproduce: "the larger the surface area of contact between adjacent molecules, the more easily induced dipoles are formed, the stronger the dispersion forces."
(c) Nitrobenzene vs water — [2]

Nitrobenzene has a much larger electron cloud / more electrons than water (64 electrons per molecule against 10). 1

More energy is therefore required to break the stronger dispersion forces between nitrobenzene molecules than the hydrogen bonding between water molecules, so nitrobenzene has the higher boiling point. 1

The pattern-match this is built to punish. "Hydrogen bonding beats dispersion" is only true when the electron clouds are comparable. A big enough electron cloud makes dispersion the dominant force — which is why \(\ce{I2}\) is a solid and \(\ce{H2O}\) is a liquid. Always compare number of electrons first, then force type.

The mark that gets dropped. You must account for the O atom already inside N — the coefficient of \(\ce{O2}\) is \((x + y/4 - \tfrac12)\), not \((x + y/4)\). Use \((x + y/4)\) and you get the alkane answer and lose the last two marks.
(b) Identifying M — [4]

\(\ce{M(NO3)2 -> MO + 2NO2 + 1/2 O2}\); then \(\ce{MO + 2HCl -> MCl2 + H2O}\), and the excess acid is neutralised by \(\ce{HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O}\). 1

\(M_r(\ce{M(NO3)2})\) = 0.118 / 0.0005575 = 211.7, so \(A_r\)(M) = 211.7 − 2(14.0 + 3 × 16.0) = 211.7 − 124.0 = 87.7. M is strontium, Sr. 1

Three places this bites. Writing \(\ce{MNO3}\) instead of \(\ce{M(NO3)2}\); forgetting that the oxide MO takes two HCl per mole, so \(n(\ce{MO})\) is half the acid consumed; and subtracting \(A_r(\ce{NO3})\) once instead of twice at the end. In any back titration write three separate lines — total added, amount found by the titration, and the difference.

The mole ratio required is \(\ce{MnO4^- : C2O4^2-}\) = 2 : 5, so 0.000250 mol of \(\ce{MnO4^-}\) needs 0.000625 mol of ethanedioate, and only 0.000250 mol is present. Hence \(\ce{C2O4^2-}\) is the limiting reagent. 1

Mole ratio \(\ce{C2O4^2- : CO2}\) = 5 : 10, so \(n(\ce{CO2})\) = 0.000250 / 5 × 10 = 0.000500 mol. At s.t.p., \(V\) = 0.000500 × 22.7 = 0.01135 dm\(^3\) = 11.4 cm\(^3\). 1

Ethanedioate always oxidises to \(\ce{CO2}\) — C goes from +3 in \(\ce{C2O4^2-}\) to +4 in \(\ce{CO2}\), a change of +1 per carbon × 2 carbons = 2 electrons released per ion. Build every half-equation the same way: (i) balance the redox electrons using the oxidation-number change, (ii) balance charge with \(\ce{H+}\), (iii) balance O with \(\ce{H2O}\).
(b) Final oxidation state of Mn — [3]

\(n(\ce{FeC2O4})\) = 0.020 × 0.020 = 4.0 × 10\(^{-4}\) mol. Each unit loses 1 electron from \(\ce{Fe^2+ -> Fe^3+}\) plus 2 electrons from \(\ce{C2O4^2- -> 2CO2}\), i.e. 3 electrons, so total electrons lost = 3 × 4.0 × 10\(^{-4}\) = 1.2 × 10\(^{-3}\) mol. 1

\(n(\ce{MnO4^-})\) = 0.015 × 0.020 = 3.0 × 10\(^{-4}\) mol, so electrons gained per Mn = 1.2 × 10\(^{-3}\) / 3.0 × 10\(^{-4}\) = 4. 1

Mn is +7 in \(\ce{MnO4^-}\), so the final oxidation state is 7 − 4 = +3. 1

The trap is the word "both". The reflex is to count only the \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) (1 electron) and land on \(\ce{Mn^2+}\), the answer you have written a hundred times. Count the ethanedioate as well — 3 electrons per formula unit — and the manganese does not reach +2 at all.

Disproportionation — chlorine (oxidation state 0 in \(\ce{Cl2}\)) is simultaneously reduced to −1 (in \(\ce{NaCl}\)) and oxidised to +5 (in \(\ce{NaClO3}\)). 1

\(\ce{KMnO4}\) is self-indicating: the purple \(\ce{MnO4^-}\) is decolourised to a very pale pink \(\ce{Mn^2+}\), and the end point is the first permanent pale pink produced by one drop of excess \(\ce{KMnO4}\). 1

You must say "self-indicating" and "pale pink". "Purple" at the end point is wrong — a full purple means you have gone well past the end point.

Question 5 — [8]

HCI Prelim / N2023 / P2 / Q4(a)(ii)  ·  HCI Prelim / N2023 / P2 / Q4(c)(ii)  ·  HCI Soln / N2016 / P1 / Q37 (converted from MCQ)  ·  HCI Soln / N2022 / P1 / Q24 = RI TYS / N2022 / P1 / Q24
(a) Enantiomerism in penta-2,3-diene — [2]

In the \(\ce{C=C=C}\) allene unit the two \(\pi\) bonds are mutually perpendicular, because the central sp carbon keeps two unhybridised p orbitals that are perpendicular to each other and each overlaps side-on with a p orbital on one of the terminal sp\(^2\) carbons. The two end carbons and their substituents therefore lie in two perpendicular planes. 1

Each end carbon carries two different groups (H and \(\ce{CH3}\)), so the rigid molecule has no plane of symmetry and is non-superimposable on its mirror image — hence it exists as a pair of enantiomers, despite having no chiral carbon. 1

(b) Rotation in X and the racemic product — [2]

In the enantiomer the \(\ce{C_y-C_z}\) bond is a full \(\ce{C=C}\) double bond (\(\sigma\) + \(\pi\)); rotation is restricted because it would require breaking the strong \(\pi\) bond. In intermediate X the two remaining \(\pi\) electrons are delocalised over \(\ce{C_x}\), \(\ce{C_y}\) and \(\ce{C_z}\), so the \(\ce{C_y-C_z}\) bond has only partial double-bond character (lower bond order, more single-bond-like) and can rotate much more easily. 1

After rotation, the \(\ce{CH3}\) on \(\ce{C_z}\) can re-form the allene pointing to either side with equal probability, so enantiomer 1 and enantiomer 2 are produced in equal (50:50) amounts — a racemic mixture. 1

(c) Minimum carbon count — [2]

Five carbon atoms is not possible. The three \(\ce{C5H10O}\) ketones are pentan-2-one \(\ce{CH3COCH2CH2CH3}\), pentan-3-one \(\ce{CH3CH2COCH2CH3}\) and 3-methylbutan-2-one \(\ce{CH3COCH(CH3)CH3}\); none of them has a carbon bonded to four different atoms or groups (in 3-methylbutan-2-one the relevant carbon carries two identical \(\ce{CH3}\) groups). 1

The minimum is six carbon atoms, for example 3-methylpentan-2-one, \(\ce{CH3COCH(CH3)CH2CH3}\), in which C3 carries \(\ce{CH3}\), H, \(\ce{COCH3}\) and \(\ce{CH2CH3}\) — four different groups, hence a chiral centre. 1

(d) Two chiral carbons, no optical activity — [2]

The molecule is a meso compound: it has two chiral carbons but also an internal plane of symmetry, so one half is the mirror image of the other and the molecule is superimposable on its own mirror image. The rotations caused by the two halves are equal and opposite and cancel within the single molecule. 1

The correct general criterion is therefore that a molecule is optically active only if it is non-superimposable on its mirror image — equivalently, only if it has no plane of symmetry. "A carbon bonded to four different groups" is the common cause of chirality, not the definition of it. 1

This question and part (a) are the two halves of the same trap. Part (a) is a chiral molecule with no chiral carbon; part (d) is an achiral molecule with two chiral carbons. Counting chiral carbons answers neither. Test for a plane of symmetry, and if there are two stereocentres, redraw the molecule rotated about the central C–C bond before you decide.

Propagation:   (a) CH₃CH₂CH₃ + Br• → CH₃ĊHCH₃ + HBr     (b) CH₃ĊHCH₃ + Br₂ → CH₃CHBrCH₃ + Br•    then (a), (b), (a), (b), … 1

Termination:   2 Br• → Br₂     CH₃ĊHCH₃ + Br• → CH₃CHBrCH₃     2 CH₃ĊHCH₃ → (CH₃)₂CHCH(CH₃)₂ 1

Examiner's common errors on this exact question. The product asked for is 2-bromopropane, not 1-bromopropane, so the radical you abstract must be the secondary hydrogen. You must name the mechanism and name every stage, include the second propagation step (it regenerates the radical), and give all three termination equations — every termination step is the collision of two radicals.
(b) Monobromo products of methylcyclohexane — [3]

There are five constitutionally isomeric monosubstituted products: substitution on the \(\ce{CH3}\) group, and at ring carbons C1 (the one bearing the methyl), C2/C6, C3/C5 and C4. 1

Exactly two of them are chiral: 1-bromo-2-methylcyclohexane and 1-bromo-3-methylcyclohexane. 1

Each of those two has two stereocentres and no internal mirror plane, so each is non-superimposable on its mirror image. The other three are achiral: in (bromomethyl)cyclohexane no carbon carries four different groups; in 1-bromo-1-methylcyclohexane and 1-bromo-4-methylcyclohexane the two ring arms leading away from the substituted carbon are identical, so there is an internal plane of symmetry. 1

Ring compounds are the place people over-count. Two ring positions are the same environment whenever the two paths round the ring to them are equivalent — so C2 and C6 give one product, not two, and C3 and C5 give one product, not two. Substitution on the \(\ce{CH3}\) gives \(\ce{HX}\), never \(\ce{H2}\).
(c) Which product predominates — [2]

1-bromo-1-methylcyclohexane is expected in greatest yield, formed by substituting the single tertiary hydrogen at C1, because the tertiary free radical produced is significantly more stable than a secondary or primary radical. 1

The overall ratio is dependent on both the probability of substitution and the stability of the radical formed. The probability of substitution is determined by the number of equivalent hydrogens available to be substituted; on that count alone the primary product would dominate. The greater stability of the tertiary radical means the tertiary product forms preferentially despite the probability of substitution. 1

Read the question before you pick your tool. When the paper says "ignore any other information" or gives you no observed ratio, prediction rests on the probability of substituting different H atoms alone. When the paper asks you to explain an observed ratio or to predict the major product, radical stability (3° > 2° > 1°) enters and can overturn the head-count.

(a) But-1-ene with bromine in methanol — [4]

Name of mechanism: electrophilic addition. Slow step: a curly arrow from the \(\ce{C=C}\) \(\pi\) bond to the \(\delta+\) Br of \(\ce{Br2}\) (drawn \(^{\delta+}\)Br–Br\(^{\delta-}\)), and a second curly arrow from the \(\ce{Br-Br}\) bond to the far Br; this forms the secondary carbocation CH3CH2–+CH–CH2Br together with \(\ce{:Br^-}\), and is labelled slow. 1

Fast step, route 1: a curly arrow from a lone pair on \(\ce{Br^-}\) to the positively charged carbon gives 1,2-dibromobutane — the minor product. 1

Fast step, route 2: a lone pair on the O of \(\ce{CH3OH}\) attacks the secondary carbocation; the oxonium ion then loses \(\ce{H+}\) (to \(\ce{Br^-}\)), giving 1-bromo-2-methoxybutane — the major product — plus HBr. It is major because methanol, the solvent, is present in a much higher concentration than the bromide ion and therefore attacks the intermediate preferentially in the second step. 1

2-bromo-1-methoxybutane is not formed because both products arise from the secondary carbocation, which is more stable than the primary carbocation due to the two electron-donating alkyl groups on the positively charged carbon. That requires \(\ce{Br}\) to add to the terminal \(\ce{CH2}\), fixing Br on C1 and the incoming nucleophile on C2. 1

The three-nucleophile rule. Only the \(\ce{Br2}\) can act as the electrophile — it is the species that is polarised and opens the \(\ce{C=C}\) in the slow step. Everything else in the flask (\(\ce{Br^-}\), \(\ce{CH3OH}\), \(\ce{H2O}\), \(\ce{Cl^-}\) if brine is present) is a nucleophile that can only attack the carbocation in step 2. That is why bromine water gives up to three products and why no di-methoxy or dichloro product ever appears.
(b) The 1,2- and 1,4-products — [3]

\(\ce{H+}\) adds to C1 of buta-1,3-diene to give a resonance-stabilised allylic carbocation. The form leading to the 1,2-product is CH3–+CH–CH=CH2 (positive charge on C2). 1

The delocalised form leading to the 1,4-product is CH3–CH=CH–+CH2 (positive charge on C4). 1

Because the positive charge is delocalised over C2 and C4 (allylic resonance), \(\ce{Br^-}\) can attack at either position — at C2 to give the 1,2-product or at C4 to give the 1,4-product. 1

(c) Why the enone reacts with CN⁻ and the diene does not — [4]

Due to the lack of electronegativity difference between the two carbon atoms, the \(\ce{C=C}\) bond in buta-1,3-diene is non-polar. Its \(\pi\) electron cloud is electron rich, making it susceptible to electrophilic attack; with no electron-deficient carbon present, nucleophilic attack is not possible. 1

The \(\ce{C=O}\) group next to the \(\ce{C=C}\) in 4-methylpent-1-en-3-one is an electron-withdrawing group, by both resonance and inductive effects. 1

The electron density in the \(\ce{C=C}\) delocalises onto the \(\ce{C=O}\) (the p orbitals of the sp\(^2\) carbons and the oxygen overlap continuously), reducing the electron density on the terminal carbon of the \(\ce{C=C}\) and so enabling attack there by the \(\ce{CN^-}\) nucleophile. 1

The same delocalisation also reduces the electron deficiency of the carbonyl carbon, making it less susceptible to nucleophilic attack. In addition the bulky \(\ce{-CH(CH3)2}\) group causes steric hindrance, repelling the incoming nucleophile, in contrast to the sterically unhindered terminal carbon of the \(\ce{C=C}\), which bears only 2 H atoms. 1

This is the four-concept question. The mark scheme wants electronegativity, electronic (inductive/resonance) effect, delocalisation and steric effect — one mark each. "Alkenes undergo electrophilic addition" is a pattern-match that scores zero here, because the whole point is a \(\ce{C=C}\) that has been made electron-poor by the group next to it.

(a) Cold alkaline KMnO₄ — mild oxidation — [3]

Cold dilute alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\) causes mild oxidation: only the \(\pi\) bond breaks, and each of the two former \(\ce{C=C}\) carbons gains an \(\ce{-OH}\) group; both oxygen atoms come from the manganate and the \(\sigma\) framework survives. With an excess, all three \(\ce{C=C}\) are dihydroxylated to give a hexa-ol. (Observation: purple \(\ce{KMnO4}\) decolourised, brown \(\ce{MnO2}\) precipitate.) 1

Taking each in turn: from bond 1, the \(\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)-}\) carbon carries two identical methyl groups and is not chiral, but the adjacent \(\ce{-CH(OH)-}\) carries H, OH, \(\ce{C(CH3)2OH}\) and \(\ce{CH2-}\) — four different groups, so it is chiral. 1

From bond 2, the central carbon now carries OH, \(\ce{CH2OH}\), the \(\ce{-CH2CH2-}\) chain and the \(\ce{-CH(OH)CH2OH}\) fragment — chiral. From bond 3, the \(\ce{-CH(OH)-}\) carries H, OH, \(\ce{CH2OH}\) and that same quaternary carbon — chiral. Hence the product contains three chiral centres. 1

(b) Hot acidified KMnO₄ — oxidative cleavage — [3]

Hot acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) causes oxidative cleavage: work backwards from each alkene carbon by its H count. \(\ce{=CR2}\) (0 H) → ketone; \(\ce{=CHR}\) (1 H) → carboxylic acid; terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) (2 H) → \(\ce{CO2}\). 1

Bond 1: the \(\ce{(CH3)2C=}\) end has 0 H → propanone, \(\ce{(CH3)2C=O}\); the \(\ce{=CH-}\) end has 1 H → a \(\ce{-CO2H}\) on the chain. Bond 2: the substituted carbon has 0 H → a ketone in the chain; its terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) → \(\ce{CO2}\). Bond 3: the \(\ce{-CH=}\) has 1 H → a second \(\ce{-CO2H}\); its terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) → \(\ce{CO2}\). 1

Rejoining the surviving chain gives \(\ce{HO2C-CH2CH2-CO-CO2H}\), 2-oxopentanedioic acid. The organic products are therefore propanone and 2-oxopentanedioic acid — only two organic products, together with 2 mol of \(\ce{CO2}\), which is inorganic. 1

Count the carbons before and after. \(\ce{C10}\) in: propanone takes 3, the keto-diacid takes 5, and the two \(\ce{CO2}\) take 1 each — 3 + 5 + 2 = 10. If your carbons do not balance you have mis-assigned a fragment. And note that the question asks for organic products, so \(\ce{CO2}\) is not counted.
(c) Why the halohydrin has a tertiary alcohol — [2]

With aqueous bromine the electrophile is the polarised \(\ce{Br2}\); \(\ce{Br}\) adds to the alkene carbon that leaves the positive charge on the more substituted carbon, so the intermediate is the tertiary carbocation at the \(\ce{(CH3)2C-}\) carbon, which is more stable because three electron-donating alkyl groups disperse the positive charge (Markovnikov). 1

In the fast step, water attacks that tertiary carbocation and, after loss of \(\ce{H+}\), the \(\ce{-OH}\) ends up on the tertiary carbon, giving \(\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)-CHBr-}\) as the major halohydrin. A secondary alcohol would require the less stable secondary carbocation and would be the minor product. 1

(a) Reading the energy profile — [4]

The two-peak profile with an intermediate between the peaks indicates an SN1 mechanism — two distinct steps with a carbocation intermediate sitting in the trough. This is consistent with a benzylic carbon, whose carbocation is stabilised by delocalisation into the ring. 1

Rate = \(k[\ce{RCl}]\) — first order, and independent of \([\ce{OH^-}]\) — because the rate-determining step is the slow heterolytic formation of the carbocation, which involves only the halogenoalkane. 1

The solution does not rotate plane-polarised light: SN1 proceeds via a planar (trigonal) carbocation, so \(\ce{OH^-}\) attacks from above and below the plane with equal probability, giving equal amounts of both enantiomers — a racemic mixture whose rotations cancel. 1

Z is the first, rate-determining transition state, corresponding to the \(\ce{C-Cl}\) bond breaking to form the carbocation. \(\ce{OH^-}\) is not yet bonded at Z, so a five-coordinate species [Cl···C···OH]− is not what Z represents — that would be an SN2 transition state. 1

(b) Order of increasing rate of hydrolysis — [3]

The relative rate increases from O to M to N. 1

All three are primary halogenoalkanes undergoing nucleophilic substitution via SN2. The \(\ce{C-I}\) bond in N is weaker than the \(\ce{C-Br}\) bond in M and O (\(\ce{C-I}\) +238 against \(\ce{C-Br}\) +280 kJ mol\(^{-1}\)), so N has a lower activation energy and the fastest rate. 1

The highly branched alkyl group in O is bulkier than in M, causing greater steric hindrance to nucleophilic attack, and hence the slowest rate. Hence O < M < N. 1

The error you made confidently. Rate is set by \(\ce{C-X}\) bond energy, not electronegativity. \(\ce{C-I}\) is the weakest bond (lowest bond energy, longest bond) so it hydrolyses fastest; \(\ce{C-F}\) is the strongest and hydrolyses slowest. Rate order \(\ce{R-I > R-Br > R-Cl}\). Whether the mechanism is SN1 or SN2, breaking of the \(\ce{C-X}\) bond occurs in the rate-determining step.

(d) Why substitution, not elimination — [2]

In \(\ce{C6H5CHBrC(CH3)3}\) the carbon bearing Br has no \(\beta\)-hydrogen — its two neighbours are the phenyl ring and the quaternary carbon of the tert-butyl group, neither of which carries a hydrogen that could be abstracted. Elimination is therefore impossible. 1

The benzylic \(\ce{C-Br}\) instead undergoes nucleophilic substitution, via SN1 through a phenyl-stabilised carbocation, to give the alcohol — even though ethanolic conditions would normally favour elimination. 1

The reagent rule and its exception. Aqueous NaOH, warm → nucleophilic substitution → alcohol. Ethanolic NaOH/KOH, heat → elimination → alkene (in ethanol \(\ce{OH^-}\) is less solvated, so it is a stronger base and abstracts a \(\beta\)-hydrogen). The exception is structural, not conditional: no \(\beta\)-hydrogen means no elimination, whatever the solvent. Always check the neighbours of the C–X carbon before you apply the rule.

(a) Comparing acidity with sodium — [4]

Record the time taken for a fixed volume (e.g. 10 cm\(^3\)) of \(\ce{H2}\) gas to be collected for each compound when Na metal is added, using the same amount of each compound at the same temperature. 1

Acidity increases from ethanol < benzene-1,4-diol < phenol, so the time taken is shortest for phenol and longest for ethanol (rate of \(\ce{H2}\) production fastest for phenol, slowest for ethanol). 1

The electron-donating alkyl group intensifies the negative charge on the oxygen of the ethoxide ion, destabilising it, so ethanol is the weakest acid. Both phenoxides have resonance stabilisation due to delocalisation of the negative charge into the benzene ring, so phenol and hydroquinone are stronger acids than ethanol. 1

However, hydroquinone has an additional electron-donating \(\ce{-OH}\) group on the ring, which intensifies the negative charge on the O and destabilises its conjugate base; hence phenol is a stronger acid than hydroquinone and gives the greatest rate of \(\ce{H2}\) production. 1

Two things this is built to catch. First, \(\ce{-OH}\) on a ring is electron-DONATING (the O lone pair is pushed into the ring), so a second \(\ce{-OH}\) makes the phenoxide worse, not better — the intuition that "more OH means more acidic" is wrong. Second, you cannot differentiate the three by the volume of gas collected: equal amounts of each give the same volume of \(\ce{H2}\) (0.010/2 × 24 000 = 120 cm\(^3\)). The measurement has to be a rate. Hold the ladder: carboxylic acid > carbonic acid > phenol > water > alcohol — so \(\ce{NaHCO3}\) effervescence separates a carboxylic acid from a phenol, NaOH separates a phenol from an alcohol, and Na reacts with all three (giving the alkoxide or phenoxide plus \(\ce{H2}\)).
(b) Deducing the position of R — [3]

(i) \(\Delta M_r\) = 335.8 − 178.0 = 157.8 = 2 × 78.9, so two bromine atoms are substituted into the ring. Phenol directs bromination to the positions ortho and para to \(\ce{-OH}\) (positions 2, 4 and 6). Since only two (not three) Br are introduced, one of these activated positions must already be occupied by R; therefore R is at position 2 or 4 — ortho or para to \(\ce{-OH}\) — and not at position 3. 1

(ii) The ring of phenol is activated because the lone pair on the O is delocalised into it, raising the electron density enough for bromination to occur with aqueous \(\ce{Br2}\) at room temperature with no halogen carrier. The reaction is electrophilic substitution. \(\ce{Br2}\) in \(\ce{CCl4}\) in the dark is the test for a \(\ce{C=C}\), where the reaction is electrophilic addition; the dark and the non-polar solvent are there to prevent free-radical substitution. 1

(iii) Substituting Br costs \(M_r\) 78.9 each (79.9 in, 1.0 H out). Adding \(\ce{Br2}\) across a \(\ce{C=C}\) removes no hydrogen, so it would give \(\Delta M_r\) = 159.8. 1

(c) Structure of Q and the yellow precipitate — [2]

Q = \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\) (ethanol). It contains the \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}\) unit required for the tri-iodomethane test, and the remaining fragment appears as methanoate, giving \(\ce{HCO2H}\) on acidification. 1

It gives a positive tri-iodomethane (iodoform) test — the yellow precipitate is \(\ce{CHI3}\), tri-iodomethane. 1

The iodoform trigger. A positive test requires \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}\) or \(\ce{CH3CO-}\) — a methyl group attached directly to a carbon carrying either an OH-and-H or a carbonyl. Ethanol is the smallest alcohol that qualifies; methanol and propan-1-ol do not. The second product tells you what the rest of the molecule was: here \(\ce{HCO2H}\) means only one carbon was left over.
