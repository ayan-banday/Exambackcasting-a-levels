Question 1 [6]
A-Level 2018 P1 Q1
(i) Given that \(y=\dfrac{\ln x}{x}\), find \(\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}\) in terms of \(x\). [2] (ii) Hence, or otherwise, find the exact value of \(\displaystyle\int_{1}^{e}\frac{\ln x}{x^2}\,\mathrm dx\), showing your working. [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(i) quotient rule
\(y=\dfrac{\ln x}{x}\): numerator \(\ln x\) (derivative \(\tfrac1x\)), denominator \(x\) (derivative \(1\)). \[ \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{x\cdot\frac1x-\ln x}{x^2}=\frac{1-\ln x}{x^2}. \] (1)(1)
(ii) read the antiderivative off part (i), then split the integrand
From (i), \(\displaystyle\int\frac{1-\ln x}{x^2}\,\mathrm dx=\frac{\ln x}{x}+C\). Write \(\dfrac{\ln x}{x^2}=\dfrac{1}{x^2}-\dfrac{1-\ln x}{x^2}\) so the second piece integrates by (i). \[ \int_{1}^{e}\frac{\ln x}{x^2}\,\mathrm dx=\int_{1}^{e}\left[\frac{1}{x^2}-\frac{1-\ln x}{x^2}\right]\mathrm dx=\Big[-\tfrac1x-\tfrac{\ln x}{x}\Big]_{1}^{e}. \] (1)(1)(1) \[ =\Big(-\tfrac1e-\tfrac1e\Big)-\big(-1-0\big)=1-\frac{2}{e}. \] (1)
Fuses: quotient-rule differentiation → reverse it as a “hence” antiderivative to evaluate a definite integral.

Question 2 [6]
A-Level 2014 P1 Q5 · DHS 2016 C1 BT Q2
(a) Find \(\displaystyle\int x\big[(1-3x^2)^5+e^{x^2+1}\big]\,\mathrm dx\). [3] (b) Find \(\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\cos^{-1}(x^2)\). Hence, or otherwise, find the exact value of \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1/\sqrt2}\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^4}}\,\mathrm dx\). [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(a) both terms are of the form \(f'(x)[f(x)]^n\) / \(f'(x)e^{f(x)}\)
\(\int x(1-3x^2)^5\,\mathrm dx\): the inside \(1-3x^2\) differentiates to \(-6x\), so pull out \(-\tfrac16\). \(\int xe^{x^2+1}\,\mathrm dx\): the inside \(x^2+1\) differentiates to \(2x\), so pull out \(\tfrac12\). \[ \int x\big[(1-3x^2)^5+e^{x^2+1}\big]\,\mathrm dx=-\frac{(1-3x^2)^6}{36}+\tfrac12 e^{x^2+1}+C. \] (1)(1)(1)
(b) differentiate the inverse-trig, then use it in reverse
MF26: \(\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm du}\cos^{-1}u=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\) with \(u=x^2\), \(u'=2x\). The integrand is then \(-\tfrac12\) of that derivative. \[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\cos^{-1}(x^2)=-\frac{2x}{\sqrt{1-x^4}}. \] (1) \[ \int_{0}^{1/\sqrt2}\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^4}}\,\mathrm dx=-\tfrac12\Big[\cos^{-1}(x^2)\Big]_{0}^{1/\sqrt2}=-\tfrac12\Big(\tfrac{\pi}{3}-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\Big)=\frac{\pi}{12}. \] (1)(1)
Fuses: standard-form integration + differentiate an inverse-trig function → run it backwards to hit an exact integral.

Question 3 [6]
HCI 2006 Promo Q7 · HCI Supplementary Q13
(i) Show that \(\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{1+x}{1-x}\right)=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\). [3] (ii) Hence find \(\displaystyle\int\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right)\mathrm dx\). [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(i) chain rule on the inverse-trig, quotient rule on the inside
Let \(y=\tan^{-1}\!\big(\tfrac{1+x}{1-x}\big)\). MF26: \(\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm du}\tan^{-1}u=\tfrac{1}{1+u^2}\); the inside \(u=\tfrac{1+x}{1-x}\) differentiates by the quotient rule to \(\tfrac{2}{(1-x)^2}\). \[ \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right)^2}\cdot\frac{(1-x)-(1+x)(-1)}{(1-x)^2}=\frac{(1-x)^2}{(1-x)^2+(1+x)^2}\cdot\frac{2}{(1-x)^2} \] (1)(1) \[ =\frac{2}{(1-2x+x^2)+(1+2x+x^2)}=\frac{2}{2+2x^2}=\frac{1}{1+x^2}\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)
(ii) integrate by parts using the derivative from (i)
Take \(u=\tan^{-1}\!\big(\tfrac{1+x}{1-x}\big)\) (so \(u'=\tfrac{1}{1+x^2}\) from part (i)) and \(v'=1\) (so \(v=x\)). \[ \int\tan^{-1}\!\Big(\tfrac{1+x}{1-x}\Big)\,\mathrm dx=x\tan^{-1}\!\Big(\tfrac{1+x}{1-x}\Big)-\int\frac{x}{1+x^2}\,\mathrm dx=x\tan^{-1}\!\Big(\tfrac{1+x}{1-x}\Big)-\tfrac12\ln(1+x^2)+C. \] (1)(1)(1)
Fuses: hard quotient + chain + inverse-trig differentiation → the result becomes \(u'\) inside an integration by parts.

Question 4 [7]
A-Level 2022 P1 Q4
(a) Show that \(\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}(\cot x)=-\operatorname{cosec}^2 x\). [2] (b) Show that \(\sin 2x\,\tan x=2\sin^2 x\). [1] (c) Hence find the exact value of \(\displaystyle\int_{\pi/18}^{\pi/9}\operatorname{cosec} 6x\,\cot 3x\,\mathrm dx\). [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(a) quotient rule on \(\cot x=\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\)
\[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\!\left(\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\right)=\frac{-\sin^2 x-\cos^2 x}{\sin^2 x}=\frac{-1}{\sin^2 x}=-\operatorname{cosec}^2 x. \] (1)(1)
(b) double-angle identity
\(\sin 2x=2\sin x\cos x\) and \(\tan x=\tfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\). \[ \sin 2x\,\tan x=2\sin x\cos x\cdot\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}=2\sin^2 x\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)
(c) use (b) with \(x\to 3x\) to simplify, then integrate with (a)
By (b), \(\sin 6x\,\tan 3x=2\sin^2 3x\), so \(\operatorname{cosec}6x\,\cot 3x=\dfrac{1}{\sin 6x\,\tan 3x}=\dfrac{1}{2\sin^2 3x}=\tfrac12\operatorname{cosec}^2 3x\); antiderivative from (a): \(\int\operatorname{cosec}^2 3x\,\mathrm dx=-\tfrac13\cot 3x\). \[ \int\operatorname{cosec}6x\,\cot 3x\,\mathrm dx=\int\frac{1}{2\sin^2 3x}\,\mathrm dx=-\tfrac16\int -3\operatorname{cosec}^2 3x\,\mathrm dx=-\tfrac16\Big[\cot 3x\Big]_{\pi/18}^{\pi/9} \] (1)(1) \[ =-\tfrac16\Big[\cot\tfrac{\pi}{3}-\cot\tfrac{\pi}{6}\Big]=-\tfrac16\Big[\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}-\sqrt3\Big]=\frac{1}{3\sqrt3}=\frac{\sqrt3}{9}. \] (1)(1)
Fuses: differentiate \(\cot\) + a trig identity → both feed a “hence” definite trig integral.

Question 5 [6]
A-Level N2002 P1 Q6
(i)(a) Write down \(\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\big(e^{x^2}\big)\). [1] (i)(b) Find \(\displaystyle\int x\,e^{x^2}\,\mathrm dx\). [1] (ii) Find \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1} x^3 e^{x^2}\,\mathrm dx\). [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(i) differentiate, then read the integral straight back
\(e^{x^2}\) by the chain rule; the antiderivative of \(x\,e^{x^2}\) is then immediate. \[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\big(e^{x^2}\big)=2x\,e^{x^2};\qquad \int x\,e^{x^2}\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac12\int 2x\,e^{x^2}\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac12 e^{x^2}+C. \] (1)(1)
(ii) split off \(x^2\), then integrate by parts using (i)(b)
Write \(x^3 e^{x^2}=x^2\big(x\,e^{x^2}\big)\). Take \(u=x^2\) (so \(u'=2x\)) and \(v'=x\,e^{x^2}\) (so \(v=\tfrac12 e^{x^2}\), from part (i)(b)). \[ \int_{0}^{1} x^3 e^{x^2}\,\mathrm dx=\Big[\tfrac12 x^2 e^{x^2}\Big]_{0}^{1}-\int_{0}^{1} x\,e^{x^2}\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac12 e-\Big[\tfrac12 e^{x^2}\Big]_{0}^{1} \] (1)(1)(1) \[ =\tfrac12 e-\Big[\tfrac12 e-\tfrac12\Big]=\tfrac12. \] (1)
Fuses: chain-rule derivative → the reverse gives \(\int x e^{x^2}\), which is then the \(v'\) in an integration by parts.

Question 6 [4]
SRJC 2006 Promo Q10 · HCI Supplementary (Applications) Q8(a)
Differentiate \(\tan^{-1}(x^3)\) with respect to \(x\), and hence, or otherwise, find \(\displaystyle\int x^2\tan^{-1}(x^3)\,\mathrm dx\).
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
Differentiate the inverse-trig function
MF26: \(\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm du}\tan^{-1}u=\tfrac{1}{1+u^2}\) with \(u=x^3\), \(u'=3x^2\). \[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\tan^{-1}(x^3)=\frac{3x^2}{1+x^6}. \] (1)
Integrate by parts, using that derivative as \(u'\)
Take \(u=\tan^{-1}(x^3)\) (so \(u'=\tfrac{3x^2}{1+x^6}\)) and \(v'=x^2\) (so \(v=\tfrac{x^3}{3}\)); the leftover integral is a log form \(\tfrac{f'}{f}\) with \(f=1+x^6\). \[ \int x^2\tan^{-1}(x^3)\,\mathrm dx=\frac{x^3}{3}\tan^{-1}(x^3)-\int\frac{x^3}{3}\cdot\frac{3x^2}{1+x^6}\,\mathrm dx=\frac{x^3}{3}\tan^{-1}(x^3)-\int\frac{x^5}{1+x^6}\,\mathrm dx \] (1)(1) \[ =\frac{x^3}{3}\tan^{-1}(x^3)-\frac16\ln(1+x^6)+C. \] (1)
Fuses: differentiate an inverse-trig → use it as \(u'\) in by parts → finish with a log-form integral.

Question 7 [9]
A-Level 2008 P2 Q2
The curve \(C\) has equation \(y^2=x\sqrt{1-x}\). The region enclosed by \(C\) is denoted by \(R\). (i) Write down an integral that gives the area of \(R\), and evaluate this integral numerically. [3] (ii) The part of \(R\) above the \(x\)-axis is rotated through \(2\pi\) radians about the \(x\)-axis. By using the substitution \(u=1-x\), or otherwise, find the exact value of the volume obtained. [3] (iii) Find the exact \(x\)-coordinate of the maximum point of \(C\). [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(i) area — the curve is symmetric about the \(x\)-axis
\(y=\pm x^{1/2}(1-x)^{1/4}\); the region runs \(0\le x\le1\), so the total area is twice the top half. \[ \text{Area of }R=2\int_{0}^{1} x^{1/2}(1-x)^{1/4}\,\mathrm dx=0.999\ \text{units}^2\ (\text{3 s.f.}). \] (1)(1)(1)
(ii) volume about the \(x\)-axis, then substitute \(u=1-x\)
\(V=\pi\int y^2\,\mathrm dx\) with \(y^2=x(1-x)^{1/2}\). Let \(u=1-x\Rightarrow x=1-u,\ \mathrm dx=-\mathrm du\); limits \(x:0\to1\) give \(u:1\to0\). \[ V=\pi\int_{0}^{1} x(1-x)^{1/2}\,\mathrm dx=\pi\int_{0}^{1}\big(u^{1/2}-u^{3/2}\big)\,\mathrm du=\pi\Big[\tfrac23 u^{3/2}-\tfrac25 u^{5/2}\Big]_{0}^{1}=\frac{4\pi}{15}\ \text{units}^3. \] (1)(1)(1)
(iii) maximum point by implicit differentiation
Differentiate \(y^2=x(1-x)^{1/2}\) implicitly; the maximum has \(\tfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=0\), i.e. the numerator vanishes. \[ 2y\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\tfrac12(1-x)^{-1/2}(2-3x)\Rightarrow\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{2-3x}{4y\sqrt{1-x}}=0\Rightarrow x=\tfrac23. \] (1)(1)(1)
Fuses: area + volume-of-revolution by substitution (integration) with a maximum point found by implicit differentiation, all on one curve.

Question 8 [13]
A-Level 2021 P1 Q8
A function \(f\) is defined by \(f(x)=e^x\cos x\), for \(0\le x\le\tfrac12\pi\). (a) Using calculus, find the stationary point of \(f(x)\) and determine its nature. [5] (b) Integrate by parts twice to show that \(\displaystyle\int e^{2x}\cos 2x\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac14 e^{2x}(\sin 2x+\cos 2x)+c\). [4] (c) The graph of \(y=f(x)\) is rotated completely about the \(x\)-axis. Find the exact volume generated. [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(a) product rule, solve \(f'=0\), test the nature
\(f(x)=e^x\cos x\) is a product; since \(e^x>0\), the stationary condition reduces to \(\cos x-\sin x=0\). \[ f'(x)=e^x\cos x-e^x\sin x=e^x(\cos x-\sin x)=0\Rightarrow\tan x=1\Rightarrow x=\tfrac{\pi}{4}. \] (1)(1) \[ f''\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)=-\sqrt2\,e^{\pi/4}<0\ \Rightarrow\ \text{maximum at }\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4},\,\tfrac{e^{\pi/4}}{\sqrt2}\right). \] (1)(1)(1)
(b) cyclic integration by parts
Take \(u=e^{2x}\), \(v'=\cos 2x\); apply parts, then apply it again to the new integral with \(u=e^{2x}\), \(v'=\sin 2x\). The original integral reappears. \[ \int e^{2x}\cos 2x\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac12 e^{2x}\sin 2x-\int e^{2x}\sin 2x\,\mathrm dx \] (1) \[ =\tfrac12 e^{2x}\sin 2x-\Big[-\tfrac12 e^{2x}\cos 2x+\int e^{2x}\cos 2x\,\mathrm dx\Big]. \] (1) \[ 2\int e^{2x}\cos 2x\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac12 e^{2x}\sin 2x+\tfrac12 e^{2x}\cos 2x+C'\Rightarrow\int e^{2x}\cos 2x\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac14 e^{2x}(\sin 2x+\cos 2x)+c. \] (1)(1)
(c) volume, using the double-angle identity and the result of (b)
\(V=\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}(e^x\cos x)^2\,\mathrm dx\); replace \(\cos^2 x=\tfrac{1+\cos 2x}{2}\), then use part (b) on the \(e^{2x}\cos 2x\) piece. \[ V=\pi\int_{0}^{\pi/2} e^{2x}\cos^2 x\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi/2} e^{2x}(1+\cos 2x)\,\mathrm dx \] (1) \[ =\tfrac{\pi}{2}\Big[\tfrac14 e^{2x}(\sin 2x+\cos 2x)\Big]_{0}^{\pi/2}+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\Big[\tfrac12 e^{2x}\Big]_{0}^{\pi/2}=\frac{\pi}{8}\big(e^{\pi}-3\big)\ \text{units}^3. \] (1)(1)(1)
Fuses: stationary point + nature (differentiation) → cyclic by-parts integral → volume of revolution using that integral.

Question 9 [12]
A-Level 2009 P1 Q11
The curve \(C\) has equation \(y=f(x)\), where \(f(x)=x\,e^{-x^2}\). (i) Sketch the curve \(C\). [2] (ii) Find the exact coordinates of the turning points on the curve. [4] (iii) Use the substitution \(u=x^2\) to find \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{n} f(x)\,\mathrm dx\), for \(n\ge0\). Hence find the area of the region between the curve and the positive \(x\)-axis. [4] (iv) Find the volume of revolution when the region bounded by the curve, the lines \(x=0\), \(x=1\) and the \(x\)-axis is rotated completely about the \(x\)-axis. Give your answer correct to 3 significant figures. [2]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(i) shape
\(f\) is an odd function through the origin, with horizontal asymptote \(y=0\) and the turning points found in (ii).

Odd-symmetric curve through \(O\), \(y\to0\) as \(x\to\pm\infty\), one maximum for \(x>0\) and one minimum for \(x<0\).

(1)(1)
(ii) turning points — product rule
\(y=x\,e^{-x^2}\): differentiate as a product, then set \(\tfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=0\) (with \(e^{-x^2}>0\)). \[ \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=e^{-x^2}+x\,e^{-x^2}(-2x)=e^{-x^2}(1-2x^2)=0\Rightarrow x^2=\tfrac12\Rightarrow x=\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}. \] (1)(1) \[ y=\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}e^{-1/2}=\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2e}};\quad\text{turning points }\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2e}}\Big),\ \Big(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2e}}\Big). \] (1)(1)
(iii) integrate by substitution, then let \(n\to\infty\)
\(u=x^2\Rightarrow\mathrm du=2x\,\mathrm dx\); limits \(x:0\to n\) give \(u:0\to n^2\). \[ \int_{0}^{n} x\,e^{-x^2}\,\mathrm dx=\int_{0}^{n^2}\tfrac12 e^{-u}\,\mathrm du=\tfrac12\big[1-e^{-n^2}\big];\qquad n\to\infty\Rightarrow\text{area}=\tfrac12. \] (1)(1)(1)(1)
(iv) volume about the \(x\)-axis
\(V=\pi\int_0^1 y^2\,\mathrm dx=\pi\int_0^1 x^2 e^{-2x^2}\,\mathrm dx\), evaluated on the GC. \[ V=\pi\int_{0}^{1} x^2 e^{-2x^2}\,\mathrm dx=0.363\ \text{units}^3\ (\text{3 s.f.}). \] (1)(1)
Fuses: turning points (differentiation) with area by substitution and volume of revolution (integration) — one curve, both toolkits.

Question 10 [10]
A-Level 2013 P1 Q10
A hemispherical bowl of fixed radius \(r\) cm is modelled by rotating the lower half of the circle \(x^2+(y-r)^2=r^2\) about the \(y\)-axis. The bowl is filled with water, which drains from a hole at the bottom at a constant rate. (i) Show that when the depth of water is \(h\) cm (where \(h\le r\)), the volume of water in the bowl is \(V=\dfrac{\pi h^2}{3}(3r-h)\). [4] (ii) Given that a full bowl of water would become empty in \(24\) s, find the rate of decrease, in terms of \(r\) and \(h\), of the depth of water at the instant when the depth is \(h\) cm. [4] (iii) Without any differentiation, determine, in terms of \(r\), the slowest rate at which the depth of water is decreasing. [2]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(i) volume of revolution about the \(y\)-axis
\(V=\pi\int_0^h x^2\,\mathrm dy\); from the circle, \(x^2=r^2-(y-r)^2\), integrated from \(y=0\) to \(y=h\). \[ V=\pi\int_{0}^{h}\big[r^2-(y-r)^2\big]\,\mathrm dy=\pi\Big[r^2 y-\tfrac{(y-r)^3}{3}\Big]_{0}^{h}=\pi\Big(h^2 r-\tfrac{h^3}{3}\Big)=\frac{\pi h^2}{3}(3r-h). \] (1)(1)(1)(1)
(ii) find the drain rate, then chain to \(\tfrac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}\)
A full bowl (\(h=r\)) holds \(\tfrac23\pi r^3\) and empties in \(24\) s, so \(\dfrac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=-\dfrac{\pi r^3}{36}\). Differentiate \(V\): \(\dfrac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dh}=\pi(2rh-h^2)\), then \(\dfrac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}=\dfrac{\mathrm dV/\mathrm dt}{\mathrm dV/\mathrm dh}\). \[ \frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=-\frac{\pi r^3}{36},\qquad \frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dh}=\pi(2rh-h^2)\Rightarrow\text{rate of decrease}=\frac{r^3}{36(2rh-h^2)}\ \text{cm s}^{-1}. \] (1)(1)(1)(1)
(iii) slowest rate — maximise the denominator by inspection
The rate is smallest when \(2rh-h^2\) is largest; for \(0 \[ 2rh-h^2\ \text{greatest at }h=r\Rightarrow\text{slowest rate}=\frac{r^3}{36 r^2}=\frac{r}{36}\ \text{cm s}^{-1}. \] (1)(1)
Fuses: prove a volume by integration → feed it into a connected rate of change → extremum by inspection.

Question 11 [13]
A-Level 2018 P1 Q9
A curve \(C\) has parametric equations \(x=2\theta-\sin 2\theta\), \(y=2\sin^2\theta\), for \(0\le\theta\le\pi\). (i) Show that \(\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\cot\theta\). [4] (ii) The normal to the curve at the point where \(\theta=\alpha\) meets the \(x\)-axis at the point \(A\). Show that the \(x\)-coordinate of \(A\) is \(k\alpha\), where \(k\) is a constant to be found. [4] (iii) Do not use a calculator in this part. The arc length between the two points on \(C\) where \(\theta=\beta\) and \(\theta=\gamma\) is given by \(\displaystyle\int_{\beta}^{\gamma}\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}\right)^2+\left(\tfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm d\theta}\right)^2}\,\mathrm d\theta\). Find the total length of \(C\). [5]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(i) parametric gradient
\(\dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}=2-2\cos 2\theta\) and \(\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm d\theta}=4\sin\theta\cos\theta\); use \(2-2\cos 2\theta=4\sin^2\theta\). \[ \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{4\sin\theta\cos\theta}{2-2\cos 2\theta}=\frac{4\sin\theta\cos\theta}{4\sin^2\theta}=\cot\theta\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)(1)(1)(1)
(ii) equation of the normal, then set \(y=0\)
Tangent gradient is \(\cot\alpha\), so the normal gradient is \(-\tan\alpha\). The point is \(\big(2\alpha-\sin 2\alpha,\ 2\sin^2\alpha\big)\). \[ y-2\sin^2\alpha=-\tan\alpha\big[x-(2\alpha-\sin 2\alpha)\big]. \] (1) \[ y=0:\ x-(2\alpha-\sin 2\alpha)=\frac{2\sin^2\alpha}{\tan\alpha}=2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha=\sin 2\alpha\Rightarrow x=2\alpha\ \Rightarrow\ k=2. \] (1)(1)(1)
(iii) arc-length integral, simplified with a double-angle identity
Substitute \(\tfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}=2-2\cos 2\theta\) and \(\tfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm d\theta}=2\sin 2\theta\); simplify under the root using \(1-\cos 2\theta=2\sin^2\theta\). \[ \text{Length}=\int_{0}^{\pi}\sqrt{(2-2\cos 2\theta)^2+(2\sin 2\theta)^2}\,\mathrm d\theta=\int_{0}^{\pi}\sqrt{8(1-\cos 2\theta)}\,\mathrm d\theta=\int_{0}^{\pi}\sqrt{16\sin^2\theta}\,\mathrm d\theta \] (1)(1) \[ =\int_{0}^{\pi}4\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta=\Big[-4\cos\theta\Big]_{0}^{\pi}=8\ \text{units}. \] (1)(1)(1)
Fuses: parametric differentiation + normal (differentiation) → the same \(\tfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta},\tfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm d\theta}\) drive an arc-length integral.

Question 12 [7]
HCI 2006 Promo Q9 · HCI Supplementary Q28
(i) Show that \(\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\Big[\ln\!\big(x+\sqrt{1+x^2}\,\big)\Big]=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}\). [2] (ii) Hence find \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}\,\mathrm dx\). [2] (iii) Verify the result in part (ii) using the substitution \(x=\tan\theta\). [3]

Solution — verbatim HCI
(i) chain rule on the log
\(\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\ln g=\tfrac{g'}{g}\) with \(g=x+\sqrt{1+x^2}\), so \(g'=1+\tfrac{2x}{2\sqrt{1+x^2}}=\tfrac{\sqrt{1+x^2}+x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}\). \[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\ln\!\big(x+\sqrt{1+x^2}\big)=\frac{1+\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}}{x+\sqrt{1+x^2}}=\frac{\frac{x+\sqrt{1+x^2}}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}}{x+\sqrt{1+x^2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)(1)
(ii) reverse part (i)
\[ \int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}\,\mathrm dx=\Big[\ln\!\big(x+\sqrt{1+x^2}\big)\Big]_{0}^{1}=\ln(1+\sqrt2)-\ln 1=\ln(1+\sqrt2). \] (1)(1)
(iii) verify by trig substitution
\(x=\tan\theta\Rightarrow\dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}=\sec^2\theta\); limits \(x=0\Rightarrow\theta=0\), \(x=1\Rightarrow\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\); and \(\sqrt{1+\tan^2\theta}=\sec\theta\). \[ \int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}\,\mathrm dx=\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\frac{\sec^2\theta}{\sqrt{1+\tan^2\theta}}\,\mathrm d\theta=\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\sec\theta\,\mathrm d\theta=\Big[\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|\Big]_{0}^{\pi/4}=\ln(1+\sqrt2). \] (1)(1)(1)
Fuses: differentiate a log to expose an integrand → evaluate it → re-derive the same value by trig substitution.

Question 13 [12]
A-Level 2011 P2 Q4
(a)(i) Obtain a formula for \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{n} x^2 e^{-2x}\,\mathrm dx\) in terms of \(n\), where \(n>0\). [5] (a)(ii) Hence evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty} x^2 e^{-2x}\,\mathrm dx\). You may assume that \(ne^{-2n}\to0\) and \(n^2 e^{-2n}\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\). [1] (b) The region bounded by the curve \(y=\dfrac{4x}{x^2+1}\), the \(x\)-axis and the lines \(x=0\) and \(x=1\) is rotated through \(2\pi\) radians about the \(x\)-axis. Use the substitution \(x=\tan\theta\) to show that the volume of the solid obtained is \(\displaystyle 16\pi\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\), and evaluate this integral exactly. [6]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(a)(i) integration by parts twice
Take \(u=x^2\) (\(u'=2x\)), \(v'=e^{-2x}\) (\(v=-\tfrac12 e^{-2x}\)); the new integral needs parts again with \(u=x\) (\(u'=1\)), \(v'=e^{-2x}\) (\(v=-\tfrac12 e^{-2x}\)). \[ \int_{0}^{n} x^2 e^{-2x}\,\mathrm dx=\Big[-\tfrac12 x^2 e^{-2x}\Big]_{0}^{n}+\int_{0}^{n} x\,e^{-2x}\,\mathrm dx=-\tfrac{n^2}{2}e^{-2n}+\Big[-\tfrac12 x e^{-2x}\Big]_{0}^{n}+\tfrac12\int_{0}^{n} e^{-2x}\,\mathrm dx \] (1)(1)(1) \[ =-\tfrac{n^2}{2}e^{-2n}-\tfrac{n}{2}e^{-2n}-\Big[\tfrac14 e^{-2x}\Big]_{0}^{n}=\tfrac14-\frac{e^{-2n}}{4}\big(2n^2+2n+1\big). \] (1)(1)
(a)(ii) take the limit
As \(n\to\infty\), \(n^2 e^{-2n}\), \(n e^{-2n}\) and \(e^{-2n}\) all \(\to0\). \[ \int_{0}^{\infty} x^2 e^{-2x}\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac14. \] (1)
(b) volume, then \(x=\tan\theta\) to reduce to \(\sin^2\theta\)
\(V=\pi\int_0^1\big(\tfrac{4x}{x^2+1}\big)^2\mathrm dx=\pi\int_0^1\tfrac{16x^2}{(x^2+1)^2}\mathrm dx\). With \(x=\tan\theta\): \(\mathrm dx=\sec^2\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\), \(x^2+1=\sec^2\theta\), limits \(0\to\tfrac{\pi}{4}\). \[ V=\pi\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\frac{16\tan^2\theta}{\sec^4\theta}\sec^2\theta\,\mathrm d\theta=16\pi\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)(1) \[ =8\pi\int_{0}^{\pi/4}(1-\cos 2\theta)\,\mathrm d\theta=8\pi\Big[\theta-\tfrac12\sin 2\theta\Big]_{0}^{\pi/4}=8\pi\Big(\tfrac{\pi}{4}-\tfrac12\Big)=2\pi^2-4\pi\ \text{units}^3. \] (1)(1)
Fuses: repeated by parts to build a reduction-style formula, then a volume of revolution via trig substitution.

Question 14 [10]
A-Level 2016 P2 Q4
(a)(i) Find \(\displaystyle\int x^2\cos nx\,\mathrm dx\), where \(n\) is a positive integer. [3] (a)(ii) Hence find \(\displaystyle\int_{\pi}^{2\pi} x^2\cos nx\,\mathrm dx\), giving your answer in the form \(\dfrac{a\pi}{n^2}\), where the possible values of \(a\) are to be determined. [2] (b) The region bounded by \(y=\dfrac{x\sqrt{x}}{9-x^2}\), the \(x\)-axis and the lines \(x=0\) and \(x=2\) is rotated through \(2\pi\) about the \(x\)-axis. Use the substitution \(u=9-x^2\) to find the exact volume, simplifying your answer. [5]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(a)(i) integration by parts twice
Take \(u=x^2\), \(v'=\cos nx\) (\(v=\tfrac{\sin nx}{n}\)); the leftover \(\int x\sin nx\,\mathrm dx\) needs parts again with \(u=x\), \(v'=\sin nx\). \[ \int x^2\cos nx\,\mathrm dx=\frac{x^2\sin nx}{n}+\frac{2x\cos nx}{n^2}-\frac{2\sin nx}{n^3}+C. \] (1)(1)(1)
(a)(ii) evaluate at \(\pi\) and \(2\pi\) (integer \(n\))
\(\sin(n\pi)=\sin(2n\pi)=0\); \(\cos(2n\pi)=1\) and \(\cos(n\pi)=(-1)^n\). \[ \int_{\pi}^{2\pi} x^2\cos nx\,\mathrm dx=\frac{4\pi}{n^2}-\frac{2\pi\cos n\pi}{n^2}=\frac{2\pi}{n^2}\ (n\text{ even})\ \text{or}\ \frac{6\pi}{n^2}\ (n\text{ odd});\quad a=2,\,6. \] (1)(1)
(b) volume, then substitute \(u=9-x^2\)
\(V=\pi\int_0^2\big(\tfrac{x\sqrt x}{9-x^2}\big)^2\mathrm dx=\pi\int_0^2\tfrac{x^3}{(9-x^2)^2}\mathrm dx\). With \(u=9-x^2\): \(\mathrm du=-2x\,\mathrm dx\), \(x^2=9-u\), limits \(x:0\to2\) give \(u:9\to5\). \[ V=\pi\int_{0}^{2}\frac{x^3}{(9-x^2)^2}\,\mathrm dx=-\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{9}^{5}\big(9u^{-2}-u^{-1}\big)\,\mathrm du=\frac{\pi}{2}\Big[\frac{9}{u}+\ln u\Big]_{9}^{5} \] (1)(1)(1) \[ =\frac{\pi}{2}\Big(\tfrac45+\ln\tfrac59\Big)=\frac{2\pi}{5}+\frac{\pi}{2}\ln\frac59\ \text{units}^3. \] (1)(1)
Fuses: by-parts-twice antiderivative feeding a definite integral, then a volume of revolution by substitution.

Question 15 [6]
A-Level 2019 P2 Q1
You are given \(I=\displaystyle\int x(1-x)^{1/2}\,\mathrm dx\). (i) Use integration by parts to find an expression for \(I\). [2] (ii) Use the substitution \(u^2=1-x\) to find another expression for \(I\). [2] (iii) Show algebraically that your answers to parts (i) and (ii) differ by a constant. [2]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim HCI
(i) by parts
Take \(u=x\) (\(u'=1\)) and \(v'=(1-x)^{1/2}\) (so \(v=-\tfrac23(1-x)^{3/2}\)). \[ I=-\tfrac23 x(1-x)^{3/2}+\tfrac23\int(1-x)^{3/2}\,\mathrm dx=-\tfrac23 x(1-x)^{3/2}-\tfrac{4}{15}(1-x)^{5/2}+C_1. \] (1)(1)
(ii) by substitution
\(u^2=1-x\Rightarrow 2u\dfrac{\mathrm du}{\mathrm dx}=-1\), and \(x=1-u^2\), \((1-x)^{1/2}=u\). \[ I=\int(1-u^2)\,u\,(-2u)\,\mathrm du=-2\int(u^2-u^4)\,\mathrm du=-\tfrac23(1-x)^{3/2}+\tfrac25(1-x)^{5/2}+C_2. \] (1)(1)
(iii) subtract the two expressions
Group the \((1-x)^{5/2}\) terms and rewrite \(-\tfrac23 x(1-x)^{3/2}+\tfrac23(1-x)^{3/2}=\tfrac23(1-x)^{5/2}\).

The \((1-x)^{5/2}\) terms combine to \(-\tfrac23(1-x)^{5/2}\), and \(-\tfrac23 x(1-x)^{3/2}+\tfrac23(1-x)^{3/2}=\tfrac23(1-x)^{5/2}\); these cancel, leaving \(C_1-C_2=\) an arbitrary constant.

(1)(1)
Fuses: two integration methods (by parts vs. substitution) reconciled algebraically — the same antiderivative up to a constant.
