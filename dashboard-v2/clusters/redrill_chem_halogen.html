Stage 1 of the Monsanto process for making ethanoic acid involves the reaction between methanol and hydrogen iodide to form methyl iodide and water.

(a) Suggest the type of reaction occurring in this stage. [1]
HCI Prelim · N2025 · P3 · Q4(b)(i)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Nucleophilic substitution 1 — the \(\ce{-OH}\) of the alcohol is substituted by \(\ce{I-}\).
(b) The mechanism involves two steps: step 1, protonation of the \(\ce{-OH}\) group of methanol by HI; step 2, attack of the intermediate by iodide ion. Draw the mechanism, showing lone pairs, dipoles, curly arrows, charges and the intermediate. [2]
HCI Prelim · N2025 · P3 · Q4(b)(ii)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Mechanism — one mark per correct step.
  • Step 1: a lone pair on the –OH oxygen of \(\ce{CH3OH}\) attacks the δ+ H of H–I (curly arrow from O to H; curly arrow from the H–I bond to I), giving the protonated alcohol (oxonium) intermediate \(\ce{CH3-O+H2}\) and \(\ce{I-}\) 1
  • Step 2: a lone pair on \(\ce{I-}\) attacks the δ+ carbon of \(\ce{CH3-O+H2}\) (curly arrow from \(\ce{I-}\) to C; curly arrow from the C–O bond to O, expelling \(\ce{H2O}\) as the good leaving group), forming \(\ce{CH3I + H2O}\) 1
This is the whole point of the question. \(\ce{-OH}\) is a terrible leaving group — hydroxide never just walks off a carbon. Every alcohol-to-halogenoalkane route exists to fix that: HX protonates the oxygen so the leaving group becomes neutral water; \(\ce{PCl5}\), \(\ce{PCl3}\) and \(\ce{SOCl2}\) convert \(\ce{-OH}\) into a phosphorus- or sulfur-based leaving group instead. Reject conc H₃PO₄ — that is the dehydration reagent and gives you an alkene, not a halogenoalkane.
(c) Succinic acid can be synthesised from ethane-1,2-diol in three steps (ethane-1,2-diol → step 1 → P → step 2 → Q → step 3 → succinic acid, \(\ce{HO2C-CH2CH2-CO2H}\)). State the reagents and conditions required for steps 1 to 3. [3]
HCI Prelim · N2023 · P2 · Q3(c)(i)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • Step 1: \(\ce{PCl5}\) (or \(\ce{SOCl2}\), or red P + \(\ce{Cl2}\)) at room temperature 1 — converts each \(\ce{-OH}\) to \(\ce{-Cl}\), giving 1,2-dichloroethane.
  • Step 2: KCN dissolved in ethanol, heat under reflux 1 — nucleophilic substitution replacing each Cl with \(\ce{-CN}\), giving butanedinitrile.
  • Step 3: dilute \(\ce{H2SO4}\)(aq) (or dilute HCl), heat under reflux 1 — acid hydrolysis of the two nitrile groups to \(\ce{-CO2H}\), giving succinic acid.
Count the carbons and the whole route falls out. Ethane-1,2-diol is \(\ce{C2}\); succinic acid is \(\ce{C4}\). You need two extra carbons, and the only A-level way to grow a chain is \(\ce{CN-}\), which adds exactly one carbon per \(\ce{-CN}\) (one C and one N). Two \(\ce{-CN}\) groups, two carbons, \(\ce{C2 -> C4}\). You said the nitrile route adds two carbons — it adds one, and it is the count that tells you how many halogen sites you need in step 1. Also note step 1 is room temperature, no heat.

1-bromopropane is reacted with NaCN in aqueous ethanolic solution.

(a) State the mechanism and draw it, showing curly arrows, the transition state and the products. [3]
HCI Soln · N2022 · P3 · Q4(f)(i)  ·  stem reconstructed from the key
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
SN2 on a primary halogenoalkane.
  • Nucleophilic substitution, \(\mathrm{S_N}2\) — 1-bromopropane is a primary halogenoalkane so it undergoes \(\mathrm{S_N}2\) 1
  • \(\ce{NC^{-}}\) attacks the δ+ C bearing Br from the side opposite to Br — curly arrow from the lone pair on the C of \(\ce{CN-}\) to that carbon, and curly arrow from the C–Br bond to Br 1
  • Pentacoordinate transition state \(\ce{[NC\bond{...}C\bond{...}Br]^-}\), which must carry the negative charge, with the three other groups in a plane → product \(\ce{NC-CH2CH2CH3}\) (butanenitrile) + \(\ce{Br-}\) 1
Two things you dropped last time, and they are separate marks. (1) Name the attacking species. The nucleophile is the cyanide ion, \(\ce{CN-}\) — an electron-rich species with a lone pair (here on carbon) that attacks an electron-deficient, δ+ carbon. Drawing the arrows without being able to say what is attacking scores the drawing mark and loses the reasoning ones. (2) Drop the sodium. With \(\ce{NaCN}\) or \(\ce{NaOH}\) the \(\ce{Na+}\) is a spectator ion — it takes no part in the mechanism and must never appear in it. The attacking nucleophile is \(\ce{CN-}\) / \(\ce{OH-}\) alone. If an examiner asks "identify the role of \(\ce{Na+}\)", the answer is the two words spectator ion.
(b) In a related compound, \(\ce{CN-}\) could in principle attack either the carbon of a C–Cl bond or the carbon of a C=O bond that sits next to a \(\ce{-CO2^{-}}\) group. Explain why the \(\ce{CN-}\) nucleophile attacks the carbon of the C–Cl bond rather than the carbon of the C=O bond. [2]
HCI Soln · N2019 · P2 · Q3(b)(iii)  ·  stem reconstructed from the key
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • The carbon of the C=O bond is less electron deficient due to the delocalisation over the \(\ce{-CO2^{-}}\) group 1
  • OR the \(\ce{CN-}\) nucleophile may be repelled by the negatively charged carboxylate group 1
  • Hence \(\ce{CN-}\) is more likely to attack the carbon of the C–Cl bond.
Read what this part is really asking. It wants you to state, in words, what a nucleophile is and what it goes for: an electron-rich species with a lone pair that seeks the most electron-deficient (most δ+) carbon in the molecule. Anything that reduces δ+ on a carbon (delocalisation) or repels an anion (a nearby negative charge) takes that carbon out of contention. You produced the word "nucleophile" as a guess last time with none of this reasoning attached — the word alone is worth nothing.

(d) Compound W is chloroacetyl chloride, \(\ce{ClCO-CH2-Cl}\). It contains an acyl chlorine and an alkyl chlorine. Compare the relative reactivity of the two chlorine atoms in W. Give two reasons. [2]
HCI Prelim · N2023 · P2 · Q2(f)(i)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
The acyl chloride Cl (Cl–C=O) is far more reactive than the alkyl Cl (\(\ce{CH2-Cl}\)) toward nucleophiles.
  • The carbonyl carbon is much more electron-deficient (δ+) because both the electronegative O and the Cl withdraw electron density, so it is more strongly attacked by nucleophiles 1
  • The sp² carbonyl carbon is trigonal planar with less steric hindrance, and the tetrahedral intermediate readily collapses to expel \(\ce{Cl-}\), a good leaving group; the alkyl C–Cl carbon is less electrophilic and reacts more slowly 1
Say "electron-deficient", not "electron-donating" or "electron-withdrawing" in the abstract. You muddled the two directions here last time. The comparison is only ever about how δ+ the attacked carbon is. On the acyl carbon, two electronegative atoms pull from the same carbon at once, so it is far more δ+ than a plain \(\ce{CH2-Cl}\) carbon, which has only one pulling on it. That single sentence is the answer. This is also why an acyl chloride gives an instant white precipitate with \(\ce{AgNO3}\) at room temperature while a chloroalkane needs warming.
3   SN1 or SN2 — read it off the rate and the profileTotal: 9
(a) Explain why 1-bromobutane and 2-bromo-2-methylpropane react with \(\ce{OH-}\) by different mechanisms. [2]
HCI Soln · N2020 · P3 · Q4(a)(i)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • 1-bromobutane undergoes \(\mathrm{S_N}2\): it has fewer alkyl groups attached to the electron-deficient carbon, so the attack by \(\ce{OH-}\) is less sterically hindered 1
  • 2-bromo-2-methylpropane undergoes \(\mathrm{S_N}1\): alkyl groups are electron-donating, so it forms a more stable tertiary carbocation 1
  • In \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) the C–Br bond breaks in the slow step to form the carbocation, and the electron-donating alkyl groups disperse the positive charge. 1-bromobutane does not undergo \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) as a primary carbocation is unstable.
Quote both factors, every time. There are exactly two, they pull in opposite directions, and the examiner wants one from each side: carbocation stability (rises 1° < 2° < 3°, so \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) rate rises 1° < 2° < 3°) and steric hindrance (rises 1° < 2° < 3°, so \(\mathrm{S_N}2\) rate rises 3° < 2° < 1°). Net: 1° → \(\mathrm{S_N}2\), 3° → \(\mathrm{S_N}1\), 2° → either. Naming the mechanism without naming both factors is half the marks.
(b) Explain the different stereochemical outcomes of the \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) and \(\mathrm{S_N}2\) reactions. [2]
HCI Soln · N2020 · P3 · Q4(a)(ii)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Same substrate, two mechanisms, two completely different products.
  • A racemic mixture is formed via \(\mathrm{S_N}1\): the positively charged carbon has a trigonal planar geometry, so \(\ce{OH-}\) has equal chances of attacking from top or bottom, giving equal amounts of both enantiomers 1
  • A single enantiomer is formed via \(\mathrm{S_N}2\): \(\ce{OH-}\) attacks the electron-deficient carbon from the side opposite to the bromine, resulting in an inverted stereochemistry; the breaking of C–Br and the forming of C–O occur simultaneously 1
(c) The energy profile for \(\ce{Cl-C(H)(D)(C6H5) + OH^{-} -> HO-C(H)(D)(C6H5) + Cl^{-}}\) has two peaks with an intermediate between them; Z labels the first (higher) peak. Which conclusions can be drawn?
  1  The resultant solution does not rotate plane-polarised light.
  2  An increase in \(\ce{[OH^{-}]}\) has no effect on the rate.
  3  The transition state at Z is \(\ce{[Cl\bond{...}C(C6H5)(H)(D)\bond{...}OH]^-}\). [1]
A  1 and 2 only     B  1 and 3 only     C  2 and 3 only     D  1, 2 and 3
HCI Prelim · N2023 · P1 · Q7
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
The shape of the profile hands you the mechanism before you read a word of the options.
A (1 and 2 only) 1
  • The two-peak profile (a carbocation intermediate) indicates an \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) mechanism at this benzylic carbon.
  • 1 is true: \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) proceeds via a planar carbocation, giving equal amounts of both enantiomers (racemic), so the product does not rotate plane-polarised light.
  • 2 is true: for \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) the rate = k[substrate], independent of \(\ce{[OH^{-}]}\).
  • 3 is false: point Z is the first (rate-determining) transition state, corresponding to C–Cl breaking to form the carbocation — \(\ce{OH-}\) is not yet bonded, so the drawn five-bonded transition state is wrong for Z (that is an \(\mathrm{S_N}2\)-type transition state).
The two rate equations, cold. \(\mathrm{S_N}2\) is rate = k[RX][Nu⁻], second order overall, bimolecular — two species in the one and only step, so changing either concentration changes the rate. \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) is rate = k[RX], first order, unimolecular — the rate-determining step is carbocation formation, which the nucleophile is not part of, so \(\ce{[Nu-]}\) does not appear. Read the profile the same way every time: count the peaks. Two peaks means an intermediate means \(\mathrm{S_N}1\), and the higher of the two peaks is the rds.
(d) E and F are the two chloro/hydroxy isomers of \(\ce{C8H9ClO}\)-type structure. When E reacts with \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\), the rate is independent of \(\ce{[OH^{-}]}\). Deduce whether E is the primary or the secondary chloroalkane, and explain. [2]
HCI Soln · N2019 · P3 · Q4(c)(ii)  ·  stem reconstructed from the key
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • Since the rate is independent of \(\ce{[OH^{-}]}\), the nucleophilic substitution occurs via an \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) mechanism 1
  • Hence E is the secondary chloroalkane, \(\ce{C6H5CHClCH3}\) (and F is \(\ce{C6H5CH(OH)CH3}\)), because the secondary / benzylic carbocation is more stable than a primary carbocation 1
Kinetics is a one-way street into the mechanism, and then into the structure. "Rate independent of \(\ce{[OH^{-}]}\)" is not a fact about \(\ce{OH-}\) — it means \(\ce{OH-}\) is absent from the rate-determining step, which only happens in \(\mathrm{S_N}1\), which only happens when the carbocation is stable enough, which tells you the carbon is 2°, 3° or benzylic. Three deductions, chained, from one line of data.
(e) Explain why P, a primary bromoalkene (\(\ce{CH3CH=CH-CH2Br}\)), is able to undergo the \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) mechanism. [2]
HCI Prelim · N2021 · P3 · Q1(c)(ii)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • The carbocation formed when P undergoes \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) is stabilised due to the delocalisation of the positive charge over the C=C (the π electrons of the C=C delocalise to the \(\ce{C+}\)) 1
  • Due to the delocalisation, the positive charge shifts to the third carbon to form an allylic carbocation (\(\ce{CH3-CH+-CH=CH2 <-> CH3-CH=CH-CH2+}\)) 1
"Primary means \(\mathrm{S_N}2\)" is a default, not a law. The real rule is carbocation stability decides, and alkyl groups are only one way to get it. Delocalisation beats induction every time: an allylic carbocation (next to C=C) and a benzylic one (next to a ring) are both stabilised by overlapping p orbitals, so \(\ce{C6H5CH2Cl}\) and \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2Br}\) go \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) despite being primary. The mirror-image exception is \(\ce{(CH3)3CCH2Cl}\): primary, but so sterically blocked that \(\mathrm{S_N}2\) cannot happen either.
4   Bond energy sets the rate — and proving which halogen it wasTotal: 10
(a) Three primary halogenoalkanes react with NaOH. N contains a C–I bond. O contains a C–Br bond and a highly branched, bulky alkyl group. M contains a C–Br bond with an unbranched chain. Arrange N, O and M in order of increasing rate of hydrolysis and explain your order. [3]
HCI Soln · N2022 · P3 · Q4(f)(iii)  ·  stem reconstructed from the key
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • The relative rate increases from O to M to N (i.e. O < M < N) 1
  • All three are primary halogenoalkanes undergoing nucleophilic substitution via \(\mathrm{S_N}2\). The C–I bond in N is weaker than the C–Br bond in M and O, so N has a lower activation energy and the fastest rate 1
  • The highly branched alkyl group in O is bulkier than in M, causing greater steric hindrance for nucleophilic attack, so O has the slowest rate 1
Bond energy, not electronegativity. You had this backwards and you were confident. The order of hydrolysis is R–I > R–Br > R–Cl, because C–I is the weakest bond (240 < 280 < 340 kJ mol⁻¹) and the C–X bond breaks in the rate-determining step whether the mechanism is \(\mathrm{S_N}1\) or \(\mathrm{S_N}2\). Reject "iodine is the least electronegative so the carbon is least δ+" — that argument is not wrong about the polarity, it is wrong about what controls the rate, and it predicts the exact opposite order. Quote the Data Booklet bond energies and the mark is unarguable. Same logic gives you why fluoroalkanes are inert: C–F is 485, far too strong to break.

HCI Soln · N2020 · P1 · Q21  ·  stem reconstructed from the key
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
C — chlorobenzene 1
  • Halogenoarenes are not reactive towards nucleophilic substitution due to steric hindrance and delocalisation of the lone pair on X into the benzene ring, giving partial double-bond character to the C–X bond; hence chlorobenzene does not react.

The aryl argument, in the exact words that score. The lone pair on the halogen delocalises into the ring, giving the C–X bond partial double-bond character, so it is shorter and stronger and will not break. This is the same structural argument that makes an aryl C–O bond survive when an alkyl C–O breaks, and that makes phenol resist substitution of its \(\ce{-OH}\).

The full procedure, in order, every single paper until it stops failing. (1) \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\), heat — hydrolyses the C–X and releases the halide: \(\ce{RX + OH- -> ROH + X-}\). The same anion can be a nucleophile or a base, and the product tells you which it was. If the anion ends up bonded to the carbon, it acted as a nucleophile and you had substitution. The nitrile route adds ONE carbon, not two. You said two. \(\ce{-CN}\) brings in one C and one N, so \(\ce{RBr -> RCN}\) is \(\ce{C_n -> C_{n+1}}\), and hydrolysis then turns \(\ce{RCN}\) into \(\ce{RCO2H}\) keeping that same carbon. This is the standard A-level way to grow a chain by one, and it is the only way, so any question where the product has one more carbon than the starting material and a halogen has gone is a KCN question.
