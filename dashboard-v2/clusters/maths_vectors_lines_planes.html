Vectors — Lines & Planes · Cluster 2 of 3

Maths H2 9758 · Topic 3.3 & 3.4 — Lines, Planes, Intersections, Distances & Reflections · Cluster 1 (basics, products, ratio theorem) is assumed

Format — teaching cluster: each card gives a worked Example so you learn the method, then a Practice so you implement it yourself. Attempt the Example cold first (no technique hint), reveal the solution to check, then do the Practice the same way. The archetype is at the bottom of each card, hidden until you've attempted. Only tests and gap clusters are questions-only, no examples.

The Practice is pitched harder than the Example — same core method, plus an extra part or a fused concept that pushes a different angle. It's meant to stretch you, not to repeat the worked example. Each Practice solution ends with a "why harder" note.
  • Every answer uses column vectors \( \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} \) — the mark scheme's presentation.
  • For every "distance / angle / intersection," first decide the geometry — line–line, line–plane, or plane–plane — the formula follows from that.
  • Marks are labelled inline at the step where they are earned.
The lines & planes toolkit — the formula follows the geometry
Line (vector form)\( \mathbf r = \mathbf a + \lambda\mathbf d \) — point \(\mathbf a\), direction \(\mathbf d\); from two points \( \mathbf d = \mathbf b - \mathbf a \). Cartesian: \( \frac{x-a_1}{d_1} = \frac{y-a_2}{d_2} = \frac{z-a_3}{d_3} \).
Plane (scalar-product form)\( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n = \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n \), normal \(\mathbf n\). Cartesian \( n_1x + n_2y + n_3z = d \). From 3 points: \( \mathbf n = (\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times(\mathbf c-\mathbf a) \).
Line \(\cap\) planeSubstitute the line's \((x,y,z)\) into the plane, solve for \(\lambda\), back-substitute for the point.
Line \(\cap\) lineEquate components. Consistent \(\to\) meet at a point; directions proportional \(\to\) parallel/identical; otherwise \(\to\) skew.
Plane \(\cap\) planeLine of intersection has direction \( \mathbf n_1\times\mathbf n_2 \); get one point by fixing a variable (e.g. set \(z=0\)) and solving the pair.
Angles — take the acute one, use \(|\cdot|\)Line–line \( \cos\theta = \dfrac{|\mathbf d_1\cdot\mathbf d_2|}{|\mathbf d_1||\mathbf d_2|} \).   Line–plane \( \sin\theta = \dfrac{|\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n|}{|\mathbf d||\mathbf n|} \) (sin — measured to the plane, not its normal).   Plane–plane \( \cos\theta = \dfrac{|\mathbf n_1\cdot\mathbf n_2|}{|\mathbf n_1||\mathbf n_2|} \).
DistancesPoint to plane \( \dfrac{|\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf n - d|}{|\mathbf n|} \).   Point \(P\) to line \( \dfrac{|\overrightarrow{AP}\times\mathbf d|}{|\mathbf d|} \).   Between skew lines \( \dfrac{|(\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1)\cdot(\mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2)|}{|\mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2|} \).
Foot of perpendicular \(F\)To a plane: \( F = P + t\mathbf n \), solve the plane equation for \(t\).   To a line: \( F = \mathbf a + \lambda\mathbf d \) with \( \overrightarrow{PF}\cdot\mathbf d = 0 \).
Reflection of \(P\)\( P' = 2F - P \), where \(F\) is the foot of the perpendicular from \(P\) onto the line/plane.

Question 1

Example
The line \( l \) has equation \( \mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \) and the plane \( p \) has equation \( 2x + y - 2z = 3 \).
(i) Find the position vector of the point where \( l \) meets \( p \). [4]
(ii) Find the acute angle between \( l \) and \( p \). [3]
Solution with mark scheme
Step 1 — (i) Substitute the line into the plane
A general point on \(l\) is \((1{+}2\lambda,\,-\lambda,\,2{+}2\lambda)\). Put it into \(2x+y-2z=3\). M1
\( 2(1{+}2\lambda) + (-\lambda) - 2(2{+}2\lambda) = 3 \).
Step 2 — Solve for \(\lambda\)
Expand and collect. A1
\( 2 + 4\lambda - \lambda - 4 - 4\lambda = 3 \Rightarrow -\lambda - 2 = 3 \Rightarrow \lambda = -5 \).
Step 3 — Back-substitute for the point
Put \(\lambda = -5\) into the line. M1 A1
\[ \mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 1-10 \\ 5 \\ 2-10 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -9 \\ 5 \\ -8 \end{pmatrix} \]
Step 4 — (ii) Angle: use \(\sin\theta\) for line–plane
\( \mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n = (2)(2)+(-1)(1)+(2)(-2) = -1 \); \( |\mathbf d| = 3,\ |\mathbf n| = 3 \). M1 A1
\[ \sin\theta = \frac{|\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n|}{|\mathbf d||\mathbf n|} = \frac{|-1|}{9} = \frac{1}{9} \]
Step 5 — Evaluate
A1
\( \theta = \sin^{-1}\tfrac{1}{9} = 6.4^\circ \) (1 d.p.).
Trap: line–plane uses \(\sin\theta\), not \(\cos\theta\). \( \cos\) would give the angle to the normal; the angle to the plane is \(90^\circ\) minus that, which is why it comes out as \(\sin\).

Practice
The line \( l \) has equation \( \mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \) and the plane \( p \) has equation \( 2x + y + 2z = 9 \).
(i) Find the position vector of the point \( Q \) where \( l \) meets \( p \). [3]
(ii) Find the acute angle between \( l \) and \( p \). [3]
(iii) Find the position vectors of the two points on \( l \) that are a distance of \( 9 \) units from \( Q \). [3]
Answer with mark scheme
Step 1 — (i) Substitute and solve
Point on \(l\): \((2{+}\lambda,\,1{-}2\lambda,\,2\lambda)\) into \(2x+y+2z=9\). M1 A1
\( 2(2{+}\lambda)+(1{-}2\lambda)+2(2\lambda) = 5 + 4\lambda = 9 \Rightarrow \lambda = 1 \Rightarrow Q = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \) A1
Step 2 — (ii) Angle
\( \mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n = (1)(2)+(-2)(1)+(2)(2) = 4 \); \( |\mathbf d| = |\mathbf n| = 3 \). M1 A1
\[ \sin\theta = \tfrac{4}{9} \Rightarrow \theta = 26.4^\circ \text{ (1 d.p.)} \] A1
Step 3 — (iii) Distance measured along the line
Moving from \(\lambda = 1\) by \(\Delta\lambda\) moves \(|\Delta\lambda|\,|\mathbf d|\) in space; \(|\mathbf d| = 3\). M1
\( |\lambda - 1|\cdot 3 = 9 \Rightarrow |\lambda - 1| = 3 \Rightarrow \lambda = 4 \text{ or } \lambda = -2 \).
Step 4 — The two points
A1 A1
\[ \lambda = 4:\ \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ -7 \\ 8 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda = -2:\ \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 5 \\ -4 \end{pmatrix} \]
Why harder: (iii) adds that distance along a line is \(|\Delta\lambda|\,|\mathbf d|\), not \(|\Delta\lambda|\) — the direction's magnitude scales it — and gives two answers (either way from \(Q\)). The example stopped at the intersection.

Archetype revealed
Line meets a plane; angle between a line and a plane.

Substitute the parametric point into the Cartesian plane, solve the single linear equation for \(\lambda\), back-substitute. The angle is \(\sin\theta = |\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n|/(|\mathbf d||\mathbf n|)\) — the standard line–plane trap is using \(\cos\).

Question 2

Example
Lines \( l_1: \mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \) and \( l_2: \mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} + \mu\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \).
(a) Show that \( l_1 \) and \( l_2 \) do not intersect and are not parallel. [4]
(b) Find the exact shortest distance between them. [4]
Solution with mark scheme
Step 1 — (a) Not parallel
Directions \(\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}\), \(\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}\) are not scalar multiples. B1
\(\therefore\) not parallel.
Step 2 — Try to intersect: equate components
\(1{+}\lambda = 2,\ \ 2 = 3{+}\mu,\ \ 1{+}\lambda = 4{+}\mu\). M1
From the first two: \(\lambda = 1,\ \mu = -1\).
Step 3 — Test the third equation
Consistency check. M1
Third: \(1{+}1 = 2\) but \(4{+}(-1) = 3\); \(2 \neq 3\), inconsistent.
Step 4 — Conclude
A1
No common point and not parallel \(\Rightarrow\) the lines are skew.
Step 5 — (b) Common perpendicular direction
\( \mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2 \). M1
\[ \mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},\quad |\mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2| = \sqrt{3} \]
Step 6 — Project the connecting vector
\( \mathbf a_2 - \mathbf a_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\3\end{pmatrix} \). M1 A1
\[ d = \frac{|(\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1)\cdot(\mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2)|}{|\mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2|} = \frac{|-1-1+3|}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} \] A1
Trap: two lines being non-parallel is not enough to be skew — you must also show they don't meet (the failed third equation). Non-parallel lines in 3-D are either skew or intersecting.

Practice
Lines \( l_1: \mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \) and \( l_2: \mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \mu\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \).
(a) Show that \( l_1 \) and \( l_2 \) are skew. [4]
(b) Find the acute angle between \( l_1 \) and \( l_2 \). [2]
(c) Find the exact shortest distance between them. [4]
Answer with mark scheme
Step 1 — (a) Not parallel
B1
\(\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}\) are not proportional \(\Rightarrow\) not parallel.
Step 2 — Equate and test
M1 M1
\(1{+}2\lambda = \mu,\ \ \lambda = 1,\ \ 1 = \mu\). From \(y\): \(\lambda = 1\); from \(z\): \(\mu = 1\). Then \(x\): \(1{+}2 = 3 \neq \mu = 1\), inconsistent.
Step 3 — Conclude
A1
No common point and not parallel \(\Rightarrow\) skew.
Step 4 — (b) Angle between the lines (use \(\cos\), directions)
\( \mathbf d_1\cdot\mathbf d_2 = (2)(1)+(1)(0)+(0)(1) = 2 \); \( |\mathbf d_1| = \sqrt{5},\ |\mathbf d_2| = \sqrt{2} \). M1
\[ \cos\theta = \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}\,\sqrt{2}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{10}} \Rightarrow \theta = 50.8^\circ \text{ (1 d.p.)} \] A1
Step 5 — (c) Shortest distance
\( \mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\-1\end{pmatrix},\ |\cdot| = \sqrt{6} \); \( \mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1 = \begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix} \). M1 M1 A1
\[ d = \frac{|(-1)(1)+(1)(-2)+(-1)(-1)|}{\sqrt{6}} = \frac{|-2|}{\sqrt{6}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{6}} = \frac{\sqrt{6}}{3} \] A1
Why harder: (b) folds in a different formula on the same figure — angle between lines uses \(\cos\) with the directions \(\mathbf d_1,\mathbf d_2\); the shortest distance in (c) uses the cross product \(\mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2\). Don't mix the two.

Skew lines — proof + shortest distance.

Two-part standard: (1) show not parallel (directions not proportional) AND not intersecting (third equation fails after solving the first two); (2) shortest distance \( = |(\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1)\cdot(\mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2)|/|\mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2| \). The cross product is the common perpendicular direction.

Question 3

Example
The points \( A(1,0,0) \), \( B(0,2,0) \), \( C(0,0,3) \) lie on a plane \( p \), and \( O \) is the origin.
(a) Find the Cartesian equation of \( p \). [3]
(b) Find the perpendicular distance from \( O \) to \( p \). [2]
Solution with mark scheme
Step 1 — (a) Two direction vectors in the plane
M1
\( \overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\0\end{pmatrix},\ \overrightarrow{AC} = \begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\\3\end{pmatrix} \).
Step 2 — Normal via cross product
A1
\[ \mathbf n = \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \]
Step 3 — Fit through \(A\)
\( d = \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf a = 6(1) = 6 \). A1
\[ 6x + 3y + 2z = 6 \]
Step 4 — (b) Point-to-plane distance
Formula \(\dfrac{|\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf n - d|}{|\mathbf n|}\), naming each symbol: P = the point you're measuring from = \(O(0,0,0)\); n = the normal, read straight off the plane's coefficients = \((6,3,2)\); d = the plane's constant = \(6\); \(|\mathbf n|\) = magnitude of the normal. M1 A1
\[ \frac{|6(0)+3(0)+2(0) - 6|}{\sqrt{36+9+4}} = \frac{6}{7} \]
Trap: the distance formula needs the plane written as \( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n = d \) with the \(d\) on the right — it is \(|\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf n - d|/|\mathbf n|\). Forgetting to subtract \(d\) (here \(6\)) is the common slip.

(a) Find the Cartesian equation of \( p \). [3]
(b) Find the exact perpendicular distance from the origin \( O \) to \( p \). [2]
(c) The point \( D(k,\,1,\,k) \) also lies on \( p \). Find \( k \). [2]
Answer with mark scheme
Step 1 — (a) Directions and normal
\( \overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix},\ \overrightarrow{AC} = \begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\\2\end{pmatrix} \). M1
\[ \mathbf n = \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ -3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \ \parallel\ \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \]
Step 2 — Fit through \(A(1,1,0)\)
\( d = 2(1)+3(1)+1(0) = 5 \). A1
\[ 2x + 3y + z = 5 \]
Step 3 — (b) Distance from \(O\)
Same formula: P = the point measured from = \(O(0,0,0)\); n = \((2,3,1)\) off the coefficients; d = the constant = \(5\); \(|\mathbf n| = \sqrt{2^2+3^2+1^2}\). M1 A1
\[ \frac{|2(0)+3(0)+0 - 5|}{\sqrt{4+9+1}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{14}} = \frac{5\sqrt{14}}{14} \]
Step 4 — (c) Put \(D\) into the plane
\(D\) lies on \(p\) \(\Leftrightarrow\) its coordinates satisfy \(2x+3y+z=5\). M1
\( 2k + 3(1) + k = 5 \Rightarrow 3k = 2 \Rightarrow k = \tfrac{2}{3} \). A1
Why harder: (c) reverses the logic of (a) — instead of building the plane from points, you test whether a point lies on a known plane by substituting it in. That "does this point satisfy the equation?" check is the same move as line\(\cap\)plane, reused.

Normal \( = (\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times(\mathbf c-\mathbf a) \); fix \(d\) by substituting any one of the three points; then the point-to-plane distance formula. Simplify the normal (divide out a common factor) before fitting \(d\) — it keeps the numbers small.

Question 4

Example
The point \( P \) has position vector \( \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \) and the plane \( p \) has equation \( x + 2y + 2z = 1 \).
(a) Find the position vector of the foot of the perpendicular \( F \) from \( P \) to \( p \). [4]
(b) Hence find the position vector of the reflection of \( P \) in \( p \). [2]
Solution with mark scheme
Step 1 — (a) Walk from \(P\) along the normal
\( \mathbf n = \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix} \), so \( F = P + t\mathbf n = (3{+}t,\ 1{+}2t,\ 2{+}2t) \). M1
Step 2 — \(F\) lies in the plane
Substitute into \(x+2y+2z=1\). M1
\( (3{+}t) + 2(1{+}2t) + 2(2{+}2t) = 1 \Rightarrow 9 + 9t = 1 \Rightarrow t = -\tfrac{8}{9} \).
Step 3 — The foot \(F\)
M1 A1
\[ F = \begin{pmatrix} 3-\tfrac{8}{9} \\ 1-\tfrac{16}{9} \\ 2-\tfrac{16}{9} \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{9}\begin{pmatrix} 19 \\ -7 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \]
Step 4 — (b) Reflect using \( P' = 2F - P \)
\(F\) is the midpoint of \(PP'\). M1 A1
\[ P' = 2F - P = \frac{1}{9}\begin{pmatrix} 38 \\ -14 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{9}\begin{pmatrix} 11 \\ -23 \\ -14 \end{pmatrix} \]
Trap: the reflection is \( P' = 2F - P \) (equivalently \( P + 2t\mathbf n \)), not \( F + t\mathbf n \). \(F\) is only halfway; you travel the same displacement \(t\mathbf n\) again.

Practice
The point \( P \) has position vector \( \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \) and the plane \( p \) has equation \( x + y + z = 3 \).
(a) Find the foot of the perpendicular \( F \) from \( P \) to \( p \). [4]
(b) Hence find the reflection \( P' \) of \( P \) in \( p \). [2]
(c) Find the exact shortest distance from \( P \) to \( p \), and hence write down the distance \( PP' \). [2]
Answer with mark scheme
Step 1 — (a) Along the normal
\( \mathbf n = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix} \), \( F = (1{+}t,\ 2{+}t,\ 3{+}t) \). M1
Step 2 — Substitute into the plane
M1
\( (1{+}t)+(2{+}t)+(3{+}t) = 3 \Rightarrow 6 + 3t = 3 \Rightarrow t = -1 \).
Step 3 — The foot
M1 A1
\[ F = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \]
Step 4 — (b) Reflection
M1 A1
\[ P' = 2F - P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \]
Step 5 — (c) Distances from the same \(t\)
\( |PF| = |t|\,|\mathbf n| \), and \(P'\) is as far again beyond \(F\). M1
\( |PF| = |-1|\,\sqrt{3} = \sqrt{3} \), so \( PP' = 2|PF| = 2\sqrt{3} \). A1
Why harder: (c) shows the same \(t\) you already found gives the shortest distance for free — \( |PF| = |t|\,|\mathbf n| \) — no separate distance formula needed, and \(PP' = 2|PF|\) because \(F\) is the midpoint of \(PP'\).

\( F = P + t\mathbf n \) with \(t\) fixed by putting \(F\) in the plane; reflection \( P' = 2F - P \). This same \(F\)-then-\(2F{-}P\) pattern gives the shortest distance too: \( |PF| = |t|\,|\mathbf n| \).

Question 5

Example
The planes \( p_1: x + y + z = 1 \) and \( p_2: x - 2y + z = 3 \).
(a) Find a vector equation of their line of intersection. [4]
(b) Find the acute angle between the two planes, and comment. [3]
Solution with mark scheme
Step 1 — (a) Direction of the line
The line lies in both planes, so its direction is perpendicular to both normals. M1 A1
\[ \mathbf n_1\times\mathbf n_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}3\\0\\-3\end{pmatrix} \ \parallel\ \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix} \]
Step 2 — One common point (set \(z = 0\))
Solve \(x+y=1,\ x-2y=3\). M1
Subtract: \(3y = -2 \Rightarrow y = -\tfrac{2}{3},\ x = \tfrac{5}{3}\).
Step 3 — Assemble the line
A1
\[ \mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 5/3 \\ -2/3 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \]
Step 4 — (b) Angle between planes
\( \mathbf n_1\cdot\mathbf n_2 = 1 - 2 + 1 = 0 \). M1 A1
\( \cos\theta = 0 \Rightarrow \theta = 90^\circ \).
Step 5 — Comment
A1
The two planes are perpendicular.
Trap: the angle between planes uses the normals (\(\cos\theta = |\mathbf n_1\cdot\mathbf n_2|/(|\mathbf n_1||\mathbf n_2|)\)) — the same formula as line–line, not line–plane. Don't slip into \(\sin\).

A1

Question 6

Example
The line \( l \) has equation \( \mathbf r = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} \) and \( P \) is the point \( (4,0,3) \).
(a) Find the position vector of the foot of the perpendicular \( F \) from \( P \) to \( l \). [4]
(b) Hence find the exact distance from \( P \) to \( l \). [3]
Solution with mark scheme
Step 1 — (a) General foot on the line
\( F = (1{+}2\lambda,\ 1{+}\lambda,\ 1{-}2\lambda) \). M1
\( \overrightarrow{PF} = (2\lambda{-}3,\ \lambda{+}1,\ -2\lambda{-}2) \).
Step 2 — Perpendicularity condition
\( \overrightarrow{PF}\cdot\mathbf d = 0 \). M1
\( 2(2\lambda{-}3) + (\lambda{+}1) - 2(-2\lambda{-}2) = 9\lambda - 1 = 0 \Rightarrow \lambda = \tfrac{1}{9} \).
Step 3 — The foot
M1 A1
\[ F = \frac{1}{9}\begin{pmatrix} 11 \\ 10 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix} \]
Step 4 — (b) Distance \( = |\overrightarrow{PF}| \)
M1 A1
\( \overrightarrow{PF} = \tfrac{1}{9}\begin{pmatrix} -25 \\ 10 \\ -20 \end{pmatrix} \). \[ |\overrightarrow{PF}| = \tfrac{1}{9}\sqrt{625+100+400} = \tfrac{1}{9}\sqrt{1125} = \frac{15\sqrt{5}}{9} = \frac{5\sqrt{5}}{3} \] A1
Trap: the condition is \( \overrightarrow{PF}\cdot\mathbf d = 0 \), using the line's direction \(\mathbf d\) — not the normal, and not \( \overrightarrow{OF}\cdot\mathbf d \). Check: the answer matches the direct formula \( |\overrightarrow{AP}\times\mathbf d|/|\mathbf d| \).
