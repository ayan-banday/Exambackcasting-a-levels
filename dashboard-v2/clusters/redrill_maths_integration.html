Card 1 · Trigonometric substitution

The identity you blanked on, and the change-of-limits ritual

What broke: you set up \(x = a+\tan\theta\) but stalled on two things. You couldn't recall \(1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta\), so the surd wouldn't simplify. And after substituting you evaluated the definite integral against the old \(x\)-limits instead of the new \(\theta\)-limits.
The method, retaught When you see a surd of a quadratic — \(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\), \(\sqrt{a^2+x^2}\), or \(\sqrt{x^2-a^2}\) — substitute a trig function to kill the surd. Which substitution and which identity is fixed by the sign pattern:
\(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\)  let \(x=a\sin\theta\)  →  \(a^2-x^2=a^2\cos^2\theta\)  (uses \(1-\sin^2\theta=\cos^2\theta\))
\(\sqrt{a^2+x^2}\)  let \(x=a\tan\theta\)  →  \(a^2+x^2=a^2\sec^2\theta\)  (uses \(1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta\) — the one you forgot)
\(\sqrt{x^2-a^2}\)  let \(x=a\sec\theta\)  →  \(x^2-a^2=a^2\tan^2\theta\)  (uses \(\sec^2\theta-1=\tan^2\theta\))
The four-step ritual — step 2 is non-negotiable for a definite integral:
  1. Substitute \(x=\ldots\); differentiate to get \(\mathrm dx=\ldots\,\mathrm d\theta\).
  2. CHANGE BOTH LIMITS. Put each \(x\)-limit into the substitution and solve for \(\theta\). Write the new \(\theta\)-limits down immediately, before you integrate.
  3. Simplify the surd with the matching identity; fix the sign of the root from the stated \(\theta\)-range (e.g. \(\sqrt{\cos^2\theta}=\cos\theta\) when \(\cos\theta\ge 0\)).
  4. Integrate in \(\theta\) and evaluate at the new limits. You never convert back to \(x\) — that is only for indefinite integrals.
Worked example — the correct method
HCI Tutorial 6A Q4(c)

Find the exact value of \(\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\sqrt3}\frac{x}{\sqrt{4-x^2}}\,\mathrm dx\), using the substitution \(x=2\sin\theta\).

1 · Substitute & differentiate
\(x=2\sin\theta\) is the substitution; differentiate w.r.t. \(\theta\). \[ x=2\sin\theta \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}=2\cos\theta. \]
2 · Change both limits (the ritual)
Put each old \(x\)-limit into \(x=2\sin\theta\) and solve for \(\theta\). \[ x=1\Rightarrow 2\sin\theta=1\Rightarrow\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{6}; \qquad x=\sqrt3\Rightarrow 2\sin\theta=\sqrt3\Rightarrow\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{3}. \]
3 · Kill the surd, then integrate
\(\sqrt{4-4\sin^2\theta}=\sqrt{4\cos^2\theta}=2\cos\theta\) since \(\cos\theta\ge0\) for \(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\le\theta\le\tfrac{\pi}{3}\). \[ \int_{1}^{\sqrt3}\frac{x}{\sqrt{4-x^2}}\,\mathrm dx=\int_{\pi/6}^{\pi/3}\frac{2\sin\theta}{2\cos\theta}\,(2\cos\theta)\,\mathrm d\theta=2\int_{\pi/6}^{\pi/3}\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta \] \[ =2\Big[-\cos\theta\Big]_{\pi/6}^{\pi/3}=2\!\left[-\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right]=\sqrt3-1. \]

Card 2 · Odd powers of secant

Cyclic integration by parts — let \(I\) be the integral, then solve for \(I\)

What broke: you reached \(\int\sec^3\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\) and froze. An odd power of \(\sec\) has no spare \(\sec^2\theta\) to become a \(u\)-substitution, so the trick is different: cyclic integration by parts — call the integral \(I\), integrate by parts once, watch the same integral reappear, then solve for \(I\) algebraically.
The method, retaught
  • Trigger: an odd power of \(\sec\) (\(\sec^3,\sec^5,\dots\)) on its own → cyclic IBP.
  • Split \(\sec^3\theta=\sec\theta\cdot\sec^2\theta\), and choose \(v'=\sec^2\theta\;(\Rightarrow v=\tan\theta)\) with \(u=\sec\theta\;(\Rightarrow u'=\sec\theta\tan\theta)\).
  • Use \(\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta-1\) to turn the leftover integral back into the original \(I\) plus a lower, known integral.
  • Collect: \(I\) now sits on both sides. You get \(2I=[\,uv\text{ term}\,]+\int\sec\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\); divide by 2.
  • Bank the base result (MF26): \(\displaystyle\int\sec\theta\,\mathrm d\theta=\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|+C\).
Worked example — the correct method
HCI Tutorial 6A Q5(a)

By writing \(\sec^3x\) as \(\sec x\cdot\sec^2x\), find \(\displaystyle\int\sec^3x\,\mathrm dx\) using integration by parts.

By parts, splitting off \(\sec^2x\)
\(u=\sec x\Rightarrow u'=\sec x\tan x\); \(\ v'=\sec^2x\Rightarrow v=\tan x\). \[ \int\sec^3x\,\mathrm dx=\int\sec x\,\sec^2x\,\mathrm dx=\sec x\tan x-\int\sec x\tan^2x\,\mathrm dx. \]
Turn \(\tan^2x\) back into the original integral
Use \(\tan^2x=\sec^2x-1\), so \(\int\sec x\tan^2x\,\mathrm dx=\int\sec^3x\,\mathrm dx-\int\sec x\,\mathrm dx\). \[ =\sec x\tan x-\int\big(\sec^3x-\sec x\big)\,\mathrm dx. \]
Collect the \(I\)'s and solve
The integral \(I=\int\sec^3x\,\mathrm dx\) reappears; move it to the left. \[ 2\int\sec^3x\,\mathrm dx=\sec x\tan x+\int\sec x\,\mathrm dx \] \[ \therefore\ \int\sec^3x\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac12\sec x\tan x+\tfrac12\ln|\sec x+\tan x|+C. \]

Card 3 · Even powers of secant

Split off one \(\sec^2\theta\), substitute \(u=\tan\theta\) — the method you were never shown

What broke: you had never been taught this one, so \(\int\sec^4\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\) looked like the \(\sec^3\) problem and you reached for parts. It isn't. An even power of \(\sec\) has a spare \(\sec^2\theta\) that is exactly \(\mathrm d(\tan\theta)\) — so it is a clean substitution, no parts at all.
The method, retaught (learn this cleanly)
  • Trigger: an even power of \(\sec\) (\(\sec^4,\sec^6,\dots\)) → peel off one \(\sec^2\theta\) to be the \(\mathrm du\).
  • Rewrite the rest in \(\tan\theta\): \(\sec^4\theta=\sec^2\theta\cdot\sec^2\theta=(1+\tan^2\theta)\sec^2\theta\).
  • Substitute \(u=\tan\theta\Rightarrow\mathrm du=\sec^2\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\). The integral becomes a polynomial in \(u\).
  • Integrate the polynomial, then substitute back \(u=\tan\theta\).
Why even and odd split apart: an odd power (\(\sec^3\)) leaves a lone \(\sec\theta\) that is not \(\mathrm d(\tan\theta)\) — no substitution is available, so odd needs cyclic parts (Card 2). An even power always keeps one \(\sec^2\theta\) spare for \(\mathrm du\).
Worked example — the correct method

Card 4 · MF27 standard-form recognition

The two booklet forms you walked past: \(\sin^{-1}\) and \(\tan^{-1}\)

What broke: twice you tried to force an integral that was simply a formula-booklet standard form. You didn't recognise \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}\to\sin^{-1}\!\dfrac{x}{a}\) or \(\dfrac{1}{a^2+x^2}\to\dfrac1a\tan^{-1}\!\dfrac{x}{a}\).
The reflex, drilled
Can't integrate directly? → Is it \(\dfrac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\) (log) or \(f'[f]^n\) (power)? → Would parts help? → Still stuck, and it's a bare \(1\) over a quadratic, or a surd of a quadratic? → It's an MF27 standard form. Go to the booklet.
\(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}\,\mathrm dx=\sin^{-1}\!\frac{x}{a}+C\)  — surd of \(a^2-x^2\) on the bottom → arcsin
\(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{a^2+x^2}\,\mathrm dx=\frac1a\tan^{-1}\!\frac{x}{a}+C\)  — quadratic \(a^2+x^2\) on the bottom, no surd → arctan
Setup rule: make the coefficient of \(x^2\) equal to \(1\) first. If you end up with \((kx)^2\), remember to differentiate \(kx\) — so divide by \(k\).

Card 5 · Area & volume of revolution

Applications — chaining it all, rotating about the \(y\)-axis, and the radians trap

What broke: the applications question chained every technique above — and two things collapsed. You rotated about the \(y\)-axis without making \(x\) the subject first, and you left the calculator in degrees for the inverse-trig value.
The method, retaught
  • Area: \(\displaystyle\int y\,\mathrm dx\) (strips in \(x\)) or \(\displaystyle\int x\,\mathrm dy\) (strips in \(y\)); between two curves, \(\displaystyle\int(\text{top}-\text{bottom})\).
  • Volume about the \(x\)-axis: \(V=\pi\displaystyle\int y^2\,\mathrm dx\).
  • Volume about the \(y\)-axis: \(V=\pi\displaystyle\int x^2\,\mathrm dy\) — so you must make \(x^2\) the subject first and integrate in \(y\), between the \(y\)-limits.
  • Symmetry short-cut: a region symmetric about the axis rotated through \(\pi\) equals one side rotated through \(2\pi\).
  • Capped / hollow solids: cylinder \(-\displaystyle\int(\text{curve part})\), with cylinder \(=\pi r^2h\) (\(r=\) largest \(x\), \(h=\) largest \(y\)).
  • Radians: any \(\sin^{-1}\) or \(\tan^{-1}\) value you type into the GC must be in radian mode. This is where you lost a whole question.

HCI Tutorial 6B Q8 · A Level N95/I/17

The curve \(C\) is \(y=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4-x^2}}\) for \(-1\le x\le1\); region \(R\) is bounded by \(C\), the \(x\)-axis and \(x=\pm1\).
