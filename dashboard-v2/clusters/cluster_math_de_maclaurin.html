H2 MATHEMATICS 9758

Differential Equations + Maclaurin Series — Drill Cluster
9758 · H2 · Paper 1 & 2 (Pure) · 11 questions · ~2h · 98 marks
Two topics, one sheet, because 9758 keeps testing them together. A differential equation is just a relation between \(y\) and its derivatives — and the way you build a Maclaurin series is by differentiating a relation between \(y\) and its derivatives over and over. Questions 1, 2 and 11 are that exact join: you are handed a DE and asked for a series, not a solution. The rest of the sheet closes every learning outcome across both topics — direct integration, separables, substitutions, forming a DE from words, families of solution curves, long-run behaviour, the standard MF26 series, binomial expansions with their validity ranges, small-angle work, and using a series to approximate an integral you then check on the GC. Every question is a real past-paper, prelim or tutorial question; every revealed solution reproduces the worked solution verbatim, with each mark tagged (1) on the line it is earned. Say your confidence out loud before every reveal — that line feeds the gap tracker.
Two things before you start.  (1) Set your calculator to radians — every \(\tan^{-1}\), \(\cot\), \(\pi\) and small-angle \(\theta\) on this sheet is in radians. (2) Every time you meet an algebraic fraction, check the degrees first. If the numerator's degree is greater than or equal to the denominator's, the fraction is improper — divide it out before you split it, integrate it or expand it. Q3 and Q4 both punish you for skipping that check.

Question 1 [9]
A-Level 2018 P2 Q1 · RI 2019 Prelim P2 Q2(a)
(a) The curve \(y=\mathrm f(x)\) passes through the point \((0,\,69)\) and has gradient given by \(\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\left(\tfrac13 y-15\right)^{1/3}\). Find \(\mathrm f(x)\). [4] (b) A different curve \(y=\mathrm g(x)\) passes through the point \((0,\,81)\) and has gradient given by \(\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\left(\tfrac13 y-15x\right)^{1/3}\).    (i) Find the first three non-zero terms in the Maclaurin series for \(y\). [4]    (ii) Deduce the equation of the tangent to the curve \(y=\mathrm g(x)\) at the point where \(x=0\). [1]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim worked solution
(a) the right-hand side is a function of \(y\) alone — invert and integrate with respect to \(y\)
Write \(u=\tfrac13y-15\), so \(\dfrac{\mathrm du}{\mathrm dy}=\tfrac13\) and \(\displaystyle\int u^{-1/3}\,\mathrm dy=3\int u^{-1/3}\,\mathrm du=3\cdot\dfrac{u^{2/3}}{2/3}=\tfrac92u^{2/3}\). \[ \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dy}=\left(\tfrac13y-15\right)^{-1/3}\ \Longrightarrow\ x=\int\left(\tfrac13y-15\right)^{-1/3}\mathrm dy=\frac92\left(\tfrac13y-15\right)^{2/3}+C. \] (1)(1) The curve passes through \((0,\,69)\), so \(x=0\) when \(y=69\); note \(\tfrac13(69)-15=8\) and \(8^{2/3}=4\). \[ 0=\tfrac92(8)^{2/3}+C=\tfrac92(4)+C=18+C\ \Longrightarrow\ C=-18. \] (1) \[ x=\tfrac92\left(\tfrac13y-15\right)^{2/3}-18\ \Rightarrow\ \tfrac29(x+18)=\left(\tfrac13y-15\right)^{2/3}\ \Rightarrow\ \tfrac13y-15=\left(\tfrac29x+4\right)^{3/2} \] \[ \mathrm f(x)=3\left(\tfrac29x+4\right)^{3/2}+45. \] (1)
(b)(i) do not solve this one — differentiate the DE itself, then evaluate at \(x=0\)
\(\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\left(\tfrac13y-15x\right)^{1/3}\). Differentiate the right-hand side by the chain rule, remembering \(y\) is a function of \(x\) so \(\tfrac13y-15x\) differentiates to \(\tfrac13\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}-15\). \[ \frac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}=\tfrac13\left(\tfrac13y-15x\right)^{-2/3}\left(\tfrac13\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}-15\right). \] (1)(1) At \(x=0\): \(y=81\), so \(\tfrac13y-15x=\tfrac13(81)-0=27\). \[ \left.\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}\right|_{x=0}=27^{1/3}=3;\qquad \left.\frac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}\right|_{x=0}=\tfrac13(27)^{-2/3}\big(\tfrac13(3)-15\big)=\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac19\cdot(-14)=-\tfrac{14}{27}. \] (1) \[ y=81+3x+\frac{-\tfrac{14}{27}}{2!}x^2+\cdots=81+3x-\frac{7}{27}x^2+\cdots \] (1)
(b)(ii) the tangent at \(x=0\) is the linear part of the series
\[ y=81+3x. \] (1)
Fuses: the same-shaped gradient function goes two different ways — in (a) the right-hand side depends only on \(y\), so you invert to \(\tfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dy}\) and integrate; in (b) the extra \(-15x\) makes it unsolvable by separation, so you differentiate the DE repeatedly and build a Maclaurin series instead. Reading which of the two a question wants is the whole skill.

Question 2 [11]
RI Prelim 9758/2023 P2 Q3
It is given that \(y=e^{x}\cos 3x\). (a) Show that \(\dfrac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}=2\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}-10y\). Hence find the first four non-zero terms of the Maclaurin expansion of \(e^{x}\cos 3x\). [6] (b) Verify that the same result in part (a) is obtained if the standard series expansions for \(e^{x}\) and \(\cos x\) are used. [2] (c) Hence show that \(\ln\!\left(1+e^{x}\cos 3x\right)\) can be expressed as \(\ln 2+\tfrac12 x-\tfrac{17}{8}x^2+\cdots\) [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim worked solution
(a) product rule twice, feeding each result back in terms of \(y\) and \(\tfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}\)
The trick is never to leave \(e^{x}\sin 3x\) standing on its own — replace it using the line above, so the final relation contains only \(y\) and its derivatives. \[ \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=e^{x}\cos 3x-3e^{x}\sin 3x=y-3e^{x}\sin 3x. \] (1) \[ \frac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}=\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}-3e^{x}\sin 3x-9e^{x}\cos 3x=\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}+\left(\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}-y\right)-9y \] (1) \[ =2\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}-10y\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1) Differentiate the shown result once more to reach the third derivative, then evaluate everything at \(x=0\). \[ \frac{\mathrm d^3y}{\mathrm dx^3}=2\frac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}-10\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}. \] (1) \[ x=0:\quad y=1,\quad \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=1,\quad \frac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}=2(1)-10(1)=-8,\quad \frac{\mathrm d^3y}{\mathrm dx^3}=2(-8)-10(1)=-26. \] (1) \[ y=1+x+\frac{-8}{2!}x^2+\frac{-26}{3!}x^3+\cdots=1+x-4x^2-\frac{13}{3}x^3+\cdots \] (1)
(b) multiply the two MF26 standard series and collect
From MF26: \(e^{x}=1+x+\tfrac{x^2}{2!}+\tfrac{x^3}{3!}+\cdots\) and \(\cos x=1-\tfrac{x^2}{2!}+\cdots\), so \(\cos 3x=1-\tfrac{(3x)^2}{2}+\cdots=1-\tfrac92x^2+\cdots\) \[ e^{x}\cos 3x=\left(1+x+\tfrac12x^2+\tfrac16x^3+\cdots\right)\left(1-\tfrac92x^2+\cdots\right) \] (1) \[ =1+x+\left(\tfrac12-\tfrac92\right)x^2+\left(\tfrac16-\tfrac92\right)x^3+\cdots=1+x-4x^2-\tfrac{13}{3}x^3+\cdots\quad(\text{verified}). \] (1)
(c) substitute the series into the logarithm, then force the bracket into \(\ln(1+u)\) form
Only terms up to \(x^2\) are needed. \(1+e^{x}\cos 3x\approx 2+x-4x^2\); factor out the 2 so the standard series \(\ln(1+u)=u-\tfrac{u^2}{2}+\cdots\) applies with \(u=\tfrac x2-2x^2\). \[ \ln\!\left(1+e^{x}\cos 3x\right)\approx\ln\!\left(2+x-4x^2\right)=\ln\!\Big[2\left(1+\tfrac x2-2x^2\right)\Big]=\ln 2+\ln\!\left(1+\left(\tfrac x2-2x^2\right)\right) \] (1) \[ =\ln 2+\left(\tfrac x2-2x^2\right)-\tfrac12\left(\tfrac x2-2x^2\right)^2+\cdots=\ln 2+\tfrac12x-2x^2-\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{x^2}{4}+\cdots \] (1) \[ =\ln 2+\tfrac12x-\tfrac{17}{8}x^2+\cdots\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)
Fuses: a second-order DE relation is manufactured out of a product, differentiated once more to give \(\tfrac{\mathrm d^3y}{\mathrm dx^3}\), then cross-checked against the MF26 standard series — and the answer is immediately fed as the \(u\) inside \(\ln(1+u)\). Three separate Maclaurin routes on one stem.

Question 3 [9]
AJC 2017 C2 BT2 P1 Q10
[The volume of a circular cone with base radius \(r\) and height \(h\) is \(\tfrac13\pi r^2h\).] An open-top conical tank has a radius equal to its height. Initially the tank is empty. Water is added at a rate of \(\pi\) cubic metres per hour, and water evaporates from the tank at a rate proportional to the surface area of the water. At time \(t\) hours, the volume of water in the tank is \(V\) cubic metres and its depth is \(h\) metres. (i) Given that there is no change in the volume of water when the depth of water is 5 metres, express \(\dfrac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}\) in terms of \(h\). [2] (ii) Show that \(\dfrac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}=\dfrac{25-h^2}{25h^2}\). [2] (iii) By solving the differential equation in part (ii), show that \(\dfrac52\ln\!\left(\dfrac{5+h}{5-h}\right)-h=\dfrac{1}{25}t\). [4] (iv) What should the height of the conical tank be so that the tank will never be filled up? [1]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim worked solution
(i) rate of change of volume = rate in − rate out
Let \(r\) be the radius of the water surface. Since the height and radius of the tank are the same, \(r=h\), so the surface area of the water is \(\pi r^2=\pi h^2\). \[ \frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=\pi-k\pi r^2=\pi-k\pi h^2. \] (1) \[ h=5:\ \frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=\pi-k\pi(5^2)=0\ \Rightarrow\ k=\tfrac{1}{25}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=\pi-\tfrac{1}{25}\pi h^2=\frac{\pi(25-h^2)}{25}. \] (1)
(ii) chain the rates through \(\dfrac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dh}\)
\[ V=\tfrac13\pi r^2h=\tfrac13\pi h^3\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dh}=\pi h^2. \] (1) \[ \frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dh}\times\frac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\pi(25-h^2)/25}{\pi h^2}=\frac{25-h^2}{25h^2}\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)
(iii) separate — and divide out the improper fraction before integrating
The check that earns the mark: in \(\dfrac{h^2}{25-h^2}\) the numerator has degree 2 and the denominator has degree 2, so it is improper. It cannot be integrated as it stands and it has no partial-fraction split until you divide. Long division gives \(\dfrac{h^2}{25-h^2}=-1+\dfrac{25}{25-h^2}\). \[ \frac{\mathrm dt}{\mathrm dh}=\frac{25h^2}{25-h^2}\ \Longrightarrow\ t=25\int\frac{h^2}{25-h^2}\,\mathrm dh=25\int\left(-1+\frac{25}{25-h^2}\right)\mathrm dh. \] (1) MF26 standard form: \(\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{a^2-x^2}\,\mathrm dx=\frac{1}{2a}\ln\!\left(\frac{a+x}{a-x}\right)\) with \(a=5\). So \(\displaystyle\int\frac{25}{25-h^2}\,\mathrm dh=\frac{25}{2(5)}\ln\!\left(\frac{5+h}{5-h}\right)=\frac52\ln\!\left(\frac{5+h}{5-h}\right)\). \[ \frac{t}{25}=\int\left(-1+\frac{25}{25-h^2}\right)\mathrm dh=-h+\frac52\ln\!\left(\frac{5+h}{5-h}\right)+C. \] (1)(1) Since the depth increases from 0 to 5 m, \(\dfrac{5+h}{5-h}>0\) and no modulus is needed. When \(t=0\), \(h=0\), so \(0=-0+\tfrac52\ln 1+C\Rightarrow C=0\). \[ \frac52\ln\!\left(\frac{5+h}{5-h}\right)-h=\frac{1}{25}t\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)
(iv) read the physical meaning of the depth at which the rates balance
Since there is no change in volume when the depth is 5 m, the water level can never rise past 5 m.

The height of the conical tank must be at least 5 m. (1)

Tests: forming a DE from a worded rate-in/rate-out model, converting \(\tfrac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}\) to \(\tfrac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt}\) through a connected rate, and the improper-fraction reflex — degree of numerator \(\ge\) degree of denominator means divide first. Then a straight MF26 standard form and a worded interpretation.

There is no binomial series for it until the polynomial part is stripped off. \[ \frac{x-4}{x+1}=\frac{(x+1)-5}{x+1}=1-\frac{5}{x+1}=1-5(1+x)^{-1}. \] (1) MF26 binomial series with \(n=-1\): \((1+x)^{-1}=1+(-1)x+\dfrac{(-1)(-2)}{2!}x^2+\dfrac{(-1)(-2)(-3)}{3!}x^3+\cdots\) \[ (1+x)^{-1}=1-x+x^2-x^3+\cdots \] (1) \[ \frac{x-4}{x+1}=1-5\left(1-x+x^2-x^3+\cdots\right)=-4+5x-5x^2+5x^3+\cdots \] (1) \[ \text{Valid for }|x|<1,\ \text{i.e. }-1(1)

The MF26 series only applies to \((1+\text{something})^n\), so \(4\) must come out of the bracket before anything else happens. \(4^{-1/2}=\tfrac12\). \[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{4+3x}}=(4+3x)^{-1/2}=4^{-1/2}\left(1+\tfrac{3x}{4}\right)^{-1/2}=\tfrac12\left(1+\tfrac{3x}{4}\right)^{-1/2}. \] (1) \[ =\tfrac12\left[1+\left(-\tfrac12\right)\!\left(\tfrac{3x}{4}\right)+\frac{\left(-\tfrac12\right)\left(-\tfrac32\right)}{2!}\left(\tfrac{3x}{4}\right)^{2}+\frac{\left(-\tfrac12\right)\left(-\tfrac32\right)\left(-\tfrac52\right)}{3!}\left(\tfrac{3x}{4}\right)^{3}+\cdots\right] \] (1) \[ =\frac12-\frac{3}{16}x+\frac{27}{256}x^2-\frac{135}{2048}x^3+\cdots \] (1)
(c) integrate the series term by term, then check the interval against the validity range
\[ \int_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4+3x}}\,\mathrm dx\approx\int_{0}^{2}\left(\frac12-\frac{3}{16}x+\frac{27}{256}x^2-\frac{135}{2048}x^3\right)\mathrm dx \] (1) \[ =\left[\frac{x}{2}-\frac{3}{32}x^2+\frac{9}{256}x^3-\frac{135}{8192}x^4\right]_{0}^{2}=1-\frac38+\frac{9}{32}-\frac{135}{512}=0.6426\ (4\text{ d.p.}). \] (1) \[ \text{By GC, }\int_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4+3x}}\,\mathrm dx=0.7749\ (4\text{ d.p.}). \] (1) The two values differ in the first decimal place, and the reason is the validity range, not arithmetic. \[ \text{Valid for }\left|\tfrac{3x}{4}\right|<1\ \Rightarrow\ -\tfrac43The region of integration runs to \(x=2\), which lies outside \(-\tfrac43not an appropriate approximation. (1)

Question 5 [7]
A-Level 2019 P1 Q10(i)
Water is heated in a container and then placed in a room kept at a constant temperature of 16 °C. The temperature of the water \(t\) minutes after being placed in the room is \(\theta\) °C, and it decreases at a rate proportional to the difference between the temperature of the water and the temperature of the room. The water falls from 80 °C to 32 °C in the first 30 minutes. (a) Write down a differential equation for this situation. Solve this differential equation to get \(\theta\) as an exact function of \(t\). [6] (b) Find the temperature of the water 45 minutes after it is placed in the room. [1]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim worked solution
(a) write the DE, then separate
"Decreases at a rate proportional to \((\theta-16)\)" gives \(\tfrac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dt}=k(\theta-16)\) where \(k\) is a negative constant. (Writing \(-k(\theta-16)\) with \(k>0\) is equally acceptable; be consistent afterwards.) \[ \frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\mathrm dt}=k(\theta-16),\qquad k<0. \] (1) \[ \frac{\mathrm dt}{\mathrm d\theta}=\frac{1}{k(\theta-16)}\ \Longrightarrow\ kt+C=\ln|\theta-16|. \] (1)(1) \[ \theta-16=Ae^{kt}\ \ (A=e^{C})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta=16+Ae^{kt}. \] (1) Remove the modulus first, then substitute the two conditions — never the other way round. \[ t=0,\ \theta=80:\ A=64.\qquad t=30,\ \theta=32:\ 16=64e^{30k}\ \Rightarrow\ 30k=\ln\tfrac14\ \Rightarrow\ k=\tfrac{1}{30}\ln\tfrac14. \] (1) \[ \theta=16+64\left(\tfrac14\right)^{t/30}\qquad\left(\text{equivalently }\theta=16+64e^{-\frac{\ln 2}{15}t}\right). \] (1)
(b) substitute \(t=45\)
\[ \theta=16+64\left(\tfrac14\right)^{45/30}=16+64\left(\tfrac14\right)^{3/2}=16+\frac{64}{8}=24\ ^\circ\text{C}. \] (1)
Tests: Newton's law of cooling stated from words, separation, the modulus-then-constant discipline, two conditions used to pin down both \(A\) and \(k\), and an exact function demanded — \(\left(\tfrac14\right)^{t/30}\), not a decimal.

Question 6 [12]
A-Level N2024 P1 Q11
A helicopter is hovering at a great height above the ground. A parachutist drops from the helicopter and his parachute opens. The parachutist falls vertically. At time \(t\) seconds after leaving the helicopter, he has fallen a distance of \(x\) metres. At this time he has velocity \(v\) m s\(^{-1}\). The motion of the parachutist is modelled by \(\dfrac{\mathrm d^2x}{\mathrm dt^2}+\alpha\dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}=9.8\), where \(\alpha\) is a constant. (a)(i) Use the equation \(v=\dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}\) to write down a differential equation in \(v\) and \(t\). [1]    (ii) Given that \(\dfrac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=5\) when \(v=2.4\), find the value of \(\alpha\). [1] (b) It is given that the initial velocity of the parachutist is zero. Show that \(v=A\left(1-e^{Bt}\right)\), where \(A\) and \(B\) are constants to be found. [5] (c) The helicopter is at a height of 2000 m above the ground. Find the time taken for the parachutist to reach the ground. [5]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim worked solution
(a) the substitution \(v=\tfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}\) drops the order from two to one
\[ v=\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\mathrm d^2x}{\mathrm dt^2}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}+\alpha v=9.8. \] (1) \[ 5+2.4\alpha=9.8\ \Longrightarrow\ \alpha=2. \] (1)
(b) now separate the first-order equation
\(\tfrac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=9.8-2v=2(4.9-v)\), so divide through by 2 to make the bracket clean. \[ \int\frac{1}{4.9-v}\,\mathrm dv=\int 2\,\mathrm dt\ \Longrightarrow\ -\ln|4.9-v|=2t+C. \] (1)(1) \[ 4.9-v=\pm e^{-2t-C}=Ae^{-2t},\qquad A=\pm e^{-C}. \] (1) Remove the modulus first, then substitute \(t=0,\ v=0\). \[ t=0,\ v=0:\ 4.9=Ae^{0}\ \Rightarrow\ A=4.9. \] (1) \[ 4.9-v=4.9e^{-2t}\ \Longrightarrow\ v=4.9\left(1-e^{-2t}\right),\quad\text{so }A=4.9,\ B=-2. \] (1)
(c) integrate the velocity a second time to recover the distance
This is the second integration the original second-order equation demanded: \(v=\tfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}\), so \(x=\int v\,\mathrm dt\), with a new arbitrary constant \(d\). \[ \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}=4.9\left(1-e^{-2t}\right)\ \Longrightarrow\ x=4.9\left(t-\frac{1}{-2}e^{-2t}\right)+d=4.9\left(t+\tfrac12e^{-2t}\right)+d. \] (1)(1) \[ t=0,\ x=0:\ 0=4.9\left(0+\tfrac12e^{0}\right)+d\ \Rightarrow\ d=-2.45. \] (1) \[ x=4.9\left(t+\tfrac12e^{-2t}\right)-2.45. \] \[ x=2000:\quad 2000=4.9\left(t+\tfrac12e^{-2t}\right)-2.45\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{by GC, }t=408.66327 \] (1) \[ t\approx 409\ \text{seconds (3 s.f.)}. \] (1)
Tests: the full second-order route — substitute \(v=\tfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}\) to reduce the order, solve the resulting separable equation, then integrate a second time with a fresh constant to get displacement, and finish on the GC. The most recent A-Level application question on this topic.

Question 7 [9]
A-Level 2013 P1 Q9 (parts (i), (ii), (iv))
The variables \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) are connected by the differential equations \[\frac{\mathrm dz}{\mathrm dx}=3-2z\quad\text{(A)},\qquad\qquad \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=z\quad\text{(B)}.\] (i) Given that \(z>\tfrac32\), solve equation (A) to find \(z\) in terms of \(x\). [4] (ii) Hence find \(y\) in terms of \(x\). [2] (iii) The result in part (ii) represents a family of curves. Some members are straight lines. Write down the equations of two of these lines. On a single diagram, sketch one of your lines together with a non-linear member of the family that has your line as an asymptote. [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim worked solution
(i) separate equation (A)
\[ \int\frac{1}{3-2z}\,\mathrm dz=\int 1\,\mathrm dx\ \Longrightarrow\ -\tfrac12\ln|3-2z|=x+C. \] (1)(1) \[ \ln|3-2z|=-2x-2C\ \Longrightarrow\ |3-2z|=e^{-2C}e^{-2x}\ \Longrightarrow\ 3-2z=Be^{-2x}. \] (1) \[ z=\tfrac32+Ae^{-2x},\qquad A=-\tfrac12 B\ \text{arbitrary}. \] (1)
(ii) integrate equation (B) — a second, independent arbitrary constant appears
\[ \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=z=\tfrac32+Ae^{-2x}\ \Longrightarrow\ y=\int\left(\tfrac32+Ae^{-2x}\right)\mathrm dx \] (1) \[ y=\tfrac32x-\tfrac{A}{2}e^{-2x}+D. \] (1)
(iii) which constants give straight lines, and which give a curve with that line as asymptote
The exponential term is the only non-linear part, so setting \(A=0\) collapses the family to the straight lines \(y=\tfrac32x+D\). Any \(D\) works; two of them: \[ y=\tfrac32x\qquad\text{and}\qquad y=\tfrac32x+1. \] (1)(1) For the non-linear member, keep the same \(D\) as the line you drew (otherwise the line is not the asymptote) and take any \(A\neq0\). With \(A=-2,\ D=0\): \(y=\tfrac32x+e^{-2x}\). As \(x\to\infty\), \(e^{-2x}\to0\), so the curve approaches \(y=\tfrac32x\) from above; as \(x\to-\infty\) the exponential blows up.
The line \(y=\tfrac32x\) (blue) drawn with the non-linear member \(y=\tfrac32x+e^{-2x}\) (red), which has that line as an asymptote as \(x\to\infty\).
(1)
Tests: a chained pair of DEs solved in order, two independent arbitrary constants, and the family-of-solution-curves outcome — recognising which constant kills the curvature, and that the line only acts as an asymptote if both members share the same \(D\).

Question 8 [9]
CJC 2017 C2 BT2 P1 Q2
(i) Show that \(\dfrac{1}{1-\cos 2\theta}=\tfrac12\operatorname{cosec}^2\theta\). [1] (ii) By means of the substitution \(z=x-y\), find the particular solution of the differential equation \(\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\cos(x-y)\), given that \(y=-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) when \(x=0\). [8]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim worked solution
(i) double-angle identity \(\cos 2\theta=1-2\sin^2\theta\)
\[ \text{LHS}=\frac{1}{1-\cos 2\theta}=\frac{1}{1-\left(1-2\sin^2\theta\right)}=\frac{1}{2\sin^2\theta}=\tfrac12\operatorname{cosec}^2\theta=\text{RHS}\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)
(ii) differentiate the substitution implicitly and eliminate \(y\)
\[ z=x-y\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{\mathrm dz}{\mathrm dx}=1-\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=1-\frac{\mathrm dz}{\mathrm dx}\quad\cdots(1) \] (1)(1) \[ \text{Given: }\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\cos(x-y)\quad\cdots(2) \] \[ \text{Substitute }(1)\text{ into }(2):\quad 1-\frac{\mathrm dz}{\mathrm dx}=\cos z\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{\mathrm dz}{\mathrm dx}=1-\cos z. \] (1)(1)
invert, use part (i) with \(\theta=\tfrac z2\), then integrate
\(1-\cos z=1-\cos\!\left(2\cdot\tfrac z2\right)\), so part (i) applies with \(\theta=\tfrac z2\). Recall \(\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\cot\!\big[\mathrm f(x)\big]=-\mathrm f'(x)\operatorname{cosec}^2\!\big[\mathrm f(x)\big]\), so \(\displaystyle\int\tfrac12\operatorname{cosec}^2\!\tfrac z2\,\mathrm dz=-\cot\tfrac z2\). \[ \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dz}=\frac{1}{1-\cos z}=\tfrac12\operatorname{cosec}^2\!\frac z2 \] (1) \[ x=\int\tfrac12\operatorname{cosec}^2\!\frac z2\,\mathrm dz=-\cot\frac z2+C=-\cot\!\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)+C. \] (1)(1) When \(x=0,\ y=-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), so \(z=0-\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\tfrac z2=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\). \[ 0=-\cot\frac{\pi}{4}+C=-1+C\ \Longrightarrow\ C=1 \] \[ x=-\cot\!\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)+1. \] (1)
Tests: reduction by a given substitution — differentiate it implicitly, eliminate the original variable, and notice that the reduced equation is only separable the "wrong way round", so you invert to \(\tfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dz}\). The earlier identity is not decoration; it is the only way the integral becomes standard. The answer is left implicit, which is fine when the question says "find the particular solution".

Question 9 [7]
MJC 2009 C2 Prelim P1 Q4
ToTo bear is an endangered species on Too Island. The number of births per unit time is proportional to the population of ToTo bears at any time, and the number of deaths per unit time is proportional to the square of the population of ToTo bears. The population at time \(t\) is \(x\). Show that \(\dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}=ax-bx^2\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are positive constants. It is also known that the population of ToTo bears remains constant when \(x=200\). Hence show that \(x=\dfrac{200}{Be^{-kt}+1}\), where \(k\) is a positive constant. Deduce that there is a limit to the size of the population of ToTo bears and explain the significance of this limit. [7]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim worked solution
form the DE — two proportionalities, two different constants
Do not use the letters \(a\) and \(b\) for the constants of proportionality: they already appear in the answer you are asked to reach. Use \(m\) and \(n\) first. Let \(B\) and \(D\) be the number of births and deaths respectively. \[ \frac{\mathrm dB}{\mathrm dt}=mx,\qquad \frac{\mathrm dD}{\mathrm dt}=nx^2,\qquad m,n>0. \] (1) \[ \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\mathrm dB}{\mathrm dt}-\frac{\mathrm dD}{\mathrm dt}=mx-nx^2=ax-bx^2\quad(\text{shown, with }m=a,\ n=b). \] (1)
"remains constant" means the rate is zero — that fixes one constant in terms of the other
\[ \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}=0\ \text{when}\ x=200:\quad 0=200a-(200)^2b\ \Longrightarrow\ a=200b. \] (1) \[ \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}=(200b)x-bx^2=bx(200-x). \]
separate, split by partial fractions, then rearrange into the required form
\(\dfrac{1}{x(200-x)}=\dfrac{1}{200}\left(\dfrac1x+\dfrac{1}{200-x}\right)\). It is not clear whether \(200-x\) is positive or negative, so a modulus must be inserted. \[ \int\frac{1}{x(200-x)}\,\mathrm dx=\int b\,\mathrm dt\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{1}{200}\Big[\ln|x|-\ln|200-x|\Big]=bt+c \] (1) \[ \ln\left|\frac{x}{200-x}\right|=200(bt+c)\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{x}{200-x}=Ae^{200bt},\qquad A=\pm e^{200c} \] \[ x=200Ae^{200bt}-Axe^{200bt}\ \Longrightarrow\ x\left(1+Ae^{200bt}\right)=200Ae^{200bt} \] \[ x=\frac{200Ae^{200bt}}{1+Ae^{200bt}}=\frac{200}{\tfrac1Ae^{-200bt}+1}=\frac{200}{Be^{-kt}+1},\quad B=\tfrac1A,\ k=200b\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)
long-run behaviour — and a worded conclusion, not just an arrow
\[ \text{As }t\to\infty,\ e^{-kt}\to0,\ \text{so } x=\frac{200}{Be^{-kt}+1}\to 200. \] (1) \[ \text{Hence there is a limit to the size of the population: }200\text{ is the maximum number of ToTo bears the environment can support.} \] (1)
Tests: the logistic model built from two competing worded proportionalities, the "population remains constant \(\Rightarrow\) rate \(=0\)" trigger, partial fractions inside a separable DE, and a limiting value that must be stated in words about the bears, not just as \(x\to200\).

Question 10 [6]
NYJC 2019 Prelim P1 Q2
The diagram shows a mechanism for converting rotational motion into linear motion. The point \(P\), on the circumference of a disc of radius \(r\), rotates about a fixed point \(O\). The point \(Q\) moves along the line \(OX\), and \(P\) and \(Q\) are connected by a rod of fixed length \(3r\). As the disc rotates, the point \(Q\) is made to slide backwards and forwards along \(OX\). At time \(t\), angle \(POQ\) is \(\theta\), measured anticlockwise from \(OX\), and the distance \(OQ\) is \(x\).
\(OP=r\), \(PQ=3r\), \(\angle POQ=\theta\), \(OQ=x\).
(i) Show that \(x=r\left(\cos\theta+\sqrt{9-\sin^2\theta}\right)\). [2] (ii) State the maximum value of \(x\). [1] (iii) Express \(x\) as a polynomial in \(\theta\) if \(\theta\) is sufficiently small for \(\theta^3\) and higher powers of \(\theta\) to be neglected. [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim worked solution
(i) drop a perpendicular from \(P\) to \(OX\) and split \(OQ\) into two horizontal pieces
Let \(F\) be the foot of the perpendicular from \(P\) to \(OX\). Then \(OF=r\cos\theta\) and \(PF=r\sin\theta\). In the right-angled triangle \(PFQ\), the hypotenuse is the rod \(PQ=3r\). \[ FQ=\sqrt{(3r)^2-(r\sin\theta)^2}=\sqrt{9r^2-r^2\sin^2\theta}=r\sqrt{9-\sin^2\theta}. \] (1) \[ x=OQ=OF+FQ=r\cos\theta+r\sqrt{9-\sin^2\theta}=r\left(\cos\theta+\sqrt{9-\sin^2\theta}\right)\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)
(ii) maximise both terms at once
Since \(0\le\sin^2\theta\le1\) and \(-1\le\cos\theta\le1\), both terms are largest at \(\theta=0\), where \(\cos\theta=1\) and \(\sqrt{9-0}=3\). \[ x_{\max}=r(1+3)=4r. \] (1)
(iii) small-angle approximations, then factor the 9 out before expanding the surd
For small \(\theta\): \(\cos\theta\approx 1-\tfrac{\theta^2}{2}\) and \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\), so \(\sin^2\theta\approx\theta^2\). The surd is then \((9-\theta^2)^{1/2}\), which is only expandable once 9 is taken out: \(9^{1/2}\left(1-\tfrac{\theta^2}{9}\right)^{1/2}=3\left(1-\tfrac{\theta^2}{9}\right)^{1/2}\). \[ x\approx r\left[\left(1-\tfrac{\theta^2}{2}\right)+3\left(1-\tfrac{\theta^2}{9}\right)^{1/2}\right] \] (1) \[ \left(1-\tfrac{\theta^2}{9}\right)^{1/2}\approx 1+\tfrac12\left(-\tfrac{\theta^2}{9}\right)=1-\tfrac{\theta^2}{18} \] (1) \[ x\approx r\left[1-\tfrac{\theta^2}{2}+3-\tfrac{\theta^2}{6}\right]=r\left(4-\tfrac23\theta^2\right). \] (1)
Tests: small-angle approximations for \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) fed into a surd that must be rewritten as \(a^n\left(1+\tfrac{bx}{a}\right)^n\) before the binomial series applies — and the \(\theta^2\) coefficients from the two separate pieces must be combined, which is where \(-\tfrac12-\tfrac16=-\tfrac23\) is usually lost.

Question 11 [8]
MI 2015 Prelim P1 Q6 (modified)
It is given that \(y=e^{\tan^{-1}x}\). (i) Show that \(\left(1+x^2\right)\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=y\). [2] (ii) Show that the value of \(\dfrac{\mathrm d^3y}{\mathrm dx^3}\) when \(x=0\) is \(-1\). [2] (iii) Write down the Maclaurin series for \(e^{\tan^{-1}x}\) up to and including the term in \(x^3\). [2] (iv) With the aid of a diagram, explain why the Maclaurin series for \(e^{\tan^{-1}x}\) up to the term in \(x^3\) may not give a good estimate of the value of \(e^{\tan^{-1}x}\) when \(x=2\). [2]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution — verbatim worked solution
(i) take logarithms first — it kills the exponential and the inverse trig in one move
MF26: \(\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\tan^{-1}x=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\). \[ \ln y=\tan^{-1}x\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac1y\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{1}{1+x^2} \] (1) \[ \left(1+x^2\right)\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=y\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)
(ii) differentiate the relation twice more — product rule each time, never expand \(y\)
\[ \left(1+x^2\right)\frac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}+2x\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}\ \Longrightarrow\ \left(1+x^2\right)\frac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}+(2x-1)\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=0 \] (1) \[ \left(1+x^2\right)\frac{\mathrm d^3y}{\mathrm dx^3}+(4x-1)\frac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}+2\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=0 \] At \(x=0\): \(y=e^{\tan^{-1}0}=e^0=1\); from (i), \(\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=1\); from the second relation, \(\dfrac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}-1=0\Rightarrow\dfrac{\mathrm d^2y}{\mathrm dx^2}=1\). \[ x=0:\quad \frac{\mathrm d^3y}{\mathrm dx^3}-(1)(1)+2(1)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{\mathrm d^3y}{\mathrm dx^3}=-1\quad(\text{shown}). \] (1)
(iii) assemble the series from the four values at \(x=0\)
\[ y=1+x+\frac{1}{2!}x^2+\frac{-1}{3!}x^3+\cdots=1+x+\tfrac12x^2-\tfrac16x^3+\cdots \] (1)(1)
(iv) sketch both curves — the series is only close to the function near \(x=0\)
The curve and its cubic Maclaurin approximation, sketched together.
Sketch \(y=e^{\tan^{-1}x}\) and \(y=1+x+\tfrac12x^2-\tfrac16x^3\) on the same axes. \[ \text{The two graphs are close only near }x=0;\text{ at }x=2\text{ they are far apart.} \] (1) \[ \text{Hence the cubic Maclaurin series does not give a good estimate of }e^{\tan^{-1}x}\text{ at }x=2. \] (1)
Fuses: the given relation \(\left(1+x^2\right)\tfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=y\) is a differential equation — you never solve it, you differentiate it repeatedly and read the derivatives at \(x=0\) straight off. Then the accuracy outcome: a Maclaurin series is a local approximation, and the justification has to be a picture of the two curves separating, not a sentence about "small \(x\)".
