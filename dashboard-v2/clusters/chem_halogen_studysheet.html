THE SPINE (Layer 1) — the entire topic hangs off one polar bond, C–X (carbon is δ+, halogen is δ−), and one mechanism family: nucleophilic substitution. The δ+ carbon is "susceptible to nucleophilic attack". Halogenoalkanes are the synthetic hub — swap the halogen for \(\ce{-OH}\), \(\ce{-CN}\), \(\ce{-NH2}\), \(\ce{-OR}\), or eliminate it to a \(\ce{C=C}\). Two sub-questions decide everything: (a) which mechanism — SN1 or SN2 (set by the substrate: 1°/2°/3°) — and (b) how fast — set by the C–X bond strength (because the rate-determining step breaks C–X in both mechanisms).

1 Nomenclature & classification Layer 1

Halogenoalkane = an alkane with one or more H atoms replaced by a halogen (F, Cl, Br, I). Named with the prefix fluoro- / chloro- / bromo- / iodo- + locant (bromoethane, 1,2-dibromoethane, 3-chloropentane). Halogenoarene = a halogen bonded directly to a benzene ring (chlorobenzene).
Layer 2 — how to classify 1° / 2° / 3°
Look only at the carbon that bears the halogen, then use either test (they agree):
ClassAlkyl (C) groups on that carbonH atoms on that carbonExample
Primary (1°)12\(\ce{CH3CH2Br}\), \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2Cl}\)
Secondary (2°)212-bromobutane, chlorocyclopentane
Tertiary (3°)302-bromo-2-methylpropane
Traps. (1) Write out "primary / secondary / tertiary" (or the superscript 1°/2°/3°) — saying "one degree, two degree" is marked wrong. (2) (chloromethyl)benzene \(\ce{C6H5CH2Cl}\) has its Cl on an sp³ carbon, so it behaves as a primary halogenoalkane, NOT like chlorobenzene — it does react. (3) 3-chloropropene \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2Cl}\) is primary (the C–Cl carbon has only one C neighbour). (4) \(\ce{C4H9Br}\) has 5 isomers — 4 constitutional, and 2-bromobutane is a chiral pair of enantiomers, counted twice.

2 Physical properties

Layer 2 — the facts + the reasoning that scores
Halogenoalkanes are generally colourless liquids, immiscible with water, and denser than water. Their main intermolecular force is dispersion (instantaneous dipole–induced dipole, id–id). For the same alkyl group the boiling point rises chloro < bromo < iodo.
The size of the electron cloud (number of electrons) increases \(\ce{C2H5Cl}\) < \(\ce{C2H5Br}\) < \(\ce{C2H5I}\), so intermolecular dispersion forces increase in strength in the same order; more energy is needed to overcome them, so the boiling point increases in the same order.
Counter-intuitive trap. C–Cl is the most polar bond, yet the iodoalkane boils highest. Explain the trend by electron-cloud size → dispersion forces, NOT by polarity. (Permanent dipole–permanent dipole "takes a back seat".) Compared with the alkane: chloroethane boils higher than propane/butane because it is polar — pd–pd on top of dispersion; state pd–pd only when comparing a halogenoalkane against a non-polar alkane of similar \(M_r\).

3 The core mechanism I — SN2 Layer 1

Layer 1 keywords
SN2 = bimolecular nucleophilic substitution. ONE step · nucleophile attacks the δ+ carbon from the side OPPOSITE the leaving group (backside) · Nu–C bond forms as C–X breaks simultaneously · passes through a transition state (not an intermediate) · rate = k[RX][Nu] (2nd order) · inversion of configuration → a single, optically active enantiomer · favoured by 1°. The "2" is the order, not the number of steps.
SN2: \(\ce{OH-}\) + bromoethane. One step, backside attack, single transition state, inversion.
What the marker checks on an SN2 drawing (HCI "things to take note of"): δ+ on C and δ− on the halogen · one arrow from the nucleophile's lone pair to the δ+ carbon · a second arrow from the C–X bond to the halogen · the transition state in square brackets with the ‡ symbol, showing partial C···Nu forming and partial C···X breaking · the overall charge on the transition state (neutral Nu like \(\ce{NH3}\) → neutral TS; anionic Nu like \(\ce{OH-}\) → negative TS) · a lone pair on the expelled \(\ce{X-}\) — and you must write the leaving group in the products (most-forgotten mark).

4 The core mechanism II — SN1 Layer 1

Layer 1 keywords
SN1 = unimolecular nucleophilic substitution. TWO steps · Step 1 (slow, rate-determining): heterolytic fission of C–X → carbocation + \(\ce{X-}\) (nucleophile is NOT in this step) · Step 2 (fast): nucleophile attacks the carbocation · rate = k[RX] (1st order; changing [Nu] does nothing) · carbocation is trigonal planar with a full + charge and only 6 electrons · nucleophile attacks top or bottom face with near-equal probability → racemic mixture (optically inactive) · favoured by 3°.
SN1: 2-chloro/bromo-2-methylpropane. Slow C–X heterolysis → planar carbocation, then fast attack.
Layer 2 — why SN1 gives a racemate (draw this to score)
If the carbocation carbon is chiral in the product, the planar intermediate is attacked from the top and bottom faces with near-equal probability, giving equal amounts of the two enantiomers → a racemic mixture whose rotations cancel → no net effect on plane-polarised light. The examiner wants the planar cation drawn with the nucleophile approaching both faces and both enantiomer products shown — a words-only answer loses marks.
Don't muddle the two. SN2 → inversion → single enantiomer → optically active. SN1 → planar cation → racemic → optically inactive. For SN1 in H2 you draw the curly arrow for C–X fission (often an equilibrium arrow) but you do not need to draw its transition state — though you must recognise it in an MCQ.

5 Which mechanism? SN1 vs SN2 rate factors

Secondary 2°mediummediumeither
Tertiary 3°fastest (stable 3° cation)slowest (3 groups block the rear)SN1
SN1 depends on carbocation stability: electron-donating alkyl groups stabilise the carbocation by dispersing/reducing its positive charge, lowering \(E_a\); stability 1° < 2° < 3°. SN2 depends on steric hindrance: the three alkyl groups of a 3° halogenoalkane obstruct the approach of the nucleophile towards the electron-deficient (δ+) carbon. Quote both arguments.
Layer 2 — the two exceptions (data would be given)
1-chloro-2,2-dimethylpropane (neopentyl) is 1° but almost inert to SN2 — the bulky adjacent quaternary carbon blocks the rear. (Chloromethyl)benzene \(\ce{C6H5CH2Cl}\) and allylic halides \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2Cl}\) are 1° but favour SN1, because the carbocation is stabilised by delocalisation into the ring / \(\ce{C=C}\) \(\pi\) system.

6 Elimination (E1 / E2) → alkene

Layer 1 keywords
A nucleophile is also a base: instead of attacking the δ+ carbon it can remove a β-hydrogen (on the carbon next to C–X), the C–X leaves, and a \(\ce{C=C}\) forms — this is β-elimination. The solvent decides substitution vs elimination: aqueous \(\ce{NaOH}\), heat → substitution (alcohol); ethanolic \(\ce{NaOH/KOH}\), heat → elimination (alkene).
Layer 2 — E1 / E2, products, and the H2 boundary
Like substitution, elimination has two kinetic flavours — E2 (one step, base removes the β-H as C–X leaves, rate = k[RX][base]) and E1 (two steps via the carbocation, rate = k[RX]). For H2 you are NOT required to draw the elimination mechanism — know the reagents/conditions and how to work out the products. Number of alkenes = number of different types of β-hydrogen. Elimination is entropy-driven (1 molecule → 2, \(\Delta S \gt 0\)), so higher temperature favours it. Saytzeff/Zaitsev: the more substituted alkene is the more stable → major product. Example: 2-bromobutane + hot ethanolic NaOH → 3 alkenes — but-1-ene (minor) + cis- and trans-but-2-ene (major).

The KCN route is the step-up — the cyanide carbon adds one carbon to the chain, so the nitrile is a masked carboxylic-acid / amine one carbon longer. Direct \(\ce{NH3}\) keeps the same carbon count. Choose the route from the target's carbon count.
Over-alkylation. The 1° amine is a stronger nucleophile than \(\ce{NH3}\) (electron-donating alkyl), so it keeps reacting: RX → 1° → 2° → 3° amine → quaternary ammonium salt \(\ce{R4N+X-}\) (N with 4 R groups, no lone pair, cannot be deprotonated). Use EXCESS \(\ce{NH3}\) to favour the 1° amine.

The rate-determining step of both SN1 and SN2 breaks the C–X bond. As the halogen gets bigger the C–X bond lengthens and weakens, so it breaks more readily: rate of hydrolysis iodo > bromo > chloro. C–F is very strong → fluoroalkanes are essentially inert. Cite the Data-Booklet bond energies.
Polarity trap. C–F is the most polar bond (carbon most δ+) yet fluoroalkanes are the least reactive — because rate tracks bond strength, not carbon polarity. (C–F is strong partly because C and F are both Period 2 → good orbital-size match, no mismatch.)

9 Halogenoarenes — why chlorobenzene is unreactive

Chlorobenzene is far less reactive towards nucleophilic substitution than a halogenoalkane, for two reasons (state both):
1. Partial double-bond character. The lone pair on the halogen is delocalised into the benzene ring, so the C–Cl bond gains partial double-bond character → it is shorter and stronger → very hard to break.
2. Steric + electronic blocking. The benzene ring blocks the rear of the C–X bond and its π-electron cloud repels the incoming (electron-rich) nucleophile.
Layer 2 — the sp² pattern & ring reactivity
The same delocalisation makes any halogen on an sp² carbon (aryl or vinyl, e.g. 1-bromocyclohexene) resistant to nucleophilic substitution. It also can't go SN1: the carbocation's empty p-orbital would be orthogonal to the \(\ce{C=C}\) π system, so it isn't stabilised. Halogenoarenes still undergo electrophilic substitution on the ring, but less readily than benzene — the halogen is deactivating yet 2,4-directing (the Topic-12 exception).

10 Distinguishing tests — the silver halide Layer 1

Layer 2 — identify the halogen (colour + NH₃ solubility)
Works only for a halogen on an sp³ carbon (it must release a free halide ion).
1. Add \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\), warm → hydrolysis releases \(\ce{X-}\).
2. Add excess dilute \(\ce{HNO3}\) to neutralise the unreacted \(\ce{OH-}\) (else brown \(\ce{Ag2O}\) forms). Not HCl (adds \(\ce{Cl-}\)); not \(\ce{H2SO4}\) (\(\ce{Ag2SO4}\) sparingly soluble).
3. Add \(\ce{AgNO3(aq)}\) → note the precipitate colour.
4. Add \(\ce{NH3(aq)}\) to confirm by solubility (colours are hard to tell apart).

Rate test (ethanolic \(\ce{AgNO3}\), hot water bath): add ethanolic \(\ce{AgNO3}\) to each halogenoalkane and time the precipitate. Rate of AgX formation: iodo (almost immediate) > bromo > chloro (slowest) — the weaker C–X breaks faster (RDS). This also separates a halogenoalkane (gives a ppt) from a halogenoarene (no ppt — C–X won't break).
Two different tests. If the halogenoalkanes are pre-hydrolysed with NaOH first, they all have the same free-halide concentration → they precipitate at the same time, so you read the colour. If they are not pre-hydrolysed, you read the rate (reflects bond strength). Dissolving forms the colourless \(\ce{[Ag(NH3)2]+}\) complex.

11 Uses, CFCs & ozone depletion

Layer 2 — the exam-testable environmental story
Fluoroalkanes / PTFE (Teflon) are used where chemical inertness matters (the C–F bond is very strong). CFCs (C–Cl + C–F only, e.g. \(\ce{CCl2F2}\)) were refrigerants, propellants and fire extinguishers — inert, non-flammable, non-toxic, easily liquefied.
Ozone depletion — it is the C–Cl bond that does the damage, not C–F (mechanism itself not required):
1. UV light causes homolytic fission of the weaker C–Cl bond → \(\ce{Cl\bullet}\) radical (C–F 485 too strong to break).
2. \(\ce{Cl\bullet + O3 -> ClO\bullet + O2}\)
3. \(\ce{ClO\bullet + O -> Cl\bullet + O2}\) — the \(\ce{Cl\bullet}\) is regenerated, so one radical catalytically destroys many ozone molecules.
Substitutes & GWP. A safe substitute has no C–Cl, C–Br or C–I bond (e.g. butane — pure alkane, but flammable). HFCs (C–H + C–F, no Cl) don't deplete ozone but are still greenhouse gases. HCFCs deplete ozone far less than CFCs. Relative GWP: CFC > HFC > HCFC > hydrocarbon. Note C–Br is even weaker than C–Cl, so bromo compounds are worse, not better.

Q1 · Why does bp rise chloro < bromo < iodo for the same alkyl group? (mark-scheme reasoning)
The electron cloud (number of electrons) increases Cl < Br < I, so dispersion forces increase; more energy is needed to overcome them, so bp increases. Explain by electron-cloud size, not polarity.

Q2 · State the rate law and stereochemical outcome for SN2 vs SN1 on a chiral substrate.
SN2: rate = k[RX][Nu], one step, backside attack → inversion → single enantiomer → optically active. SN1: rate = k[RX], two steps via a planar carbocation → racemic mixture → optically inactive.

Q3 · Using both factors, why do 1° favour SN2 and 3° favour SN1?
Carbocation stability (SN1): three electron-donating alkyl groups stabilise the 3° cation by dispersing its + charge → lower \(E_a\). Steric hindrance (SN2): those three groups block the nucleophile's rear approach to the δ+ carbon. So 1° → SN2, 3° → SN1.

Q4 · Reagents/conditions + product for bromoethane with (a) OH⁻ (b) CN⁻ (c) NH₃. Which is the step-up and why?
(a) NaOH(aq), heat → ethanol. (b) ethanolic KCN, heat → propanenitrile — the step-up (cyanide carbon adds one C). (c) ethanolic NH₃, heat under pressure → ethylamine (use excess NH₃ to limit over-alkylation).

Q5 · Same halogenoalkane, same OH⁻ — what one change of conditions switches alcohol ↔ alkene?
