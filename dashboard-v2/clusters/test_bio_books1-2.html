1 A virus particle consists of a nucleic acid genome enclosed in a protein coat. It has no cytoplasm, no ribosomes and no membrane-bound organelles, and it carries out no metabolic reactions of its own. Four observations about viruses are listed.

  1. 1A virus particle has no plasma membrane enclosing cytoplasm.
  2. 2New virus particles are assembled only inside a host cell, using host ribosomes.
  3. 3The viral genome is built from nucleotides joined by phosphodiester bonds.
  4. 4Purified virus particles can be stored indefinitely as a crystalline solid.

Which observations are evidence that viruses fall outside the cell theory?

  • A1, 2 and 3
  • B1, 2 and 4
  • C2, 3 and 4
  • D1 and 4 only
Q1. Answer B OFFICIAL LO (f)
Awrong. Contains statement 3. A phosphodiester backbone is shared with every living organism, so it is evidence of chemical similarity to life, not of falling outside the cell theory.
Bcorrect. Statement 1: the cell theory holds that the cell is the basic unit of structure, and a virus is not a cell because it has no plasma membrane enclosing cytoplasm. Statement 2: the cell theory holds that all cells arise from pre-existing cells by division, whereas virus particles are assembled from separately made components inside a host cell and never divide. Statement 4: crystallisation is a property of non-living chemicals, and no cell can be crystallised and remain a cell. 1
Cwrong. Contains statement 3, and omits statement 1, which is the strongest single piece of evidence.
Dwrong. 1 and 4 are both valid, but omitting 2 loses the reproduction argument, which is the tenet of the cell theory viruses breach most directly.
Gap targeted: A23, how viruses challenge the cell theory as a multi-point answer. Book 1 LO (f) is examined under Core Idea 1 even though virus structure is taught later, so all the material needed is supplied in the stem.

12 DNA from four individuals was amplified using one pair of primers flanking a single locus, then separated by agarose gel electrophoresis. Individuals 1 and 4 each gave a single band in the same position. Individual 2 gave two bands. Individual 3 gave a single band, in a position further from the well than the band given by individual 1. Which conclusion can be drawn from this gel?

  • AThe allele responsible for the band nearer the well is dominant to the allele responsible for the band further from the well.
  • BIndividual 2 is heterozygous at this locus.
  • CThe allele in individual 3 differs from the allele in individual 1 by a base substitution.
  • DIndividual 1 loaded half as much DNA onto the gel as individual 2.
Q12. Answer B SCHOOL LO (j)(ii)
Awrong. A gel shows which alleles are present, not how they interact. Dominance is a statement about phenotype and cannot be read off a gel at all.
Bcorrect. Two bands means two amplification products of different length from one locus, so the individual carries two different alleles at the same locus on the two homologous chromosomes, which is heterozygous. 1
Cwrong. A gel separates on length. A base substitution does not change the length of the product, so it would give a band in the same position. A band in a different position means bases have been added or deleted. And the sequence itself cannot be read from a gel.
Dwrong. A homozygote's two identical alleles co-migrate into one band, so that single band is about twice as intense as either of a heterozygote's two bands. The totals are comparable, not halved.
Gaps targeted: B2-43 (what a gel cannot establish), B2-59 (band thickness is quantity, band position is length), B2-65 (every option gets a verdict and a reason, out loud).

(a) Naming A, C, D and the order of labelling [4]

A is the rough endoplasmic reticulum and C is the Golgi body. 1 both

Radioactivity appears in A first because the labelled amino acids are assembled into polypeptide at the ribosomes on the rough ER, which is the site of synthesis of proteins destined for secretion, and the polypeptide passes into the lumen of the rough ER where it folds. 1

Gap targeted: decay-prone set piece 10, the secretory pathway end to end with organelle roles.
(b) Large nucleolus [3]

The gene coding for rRNA is transcribed in the nucleolus to form rRNA, and a larger nucleolus means more rRNA is transcribed per unit time. 1

The rRNA is assembled with ribosomal proteins in the nucleolus to form the large and small ribosomal subunits, so more ribosomes are produced. 1

More ribosomes on the rough ER means more mRNA molecules can be translated at the same time, so more pepsinogen is synthesised per unit time. 1

Gap targeted: A14, missed in Cluster 3 and again in Cluster 4. The chain must run more rRNA transcribed → more ribosomes → more protein per unit time. Stopping at more ribosomes forfeits the last mark.
(c) Levels of organisation in pepsin and haemoglobin [5]

Primary structure, present in both: the number and sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide, joined by peptide bonds. 1

Secondary structure, present in both: regular coiling and folding of the polypeptide backbone into α-helices and β-pleated sheets, stabilised by hydrogen bonds between the C=O and the N–H groups of the peptide bonds of the backbone. 1

Tertiary structure, present in both: the polypeptide is further bent, coiled and folded into a specific 3D conformation, maintained by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions between the R groups of amino acid residues. In pepsin this is where the three disulfide bridges act. 1

Quaternary structure is present in haemoglobin but absent from pepsin, because pepsin is a single polypeptide: haemoglobin's four polypeptide subunits, each with its own tertiary structure, associate through hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and hydrophobic interactions between R groups on different chains. 1

Haemoglobin additionally carries a non-protein prosthetic group, the haem group, one per subunit, making it a conjugated protein, whereas pepsin does not. 1

Gap targeted: decay-prone set piece 7, the four levels with the exact bond at each, plus A11 (describe the structure of a quaternary protein means all four levels, not just the quaternary one).
(d) Zymogen activation [3]

This is control at the post-translational level, specifically biochemical modification of the protein after translation, here by cleavage of a precursor. 1

Because the enzyme is made as an inactive precursor, it cannot hydrolyse the peptide bonds of the cell's own proteins as it passes through the rough ER, Golgi and secretory vesicles. 1

Activation is delayed until the enzyme reaches the site where it is needed, and it is faster to activate a stockpile of stored precursor than to transcribe and translate the gene from scratch, so the cell can respond quickly. 1

Do not answer translational level. Translation has already happened; the change is to the protein, not to the message. Also note: of the post-translational modifications, only phosphorylation is reversible. Proteolytic cleavage, glycosylation and ubiquitination are not (B2-34).

Figure taken from 9477 Specimen Paper 2 Q2 (real graph, three incubation times). Stem rebuilt; parts (b), (c) and (d) are new. Marking wording from MI Prelim 2023 P2 Q2b (active-site substitution, compulsory point), HCI 2024 P2 (induced fit) and the NJC enzyme schemes.
(a) Why the optimum falls as time increases [4]

Raising temperature increases the kinetic energy of enzyme and substrate molecules, so there are more frequent successful collisions and more enzyme-substrate complexes form per unit time, which raises the rate. 1

Above the optimum, the hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds maintaining the 3D conformation of the enzyme are disrupted, so the active site is no longer complementary in shape and charge to the substrate, no enzyme-substrate complex forms, and the enzyme is denatured. 1

Denaturation is cumulative and irreversible: at any temperature above the optimum a proportion of the enzyme molecules is denatured in every minute that passes, so the longer the incubation, the smaller the fraction of enzyme still active. 1

Therefore over five hours the higher temperatures lose their early rate advantage because so little active enzyme survives, and the greatest quantity of product is instead produced at a lower temperature where the enzyme remains active for the whole period. The optimum for quantity of product is time-dependent, whereas the optimum for initial rate is not. 1

Trap: denaturation does not break peptide bonds. The primary structure is unchanged; the conformation is not.
(b) The amphipathic solvent [4]

The solvent described is amphipathic: it has a non-polar hydrocarbon region and a polar hydroxyl region. The interaction it disrupts first is the hydrophobic interaction between the non-polar R groups of amino acid residues in the tertiary structure. 1

Hydrophobic interactions are not true bonds. They exist because non-polar R groups cannot form hydrogen bonds with water, so in an aqueous environment they are forced together into the interior of the molecule, away from water. Water alone cannot disrupt them, because water is the very thing that drives them together; they persist for as long as water is excluded from that interior. 1

The amphipathic solvent is miscible with water yet can also surround non-polar R groups with its hydrocarbon end, so the R groups are no longer driven together, the hydrophobic core is solvated and the tertiary structure unfolds. The active site loses its specific 3D conformation, so no enzyme-substrate complex can form and activity falls to zero. 1

Solubility is lost because the hydrophobic R groups that were buried are now exposed at the surface. They cannot form hydrogen bonds with the surrounding water, so the unfolded molecules associate with one another to shield those exposed R groups from the aqueous environment and aggregate out of solution. 1

Gap targeted: R1, three exposures. Missed in Cluster 2, missed in Cluster 4, unstable in the gap drill. He reaches for ionic bonds every time. New angle used here: the solvent is given by structure, not by name, and the question asks which interaction can only exist where water is excluded. Also picks up A7, misfolded proteins aggregate to shield exposed hydrophobic R groups from the aqueous lumen.
(c) Which variants carry an active-site substitution [5]

Variant 1: outside the active site. Both Vmax and Km are unchanged, so neither substrate binding nor the rate of breakdown of the enzyme-substrate complex has altered. The substituted residue therefore takes no part in the active site and its R group makes no interaction that affects the active site's conformation. 1

Variant 2: inside the active site, in the substrate-binding region. Km has risen twelve-fold, and since Km and affinity are inverse, a higher Km means much lower affinity of the enzyme for its substrate. 1

The compulsory reasoning: the amino acid at position 102 is part of the active site of the enzyme; the R groups of the two amino acids are different and have different properties, so the active site is no longer as complementary in shape and charge to the substrate and more substrate is needed to reach half Vmax. Vmax is unchanged, so once the complex does form, catalysis proceeds normally. 1

Variant 3: also acting on the active site, but on catalysis rather than on binding. Km is unchanged, so substrate binds as well as ever, but Vmax has fallen to about a third, so the rate at which the enzyme-substrate complex is converted to product has fallen. 1

The substituted residue at position 241 is therefore either a catalytic residue within the active site, whose R group no longer strains the bond to be broken or lowers the activation energy, or a residue outside the active site whose substitution alters the R-group interactions of the tertiary structure and so distorts the catalytic geometry of the active site. Either is accepted provided the answer states that the effect is exerted on the active site. 1

Gap targeted: R2, three exposures. New angle used here: three variants rather than two, with kinetic data rather than a bare activity figure, so he has to locate each substitution rather than be told one is in the active site. The compulsory mark-scheme line is Amino acid at position n is part of the active site of enzyme, and a real scheme awards nothing downstream without it. Also re-tests A6 and A10.
(d) One synthetase, several tRNAs [2]

This is induced fit: the active site is not in a precise complementary conformation to the substrate before binding. 1

On binding, the active site changes its 3D conformation slightly to accommodate the small variations between the different tRNA molecules that carry the same amino acid, so one enzyme can charge several of them. 1

Degeneracy explains why there are more codons than amino acids; it explains nothing about one enzyme's active site. This is gap B2-20, which closed once, and is re-tested here under the decay rule.

(c) Glycogen: no tensile strength, good storage [5]

No tensile strength, because the chains are highly branched by α(1,6) glycosidic bonds and coil into helices, so they cannot lie straight and parallel to one another, and there is no cross-linking between chains to bundle them into fibres. 1

The hydroxyl groups project into the interior of the helix rather than outwards in all directions, so few –OH groups are available to form interchain hydrogen bonds and there is nothing to bind neighbouring chains together. 1

The same features make it a good store. It is a very large molecule made of many thousands of α-glucose residues, therefore insoluble in water, so it can be stored in large quantities without affecting the water potential of the cell, and it cannot diffuse out of the cell. 1

The molecule coils and folds into a compact shape precisely because the chains are not cross-linked, so a large carbon store is held in a small volume. 1

The α(1,4) glycosidic bonds are readily hydrolysed by enzymes, and the many branch ends give many points at which hydrolysis can occur at the same time, so glucose is mobilised rapidly for respiration when it is required. (b) Glycogen hydrolysed with the transporters blocked [4]

(c) Why two curves plateau and one does not [4]

Facilitated diffusion and active transport both depend on transport proteins, of which there is a fixed number in the membrane. 1

As external concentration rises, more and more of those channel or carrier proteins are occupied, until every one of them is working at its maximum rate. All the transport proteins are saturated, so no further increase in solute concentration can raise the rate, and the curve levels off. 1

Simple diffusion of a small non-polar molecule requires no protein at all: the molecule dissolves in the hydrophobic core of the phospholipid bilayer and passes straight through. There is no limited number of sites to saturate, so the rate stays directly proportional to the concentration gradient and the line does not plateau. 1

The further circumstance that stops active transport only is the failure of the ATP supply, for instance under a respiratory inhibitor or in the absence of oxygen. The pump protein cannot be phosphorylated and so cannot change conformation to move the solute against its gradient. Facilitated diffusion is unaffected because it requires no metabolic energy. 1

(c) Ranking PCR's limitations for this sample, and why a Southern blot fails [4]

Greatest threat: contamination. PCR is so sensitive that it amplifies whatever DNA is present, and with only about 20 cells of genuine template a few molecules of DNA from the analyst or from another sample will be amplified alongside, or instead of, the target, giving a banding pattern that cannot be distinguished from a true result. On this sample the true template is at its scarcest and the contaminant is at its most competitive. 1

Second: the product size range, 0.1 to 5 kb, combined with template degradation. Forty-year-old DNA is fragmented, and PCR cannot amplify a sequence longer than the surviving template: if the strands have broken between the two primer sites, no product forms at all and the lane is blank. Third: Taq lacks 3′ to 5′ exonuclease activity, so there is no proofreading and a higher than normal frequency of replication errors. Here this matters least, because the test reads fragment length, not base sequence, and a copying error introduced in one cycle affects only a minority of the final product and would not move a band. 1

About 20 cells of degraded DNA gives far too little material, already cut into random fragments, for a probe to give a detectable band. 1
