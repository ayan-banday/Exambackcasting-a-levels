COMPLEX NUMBERS

H2 Mathematics 9758  ·  Topic 4.1  ·  2026 scope only
Cartesian form and Argand diagrams · polar and exponential form are OUT for your sitting
Read this before you start — the topic shrank for your exam. The 2026 syllabus renames this topic “Introduction to Complex numbers”, keeps a single subtopic 4.1 “Complex numbers expressed in cartesian form and Argand diagrams”, and states verbatim: “Exclude complex numbers expressed in polar (or modulus-argument) form and exponential form.” There is no mention of de Moivre's theorem, roots of unity, or loci anywhere in the content outline.

That means most Complex Numbers questions in the prelim papers you have cannot be set on your paper. Anything written \(r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\) or \(re^{i\theta}\), anything asking for “the locus of points such that \(|z-a|=|z-b|\)”, and anything using de Moivre is off your syllabus. If you meet one while practising, skip it.

What survives: modulus, argument and conjugate as quantities, the four operations, equality, complex roots of quadratics, conjugate roots of real-coefficient polynomials, the Argand diagram, and the geometrical effect of conjugating, negating, adding, subtracting, and multiplying by \(i\). That is the whole topic, and it is why 60 minutes is generous for it.

1Cartesian arithmetic, equality, and the conjugate
The four operations
Write everything as \(a+bi\) with \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\). Then \(i^2=-1\) and ordinary algebra applies.

Multiply: expand and replace \(i^2\) by \(-1\).
\((2+i)^2=4+4i+i^2=3+4i\).

Divide: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator. This is the single most-used technique in the topic.
\(\dfrac{1}{2+i}=\dfrac{2-i}{(2+i)(2-i)}=\dfrac{2-i}{4+1}=\dfrac{2}{5}-\dfrac{1}{5}i\).

Why it works: \(zz^*=|z|^2\), which is always real and non-negative.
Equality, modulus, argument, conjugate
Equality. \(a+bi=c+di \iff a=c\) and \(b=d\). Comparing real and imaginary parts turns one complex equation into two real ones — that is how most “find the constants” questions are solved.

Modulus. \(|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), the distance from the origin. \(|z|^2=zz^*\).

Argument. \(\arg z\) is the angle from the positive real axis, taken in \(-\pi<\arg z\le\pi\). Find the acute reference angle \(\tan^{-1}\left|\dfrac ba\right|\) first, then place it by quadrant.

Conjugate. \(z^*=a-bi\). Useful identities: \((z_1z_2)^*=z_1^*z_2^*\), \(z+z^*=2\,\mathrm{Re}(z)\), \(z-z^*=2i\,\mathrm{Im}(z)\).
Example
Given \(z=3+4i\), find \(|z|\), \(\arg z\), and \(\dfrac{1}{z}\) in cartesian form.

\(|z|=\sqrt{9+16}=5\).
\(z\) is in the first quadrant, so \(\arg z=\tan^{-1}\dfrac43=0.927\) rad (3 s.f.).
\(\dfrac{1}{3+4i}=\dfrac{3-4i}{(3+4i)(3-4i)}=\dfrac{3-4i}{25}=\dfrac{3}{25}-\dfrac{4}{25}i\).

Note \(\left|\dfrac1z\right|=\dfrac{1}{|z|}=\dfrac15\), which checks out: \(\sqrt{(3/25)^2+(4/25)^2}=\dfrac{5}{25}=\dfrac15\).
Trap Taking \(\arg z=\tan^{-1}\dfrac ba\) straight off the calculator. For \(z=-1+i\) that gives \(-\dfrac\pi4\), but \(z\) is in the second quadrant and the true argument is \(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\). Always sketch the point first, then place the reference angle.
Practice — do this before revealing
The complex number \(z_1\) is given by \(z_1=a+(a-3)i\), where \(a\) is a positive real constant, and \(\arg z_1=\theta\) where \(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}<\theta<0\). Find, in terms of \(\theta\), (i) \(\arg(-2z_1)\) and (ii) \(\arg(z_1-2a)\).

2Complex roots of quadratics
The formula still works
For \(az^2+bz+c=0\) with real \(a,b,c\) and \(b^2-4ac<0\):
\[z=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}=\frac{-b\pm i\sqrt{4ac-b^2}}{2a}\] The two roots are a conjugate pair. Write \(\sqrt{-n}=i\sqrt n\) and carry on.

Sum and product. \(z_1+z_2=-\dfrac ba\) and \(z_1z_2=\dfrac ca\), exactly as for real roots. These give a fast check, and often a faster route than the formula.

Building the quadratic back. If \(p+qi\) is a root of a real quadratic, the quadratic is a multiple of
\(z^2-2pz+(p^2+q^2)\), i.e. \(z^2-(\text{sum})z+(\text{product})\).
Example
Solve \(z^2-4z+13=0\).

Discriminant \(=16-52=-36<0\), so the roots are complex.
\(z=\dfrac{4\pm\sqrt{-36}}{2}=\dfrac{4\pm 6i}{2}=2\pm 3i\).

Check: sum \(=4=-\dfrac{-4}{1}\) ✓, product \(=(2+3i)(2-3i)=4+9=13\) ✓.
Trap Writing \(\sqrt{-36}=-6\). It is \(6i\). And when the coefficients are not all real, the roots need not be a conjugate pair — the conjugate-pair result depends entirely on the coefficients being real.
Practice — do this before revealing
The equation \(z^2+(2+i)z+k=0\), where \(k\) is a complex constant, has \(z=1-i\) as a root. Find \(k\) and the other root.
Non-real coefficients, so the conjugate-root shortcut is deliberately unavailable
Find \(k\): substitute \(z=1-i\).
\((1-i)^2=1-2i+i^2=-2i\).
\((2+i)(1-i)=2-2i+i-i^2=2-i+1=3-i\).
So \(-2i+3-i+k=0\Rightarrow k=-3+3i\).

Other root: sum of roots \(=-(2+i)\).
\((1-i)+z_2=-2-i\Rightarrow z_2=-2-i-1+i=-3\).

Check with the product: \(z_1z_2=(1-i)(-3)=-3+3i=k\) ✓.

The point of this question: the other root is \(-3\), which is not the conjugate of \(1-i\). Because the coefficient \(2+i\) is not real, conjugate pairing does not apply — and a real root sitting alongside a complex one is perfectly possible.
3Conjugate roots of real-coefficient polynomials
The theorem and how it is used
If a polynomial has all real coefficients and \(z=p+qi\) (with \(q\ne0\)) is a root, then \(z^*=p-qi\) is also a root.

Say the words when you use it: “since all the coefficients are real and \(p+qi\) is a root, \(p-qi\) is also a root”. That sentence is a mark.

Consequences worth knowing:
• Non-real roots come in pairs, so a polynomial of odd degree with real coefficients must have at least one real root.
• A conjugate pair \(p\pm qi\) contributes the real quadratic factor \(z^2-2pz+(p^2+q^2)\). Dividing the polynomial by that factor leaves a lower-degree real polynomial to finish.
• \((p+qi)(p-qi)=p^2+q^2=|z|^2\), so each conjugate pair contributes \(|z|^2\) to the product of the roots.
Symmetry shortcuts
If a quartic has only even powers, \(f(-z)=f(z)\), so roots come in \(\pm\) pairs as well. Combined with conjugation, one root generates all four: \(p+qi\Rightarrow p-qi,\ -p-qi,\ -p+qi\).

Spotting that symmetry turns a 6-mark question into two lines.
Example
\(z=1+2i\) is a root of \(z^3+az^2+bz+10=0\) where \(a,b\) are real. Find \(a\) and \(b\) and the third root.

Coefficients are real, so \(1-2i\) is also a root.
That pair gives the factor \(z^2-2z+5\) (sum \(2\), product \(1+4=5\)).
Product of all three roots \(=-\dfrac{10}{1}=-10\), and \((1+2i)(1-2i)=5\), so the third root is \(-\dfrac{10}{5}=-2\).

Then \(z^3+az^2+bz+10=(z^2-2z+5)(z+2)=z^3+2z^2-2z^2-4z+5z+10=z^3+z+10\).
So \(a=0\), \(b=1\).
Trap Applying conjugate pairing without checking the coefficients are real. If any coefficient is complex, the theorem is false — see the practice question in the previous section.
Practice — do this before revealing
Do not use a graphing calculator for this question. It is given that \(\mathrm{f}(z)=z^4-6z^2+k\), where \(k\) is a non-zero constant.
(a) If \(k\) is purely imaginary, determine with justification whether \(\mathrm{f}(z)=0\) can have real roots. [1]
(b) Show that \(\mathrm{f}(-z)=\mathrm{f}(z)\). [1]
(c) Given that \(2+i\) is a root of \(\mathrm{f}(z)=0\), determine \(k\), and hence find the remaining roots. [6]
(d) Given that the product of all the roots is \(D\), find \(D\). [2]
(e) A complex number \(w_1\) satisfies \(kw^4-6w^2+1=0\). Given that \(w_1\) can be obtained from \(2+i\), find \(w_1\). [2]
HCI 2025 Prelim Paper 1 Q12 · [12] · solution verbatim from the HCI mark scheme, every value re-verified symbolically
(a) Suppose \(\mathrm{f}(z)=0\) had a real root \(x\). Then \(x^4-6x^2=-k\). The left side is real but the right side is purely imaginary and non-zero, a contradiction. Hence there are no real roots.

(b) \(\mathrm{f}(-z)=(-z)^4-6(-z)^2+k=z^4-6z^2+k=\mathrm{f}(z)\). Shown.

(c) \((2+i)^2=4+4i+i^2=3+4i\), and \((2+i)^4=(3+4i)^2=9+24i+16i^2=-7+24i\).
Substituting: \(-7+24i-6(3+4i)+k=0\Rightarrow -7+24i-18-24i+k=0\Rightarrow \boxed{k=25}\).
Since all coefficients are real and \(2+i\) is a root, \(2-i\) is also a root.
Since \(\mathrm{f}(-z)=\mathrm{f}(z)\), if \(z\) is a root then so is \(-z\).
Remaining roots: \(2-i,\ -2-i,\ -2+i\).

(d) \((2+i)(2-i)=4-i^2=5\) and \((-2+i)(-2-i)=4-i^2=5\).
\(D=5\times5=25\). (Consistent with the constant term: for a monic quartic the product of the roots is \(+k=25\).)

(e) Start from \(z^4-6z^2+25=0\) and divide through by \(z^4\):
\(1-\dfrac{6}{z^2}+\dfrac{25}{z^4}=0\), i.e. \(25\left(\dfrac1z\right)^4-6\left(\dfrac1z\right)^2+1=0\).
So \(w=\dfrac1z\) satisfies the new equation, and with \(z=2+i\):
\(w_1=\dfrac{1}{2+i}=\dfrac{2-i}{5}=\boxed{\dfrac25-\dfrac15 i}\).

A second valid route the HCI key also gives: multiply \(25w^4-6w^2+1=0\) by \(25\) to get \((5w)^4-6(5w)^2+25=0\), so \(5w\) is a root of the original, giving \(w=\dfrac{2+i}{5}=\dfrac25+\dfrac15 i\). Both are genuine roots — the four roots of the new equation are \(\dfrac{\pm2\pm i}{5}\), so either answer is defensible.
4Argand diagrams and geometrical effects
What each operation does to the point
OperationEffect on the Argand diagram
\(z^*\)Reflection in the real axis. \(|z^*|=|z|\), \(\arg z^*=-\arg z\)
\(-z\)Rotation by \(\pi\) about \(O\) (equivalently, reflection in \(O\)). \(\arg(-z)=\arg z\pm\pi\)
\(z_1+z_2\)Vector addition — the parallelogram rule
\(z_1-z_2\)The vector from \(z_2\) to \(z_1\). So \(|z_1-z_2|\) is the distance between the two points
\(iz\)Rotation by \(+\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) about \(O\). Modulus unchanged
\(kz\), \(k>0\) realScaling by \(k\) from \(O\). Argument unchanged
The reading that unlocks most questions: \(|z_1-z_2|\) is a distance and \(\arg(z_1-z_2)\) is the direction of the line from \(z_2\) to \(z_1\).
Example
\(z=3+i\). Plot \(z\), \(z^*\), \(iz\) and \(-z\), and describe the quadrilateral they form.

\(z=3+i\) → \((3,1)\).
\(z^*=3-i\) → \((3,-1)\), the reflection in the real axis.
\(iz=i(3+i)=3i+i^2=-1+3i\) → \((-1,3)\), which is \(z\) rotated a quarter turn anticlockwise.
\(-z=-3-i\) → \((-3,-1)\).

All four lie on the circle of radius \(\sqrt{10}\) centred at \(O\), since each operation preserves the modulus.
Trap Reading \(|z_1-z_2|\) as “the difference of the moduli”. It is the distance between the points, and in general \(|z_1-z_2|\ne|z_1|-|z_2|\). The triangle inequality \(|z_1+z_2|\le|z_1|+|z_2|\) is the correct relation between them.
Practice — do this before revealing
A curve \(C\) has equation \(x^2-3y^2=3\). A complex number is given by \(z=x+iy\), where \(x\) and \(y\) are real and satisfy that equation, and \(\mathrm{Re}(z)\) is positive. State the behaviour of \(\arg z\) as \(\mathrm{Re}(z)\to\infty\).
HCI 2022 Prelim Paper 1 Q4(ii) · [3] · a conic and an argument fused in one question
Divide the conic by 3: \(\dfrac{x^2}{3}-y^2=1\), a hyperbola opening left and right with asymptotes \(y=\pm\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}x\).

\(\mathrm{Re}(z)=x>0\) selects the right-hand branch, which has two arms: one going up along \(y=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}x\), one going down along \(y=-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}x\).

As \(x\to\infty\) the point approaches an asymptote, and \(\arg z=\tan^{-1}\dfrac yx\to\tan^{-1}\left(\pm\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\right)\).

So \(\arg z\to\dfrac{\pi}{6}\) for the upper arm and \(\arg z\to-\dfrac{\pi}{6}\) for the lower arm.
The argument does not tend to a single value — it tends to \(\pm\dfrac\pi6\) depending on which arm the point runs along, and it approaches those values without reaching them.

Why this works: \(\arg z\) is the angle the line \(Oz\) makes with the real axis, and far out along the branch that line becomes parallel to an asymptote. The asymptote gradient is the limiting tangent of the argument.
