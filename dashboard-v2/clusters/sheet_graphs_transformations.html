1Conics — the five standard shapes
The forms you must recognise on sight
EquationShapeKey features
\(y^2=ax\)Parabola, opens along \(x\)Vertex \((0,0)\); symmetric about the \(x\)-axis; opens right if \(a>0\)
\(x^2=by\)Parabola, opens along \(y\)Vertex \((0,0)\); symmetric about the \(y\)-axis
\(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=1\)EllipseCentre \((0,0)\); \(x\)-intercepts \(\pm a\), \(y\)-intercepts \(\pm b\). No asymptotes. \(-a\le x\le a\), \(-b\le y\le b\)
\(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=1\)Hyperbola, opens left/rightVertices \((\pm a,0)\); no \(y\)-intercept; asymptotes \(y=\pm\dfrac{b}{a}x\); \(|x|\ge a\)
\(\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}-\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}=1\)Hyperbola, opens up/downVertices \((0,\pm b)\); no \(x\)-intercept; asymptotes \(y=\pm\dfrac{b}{a}x\); \(|y|\ge b\)
Shifted centre. Replace \(x\) by \(x-h\) and \(y\) by \(y-k\) to move the centre to \((h,k)\). Every feature shifts with it, asymptotes included.
Getting a conic into standard form
Divide through until the right-hand side is \(1\). For \(x^2-3y^2=3\): divide by 3 to get \(\dfrac{x^2}{3}-\dfrac{y^2}{1}=1\), so \(a=\sqrt3\), \(b=1\).
Example
Sketch \(x^2-3y^2=3\), giving asymptotes and intercepts in exact form.

Divide by 3: \(\dfrac{x^2}{3}-\dfrac{y^2}{1}=1\). This is the left/right hyperbola with \(a=\sqrt3\), \(b=1\).
\(x\)-intercepts: set \(y=0\) ⇒ \(x^2=3\) ⇒ \(x=\pm\sqrt3\).
\(y\)-intercepts: set \(x=0\) ⇒ \(-3y^2=3\) ⇒ \(y^2=-1\), no real solution, so no \(y\)-intercept.
Asymptotes: \(y=\pm\dfrac{b}{a}x=\pm\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}x\).
Two branches, one through \((\sqrt3,0)\) opening right, one through \((-\sqrt3,0)\) opening left, each approaching the asymptotes.
Trap Quoting \(y=\pm\dfrac{a}{b}x\) instead of \(y=\pm\dfrac{b}{a}x\). The gradient is the \(y\)-denominator over the \(x\)-denominator. Check it against the vertices: the curve must open towards the axis whose variable appears with the \(+\) sign.
Practice — do this before revealing
An ellipse has equation \(\dfrac{x^2}{4}+\dfrac{(y-1)^2}{9}=1\). Sketch it, stating the centre, the exact axial intercepts, and the range of possible values of \(x\) and of \(y\).
Standard-form conic with a shifted centre · the 2021 HCI P2 Q2 ellipse is the same idea with a "clear workings" instruction
Centre \((0,1)\). Semi-axis \(2\) in \(x\), \(3\) in \(y\).
\(y\)-intercepts: \(x=0\Rightarrow (y-1)^2=9\Rightarrow y=4\) or \(y=-2\), so \((0,4)\) and \((0,-2)\).
\(x\)-intercepts: \(y=0\Rightarrow \dfrac{x^2}{4}+\dfrac{1}{9}=1\Rightarrow x^2=\dfrac{32}{9}\Rightarrow x=\pm\dfrac{4\sqrt2}{3}\).
Restrictions: \(-2\le x\le 2\) and \(-2\le y\le 4\).
No asymptotes — an ellipse is a closed curve.
2Rational functions and their asymptotes
Two families, two different asymptote rules
(a) \(y=\dfrac{ax+b}{cx+d}\) — linear over linear.
Vertical asymptote where the denominator is zero: \(x=-\dfrac{d}{c}\).
Horizontal asymptote \(y=\dfrac{a}{c}\) (divide the leading coefficients).

(b) \(y=\dfrac{ax^2+bx+c}{dx+e}\) — quadratic over linear.
Vertical asymptote \(x=-\dfrac{e}{d}\).
Oblique asymptote: long-divide to write \(y=(\text{linear})+\dfrac{R}{dx+e}\). The linear part is the oblique asymptote, because the remainder term \(\to 0\) as \(x\to\pm\infty\).
Restrictions on \(y\) — the discriminant method
To find which \(y\)-values are impossible: rearrange the equation into a quadratic in \(x\) with \(y\) as a parameter, then impose \(\text{discriminant}\ge 0\) for real \(x\) to exist. The set of \(y\) failing that test is the gap in the range.
Example
Find the asymptotes and the set of values \(y\) cannot take for \(y=\dfrac{x^2+1}{x-1}\).

Long division: \(\dfrac{x^2+1}{x-1}=x+1+\dfrac{2}{x-1}\).
Vertical asymptote \(x=1\); oblique asymptote \(y=x+1\).

Range: \(y(x-1)=x^2+1 \Rightarrow x^2-yx+(1+y)=0\).
Real \(x\) needs \(y^2-4(1+y)\ge 0 \Rightarrow y^2-4y-4\ge 0\).
Roots \(y=\dfrac{4\pm\sqrt{16+16}}{2}=2\pm 2\sqrt2\).
So \(y\le 2-2\sqrt2\) or \(y\ge 2+2\sqrt2\); the curve cannot take \(2-2\sqrt2
Trap Reading the horizontal asymptote off a quadratic-over-linear. There isn't one — it is oblique, and you only get it by long division. Conversely, a linear-over-linear never has an oblique asymptote.
Practice — do this before revealing
A function is defined by \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^2-2x+k}{x-2}\), where \(k\) is a constant. Find, in terms of \(k\), the equations of the asymptotes of \(y=f(x)\).
Same shape as HCI 2022 Prelim P1 Q5 (a function \(\frac{\cdot}{x^2+k}\) with a constant to carry through)
Long-divide: \(x^2-2x+k=(x-2)(x)+k\), so \(f(x)=x+\dfrac{k}{x-2}\).
Vertical asymptote \(x=2\) (provided \(k\ne 0\); if \(k=0\) the factor cancels and there is no asymptote, just a hole).
Oblique asymptote \(y=x\).
Note the oblique asymptote does not depend on \(k\) at all — \(k\) only controls how fast the curve approaches it.
3Transformations — and the order they act in
The four single moves
New equationWhat happensDirection
\(y=f(x)+a\)Translate \(a\) units in the \(+y\) directionOutside, so as expected
\(y=af(x)\)Stretch parallel to \(y\), factor \(a\)Outside, so as expected
\(y=f(x+a)\)Translate \(a\) units in the \(-x\) directionInside, so opposite
\(y=f(ax)\)Stretch parallel to \(x\), factor \(\dfrac{1}{a}\)Inside, so reciprocal
Reflections are the special cases \(a=-1\): \(y=-f(x)\) reflects in the \(x\)-axis, \(y=f(-x)\) reflects in the \(y\)-axis.
Order — the rule that decides the marks
Anything outside \(f\) acts on \(y\); anything inside acts on \(x\). Within each group, work outwards from \(x\) when going \(f(x)\to\) target, and inwards when reversing.

For \(y=f(3x-2)+a\), the inside is \(3x-2=3\left(x-\tfrac23\right)\). Going from \(f(x)\): translate \(\tfrac23\) in \(+x\), then stretch factor \(\tfrac13\) parallel to \(x\), then translate \(a\) in \(+y\).
Going the other way (from \(G\) back to \(f\)) every step reverses and the order flips.
Example
State a sequence of 3 transformations taking \(y=x^2\) to \(y=-x^2+3x-4\).

Complete the square first — you cannot read transformations off an expanded quadratic:
\(-x^2+3x-4=-\left(x^2-3x\right)-4=-\left[\left(x-\tfrac32\right)^2-\tfrac94\right]-4=-\left(x-\tfrac32\right)^2-\tfrac74\).

So from \(y=x^2\):
1. Translate \(\tfrac32\) units in the \(+x\) direction → \(y=\left(x-\tfrac32\right)^2\).
2. Reflect in the \(x\)-axis → \(y=-\left(x-\tfrac32\right)^2\).
3. Translate \(\tfrac74\) units in the \(-y\) direction → \(y=-\left(x-\tfrac32\right)^2-\tfrac74\).
Trap Two things lose marks here every year. First, not completing the square — you cannot see the translation inside an expanded form. Second, describing a transformation without saying how far and in which direction: “a translation” scores nothing, “a translation of \(\tfrac32\) units in the positive \(x\)-direction” scores the mark.

4Related graphs — |f(x)|, f(|x|) and 1/f(x)
The three transformations you must be able to do by hand
\(y=|f(x)|\) — reflect every part of the curve below the \(x\)-axis up into it. Everything above is unchanged. Sharp corners appear at the old \(x\)-intercepts.

\(y=f(|x|)\) — delete everything with \(x<0\), then reflect the \(x\ge 0\) part in the \(y\)-axis. The result is always symmetric about the \(y\)-axis.

\(y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}\) — the one with the most marks in it:
• \(x\)-intercept of \(f\) at \(x=k\) → vertical asymptote \(x=k\).
• Vertical asymptote of \(f\) at \(x=k\) → \(x\)-intercept at \(x=k\)... more precisely the curve tends to \(0\).
• Horizontal asymptote \(y=c\ (c\ne0)\) → horizontal asymptote \(y=\dfrac1c\).
• Maximum point \((p,q)\) with \(q\ne 0\) → minimum point \(\left(p,\dfrac1q\right)\), and vice versa.
• The sign never changes, and points where \(f=\pm1\) stay put.
Example
\(y=f(x)\) has a maximum at \((2,-3)\), an \(x\)-intercept at \(x=5\) and a horizontal asymptote \(y=-4\). Describe \(y=\dfrac{1}{f(x)}\).

Turning point. At \(x=2\), \(f=-3\) so \(\dfrac1f=-\dfrac13\). Just off the maximum, \(f\) is more negative, say \(-4\), giving \(\dfrac1f=-\dfrac14\), which is larger. So the reciprocal is smallest at \(x=2\): the maximum of \(f\) becomes a minimum of \(\dfrac1f\), at \(\left(2,-\dfrac13\right)\).
\(x\)-intercept at \(x=5\) → vertical asymptote \(x=5\).
Horizontal asymptote \(y=-4\) → horizontal asymptote \(y=-\tfrac14\).
Trap Forgetting that a turning point of \(f\) at height \(q\) maps to height \(\dfrac1q\), not to \(-q\) or to \(q\). And a turning point on the \(x\)-axis (\(q=0\)) does not map to a point at all — it becomes a vertical asymptote.

5Parametric curves
Sketching and converting
To sketch: either use the GC in parametric mode (MODE → PAR, and set a sensible \(T\)-range), or build a small table of \(t\) against \((x,y)\) and mark the direction of increasing \(t\).

To convert to cartesian: eliminate the parameter. Make \(t\) the subject of the simpler equation and substitute; or use an identity such as \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\) or \(1+\cot^2\theta=\csc^2\theta\).

Gradient: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}\). Then substitute the given \(t\) to get a number before writing any tangent or normal.

Restrictions matter. The stated range of \(t\) often means only part of the cartesian curve is drawn — say so.
Example
\(x=t^2\), \(y=t^3+1\) for \(t\in\mathbb{R}\). Find the equation of the tangent at \(t=1\).

\(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=2t\), \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=3t^2\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{3t^2}{2t}=\dfrac{3t}{2}\) for \(t\ne0\).
At \(t=1\): gradient \(=\dfrac32\), point \(=(1,2)\).
Tangent: \(y-2=\dfrac32(x-1)\), i.e. \(y=\dfrac32x+\dfrac12\).

Note \(x=t^2\ge 0\) always, so the whole curve lies in \(x\ge0\).
Trap Leaving \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) in terms of \(t\) and writing the tangent with a \(t\) in it. Substitute the parameter value first. This is the single most common parametric slip.
Practice — do this before revealing
A curve \(C\) has parametric equations \(x=t^2\), \(y=t^3+1\) for \(t\in\mathbb{R}\). The tangent to \(C\) at the point \(P\) where \(t=1\) meets the curve again at \(Q\). Find the exact coordinates of \(Q\).
HCI 2023 Prelim Paper 2 Q4(c) · [3]
The tangent is \(y=\dfrac32x+\dfrac12\) from the example. A general point on \(C\) is \((t^2,\ t^3+1)\).
Substitute: \(t^3+1=\dfrac32t^2+\dfrac12 \Rightarrow 2t^3-3t^2+1=0\).
\(t=1\) is a root (it is \(P\), and a repeated root because the line is a tangent there), so factorise:
\(2t^3-3t^2+1=(t-1)^2(2t+1)\).
The new root is \(t=-\dfrac12\), giving \(Q=\left(\dfrac14,\ 1-\dfrac18\right)=\left(\dfrac14,\ \dfrac78\right)\).

The idea worth keeping: a tangent touching at \(t=1\) forces \((t-1)^2\) as a factor. Spotting the repeated root is what turns a cubic into a one-line factorisation.
