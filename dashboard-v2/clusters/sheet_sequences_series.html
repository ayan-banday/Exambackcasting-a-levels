1AP and GP — the formulae and the standard traps
Everything you need, in one block
Arithmetic (AP)Geometric (GP)

All of these are in MF27 — do not waste memory on them, waste it on knowing when each applies.

Proving a sequence is a GP means showing \(\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}\) is a constant independent of \(n\). Write the ratio out and say the words.
Where marks actually go
• “Sum of the first \(n\) even-numbered terms” — these form a new AP or GP. For an AP, the odd-numbered terms \(u_1,u_3,u_5,\dots\) form an AP with first term \(a\) and common difference \(2d\).
• Number of terms. From \(u_k\) to \(u_m\) inclusive there are \(m-k+1\) terms, not \(m-k\).
• “Least \(n\) such that ...” — solve the inequality, then round up to the next integer and verify by substituting \(n\) and \(n-1\).
Example
A GP has \(S_8:S_4=17:16\). Find the possible common ratios.

\(\dfrac{S_8}{S_4}=\dfrac{a(1-r^8)/(1-r)}{a(1-r^4)/(1-r)}=\dfrac{1-r^8}{1-r^4}=\dfrac{(1-r^4)(1+r^4)}{1-r^4}=1+r^4\).
So \(1+r^4=\dfrac{17}{16}\Rightarrow r^4=\dfrac1{16}\Rightarrow r=\pm\dfrac12\).

The factorisation \(1-r^8=(1-r^4)(1+r^4)\) is what makes this a two-line question instead of a mess.
Trap Using \(S_\infty=\dfrac{a}{1-r}\) without first checking and stating \(|r|<1\). If the question gives you \(r\) in terms of an unknown, the condition \(|r|<1\) is usually the source of a whole sub-part asking for the range of that unknown.
Practice — do this before revealing
The first term of an arithmetic series is positive. The sum of the first 6 terms is 4.5, and the product of the first four terms is 0. Find the 13th term of the series.
HCI 2022 Prelim Paper 2 Q4(b) · [4]
The product of the first four terms is \(0\), so one of them is zero. Since \(a>0\), it is not the first. So \(a+d=0\), \(a+2d=0\) or \(a+3d=0\).

\(S_6=\dfrac62\big[2a+5d\big]=3(2a+5d)=4.5\Rightarrow 2a+5d=1.5\).

Case \(a+d=0\): \(a=-d\Rightarrow -2d+5d=3d=1.5\Rightarrow d=0.5,\ a=-0.5\). Rejected, \(a>0\) fails.
Case \(a+2d=0\): \(a=-2d\Rightarrow -4d+5d=d=1.5\Rightarrow d=1.5,\ a=-3\). Rejected.
Case \(a+3d=0\): \(a=-3d\Rightarrow -6d+5d=-d=1.5\Rightarrow d=-1.5,\ a=4.5\). Accepted.

Check: first four terms are \(4.5,\ 3,\ 1.5,\ 0\) — product zero ✓, and \(S_6=4.5+3+1.5+0-1.5-3=4.5\) ✓.

\(u_{13}=a+12d=4.5+12(-1.5)=\boxed{-13.5}\).

The move worth keeping: “product is zero” is never an algebra problem — it is a case split. Enumerate which factor vanishes, and use the other condition to kill the impossible cases.
2The link between \(u_n\) and \(S_n\)
One formula, used constantly
\[u_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\quad\text{for }n\ge 2,\qquad u_1=S_1\] The \(n\ge2\) restriction matters: \(S_0\) is not defined by the formula, so \(u_1\) must always be checked separately. Sometimes the general expression happens to give the right \(u_1\) and sometimes it does not — say which.

Going the other way, \(S_n=\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}u_r\).

Useful standard results (all in MF27):
\(\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}r=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\), \(\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}r^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}\), \(\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}r^3=\left[\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right]^2\).
Shifting the index
\(\displaystyle\sum_{r=k}^{n}u_r=\sum_{r=1}^{n}u_r-\sum_{r=1}^{k-1}u_r\). Subtract the part you do not want — and note the upper limit \(k-1\), not \(k\).

For a shifted summand, substitute: \(\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}u_{r+2}=\sum_{s=3}^{n+2}u_s\).
Example
The sum of the first \(n\) terms of a series is \(S_n=3n(n+2)\). Show the series is an AP and find its first term and common difference.

For \(n\ge2\): \(u_n=S_n-S_{n-1}=3n(n+2)-3(n-1)(n+1)=3(n^2+2n)-3(n^2-1)=6n+3\).
Check \(n=1\): the formula gives \(9\), and \(S_1=3(1)(3)=9\) ✓, so \(u_n=6n+3\) holds for all \(n\ge1\).

\(u_{n+1}-u_n=6(n+1)+3-(6n+3)=6\), a constant, so the series is an AP with \(a=9\), \(d=6\).
Trap Applying \(u_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\) at \(n=1\) without checking. Always compute \(u_1=S_1\) separately and compare — and if they differ, say so and define the sequence piecewise.
Practice — do this before revealing
A data processing centre processes \(u_n\) units of data on day \(n\), where \(u_{n+1}=(1+p)u_n\) and \(p\) is a positive real constant.
(a) Explain why \(\{u_n\}\) is a geometric progression.
(b) On day 1, \(2^m\) units were processed. The total processed over the first three days is \(\dfrac{127}{36}\left(2^m\right)\) units. Find \(p\).
(c) Explain whether there is a limit to the total amount of data the centre can handle in the long run.
HCI 2023 Prelim Paper 1 Q12(a)–(c) · [1] [3] [1]
(a) \(\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=1+p\), which is a constant independent of \(n\). A sequence whose consecutive terms have a constant ratio is a geometric progression. (And \(1+p\ne 0\) since \(p>0\).)

(b) With \(a=2^m\) and \(r=1+p\):
\(u_1+u_2+u_3=2^m\left[1+(1+p)+(1+p)^2\right]=\dfrac{127}{36}\,2^m\).
Divide by \(2^m\) and let \(t=1+p\): \(1+t+t^2=\dfrac{127}{36}\).
\(36t^2+36t+36=127\Rightarrow 36t^2+36t-91=0\).
Discriminant \(=36^2+4(36)(91)=1296+13104=14400=120^2\).
\(t=\dfrac{-36\pm120}{72}=\dfrac{84}{72}=\dfrac76\ \text{ or }\ -\dfrac{13}{6}\).
Since \(p>0\), \(t=1+p>1\), so \(t=\dfrac76\) and \(\boxed{p=\dfrac16}\).

(c) The common ratio is \(r=1+p>1\), so \(|r|>1\) and the geometric series diverges. There is no limit — the cumulative total grows without bound.

Note how (a) pays for (c): once you have named it a GP with ratio \(1+p\), the convergence question is answered by a one-line appeal to \(|r|<1\) failing. Sub-parts in this topic are almost always chained like that.
3Recurrence relations
Reading and using \(u_{n+1}=f(u_n)\)
A recurrence defines each term from the previous one, plus a starting value. To work with it:

Generating terms. On the TI-84: MODE → SEQ, then in Y= set nMin, u(n) in terms of u(n-1), and u(nMin). Use TABLE to read terms off.

Finding unknown constants. Substitute the given terms to build simultaneous equations, then solve — on the GC if a calculator is allowed.

Limiting behaviour. If \(u_n\to L\) then \(u_{n+1}\to L\) too, so put \(L=f(L)\) and solve. State that the sequence converges before doing it; a sequence that diverges has no such \(L\).

Proving a related sequence is an AP or GP. Form \(v_{n+1}-v_n\) or \(\dfrac{v_{n+1}}{v_n}\) from the recurrence and simplify until the \(n\) cancels.
Example
A sequence satisfies \(u_{n+1}=\dfrac{1}{2}u_n+3\) with \(u_1=1\). Given that it converges, find its limit.

Let \(u_n\to L\). Then \(u_{n+1}\to L\) as well, so
\(L=\dfrac12L+3\Rightarrow \dfrac12 L=3\Rightarrow L=6\).

Sanity check by iterating: \(1,\ 3.5,\ 4.75,\ 5.375,\ 5.6875,\dots\) climbing towards \(6\) ✓.
Trap Solving \(L=f(L)\) without being told, or without showing, that the sequence converges. If \(|f'|>1\) near the fixed point the sequence runs away and \(L\) is meaningless — the equation still has a root, but it is not a limit.
Practice — do this before revealing
A sequence of real numbers \(x_n\) satisfies \(x_{n+1}=a(-3)^n+bn^2+cx_n\) for \(n\ge1\), where \(a,b,c\) are constants. Given \(x_1=1\), \(x_2=-8\), \(x_3=70\) and \(x_4=-377\), find \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\).
HCI 2025 Prelim Paper 1 Q2 · [3]
Substitute \(n=1,2,3\) in turn.

\(n=1\): \(x_2=a(-3)^1+b(1)^2+cx_1\Rightarrow -8=-3a+b+c\).
\(n=2\): \(x_3=a(-3)^2+b(2)^2+cx_2\Rightarrow 70=9a+4b-8c\).
\(n=3\): \(x_4=a(-3)^3+b(3)^2+cx_3\Rightarrow -377=-27a+9b+70c\).

Three linear equations in \(a,b,c\). On the GC: APPS → PlySmlt2 → SIMULT EQN SOLVER, 3 equations and 3 unknowns, coefficient matrix
\(\begin{pmatrix}-3&1&1\\9&4&-8\\-27&9&70\end{pmatrix}\), right-hand side \(\begin{pmatrix}-8\\70\\-377\end{pmatrix}\).

This gives \(\boxed{a=2,\ b=3,\ c=-5}\).

Now verify by running the recurrence forward — not by re-substituting into your own equations:
\(x_2=2(-3)^1+3(1)^2+(-5)(1)=-6+3-5=-8\) ✓
\(x_3=2(-3)^2+3(2)^2+(-5)(-8)=18+12+40=70\) ✓
\(x_4=2(-3)^3+3(3)^2+(-5)(70)=-54+27-350=-377\) ✓

All four given terms reproduce, so the constants are right. Continuing the iteration gives \(x_{10}=-7\,210\,868\) and \(x_{11}=36\,172\,738\).

The lesson this question is really teaching: a recurrence with three unknowns is a linear system in disguise, and it must be solved on the GC and then checked by forward-iterating the recurrence. Substituting back into your own equations only proves you solved them; iterating the recurrence proves you set them up right.
4Method of differences and telescoping sums
The technique
When a summand splits into a difference of consecutive terms of some sequence \(f\), the sum telescopes:
\[\sum_{r=1}^{n}\big[f(r)-f(r+1)\big]=f(1)-f(n+1)\] Procedure:
1. Split the summand — usually by partial fractions, or by spotting a given identity in an earlier “show that” part.
2. Write out the first two or three terms and the last two, in full, with the cancellation visible. Examiners want to see the cancellation, not just the answer.
3. Collect what survives.
4. For the infinite sum, let \(n\to\infty\) and state which terms vanish.

Shifted gaps. If the split is \(f(r)-f(r+2)\), two terms survive at each end, not one. Always write out enough terms to see how many.
Example
Find \(\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}\frac{1}{r(r+1)}\) and hence the sum to infinity.

Partial fractions: \(\dfrac{1}{r(r+1)}=\dfrac1r-\dfrac1{r+1}\).

\(\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left(\dfrac1r-\dfrac1{r+1}\right) =\left(1-\tfrac12\right)+\left(\tfrac12-\tfrac13\right)+\cdots+\left(\tfrac1n-\tfrac1{n+1}\right)\)
Everything cancels except the first and last: \(=1-\dfrac{1}{n+1}=\dfrac{n}{n+1}\).

As \(n\to\infty\), \(\dfrac{1}{n+1}\to0\), so the sum to infinity is \(\boxed{1}\).
Trap Jumping to “everything cancels except the first and last” without writing the terms out. With a gap of 2 or more, that statement is false, and it is exactly the case examiners set.
Practice — do this before revealing
(a) Show that \(\dfrac{1}{r!}-\dfrac{1}{(r+1)!}=\dfrac{r}{(r+1)!}\).
(b) Hence find \(\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}\frac{r}{(r+1)!}\), and state the sum to infinity.
The telescoping-factorial pattern; HCI set a factorial series in 2025 Paper 1 Q7 and SAJC set this exact identity in a 2023 prelim
(a) \(\dfrac{1}{r!}-\dfrac{1}{(r+1)!}=\dfrac{(r+1)}{(r+1)!}-\dfrac{1}{(r+1)!}=\dfrac{(r+1)-1}{(r+1)!}=\dfrac{r}{(r+1)!}\). Shown.
(Using \((r+1)!=(r+1)\cdot r!\), so \(\dfrac{1}{r!}=\dfrac{r+1}{(r+1)!}\).)

(b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}\frac{r}{(r+1)!}=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left[\frac{1}{r!}-\frac{1}{(r+1)!}\right]\)
\(=\left(\dfrac{1}{1!}-\dfrac{1}{2!}\right)+\left(\dfrac{1}{2!}-\dfrac{1}{3!}\right)+\cdots+\left(\dfrac{1}{n!}-\dfrac{1}{(n+1)!}\right)\)
\(=\dfrac{1}{1!}-\dfrac{1}{(n+1)!}=\boxed{1-\dfrac{1}{(n+1)!}}\).

Sum to infinity: \((n+1)!\to\infty\), so \(\dfrac{1}{(n+1)!}\to0\) and the sum converges to \(\boxed{1}\).

Check with \(n=2\): \(\dfrac{1}{2!}+\dfrac{2}{3!}=\dfrac12+\dfrac13=\dfrac56\), and the formula gives \(1-\dfrac{1}{3!}=1-\dfrac16=\dfrac56\) ✓.
5Convergence, and sums of two series
What converges and what does not
Geometric. \(\sum ar^{\,r-1}\) converges \(\iff |r|<1\), to \(\dfrac{a}{1-r}\). This is the only convergence test in the syllabus, so almost every convergence question reduces to it.

Arithmetic. Never converges for a non-trivial AP — the terms do not tend to zero.

Telescoping. Converges when the surviving tail term tends to a limit. Compute \(S_n\) first, then take the limit.

Combining series. \(\displaystyle\sum(u_r\pm v_r)=\sum u_r\pm\sum v_r\), valid whenever both sides make sense. Split a mixed summand into the parts you can already do — typically one geometric and one telescoping, or one telescoping and one standard \(\sum r\).

Convergence of a sum: if both parts converge, so does the total, and the limits add. If one part diverges, the total diverges.
Example
Find \(\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left[\left(\tfrac13\right)^{r}+\frac{1}{r(r+1)}\right]\) and determine whether it converges.

Split it. The geometric part has \(a=\tfrac13\), \(r=\tfrac13\):
\(\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left(\tfrac13\right)^r=\frac{\tfrac13\left(1-\left(\tfrac13\right)^n\right)}{1-\tfrac13}=\frac12\left(1-\left(\tfrac13\right)^n\right)\).

The telescoping part is \(\dfrac{n}{n+1}\) from the previous section.

Total: \(\dfrac12\left(1-\left(\tfrac13\right)^n\right)+\dfrac{n}{n+1}\).
As \(n\to\infty\): \(\left(\tfrac13\right)^n\to0\) since \(\left|\tfrac13\right|<1\), and \(\dfrac{n}{n+1}\to1\).
The series converges to \(\dfrac12+1=\dfrac32\).
Trap Quoting \(\dfrac{a}{1-r}\) with \(a\) taken as the index-1 value of the formula rather than the actual first term. For \(\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left(\tfrac13\right)^r\) the first term is \(\tfrac13\), not \(1\). Write out \(u_1\) explicitly before using any GP formula.
Practice — do this before revealing
An infinite geometric series has first term \(a\) and common ratio \(r\), where \(a\ne0\). The sum of the series is \(4a\).
(a) Find \(r\), and verify that the series converges.
(b) The first, third and fifth terms of this series form a new geometric series. Find its sum to infinity in terms of \(a\).
Built on the infinite-GP structures HCI and NJC both set in 2025 prelims
(a) \(S_\infty=\dfrac{a}{1-r}=4a\). Since \(a\ne0\), divide by \(a\): \(\dfrac{1}{1-r}=4\Rightarrow 1-r=\dfrac14\Rightarrow \boxed{r=\dfrac34}\).
\(|r|=\dfrac34<1\), so the series does converge and the use of \(S_\infty\) was legitimate.

(b) The terms \(u_1,u_3,u_5,\dots\) are \(a,\ ar^2,\ ar^4,\dots\) — a GP with first term \(a\) and common ratio \(r^2=\dfrac{9}{16}\).
\(\left|\dfrac{9}{16}\right|<1\), so it converges:
\(S_\infty=\dfrac{a}{1-\frac{9}{16}}=\dfrac{a}{\frac{7}{16}}=\boxed{\dfrac{16a}{7}}\).

Sanity check: the odd-numbered terms are a subset of the original series, all with the same sign as \(a\), so their sum should be smaller in magnitude than \(4a\). \(\dfrac{16}{7}\approx2.29<4\) ✓.
