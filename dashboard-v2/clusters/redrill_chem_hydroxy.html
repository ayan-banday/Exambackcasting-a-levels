1   Which O–H is the strongest acidTotal: 11

Hydroquinone (benzene-1,4-diol), phenol and ethanol all contain an O–H group, but they do not all behave as acids to the same extent.

(a) Write an equation for the reaction of hydroquinone (benzene-1,4-diol) with excess \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\). [1]
HCI Soln · N2020 · P2 · Q4(a)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • \(\ce{HO-C6H4-OH + 2OH^{-} -> ^{-}O-C6H4-O^{-} + 2H2O}\) 1
  • Phenol is acidic, hence in excess strong base \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\) both phenolic groups are deprotonated.
The word "excess" is doing the work. Two phenolic \(\ce{-OH}\) groups means two \(\ce{OH-}\) and two \(\ce{H2O}\), and a dianion product. Balance the charge before you move on: −2 on the left from the hydroxide, −2 on the right on the dianion.
(b) Describe an experiment to compare the acidity of phenol, ethanol and hydroquinone, and explain the order of acidity. [4]
HCI Soln · N2020 · P2 · Q4(b)  ·  species order scrambled
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • Add Na metal to each and record the time taken for a fixed volume (e.g. 10 \(\ce{cm^3}\)) of \(\ce{H2}\) gas to be collected for each compound 1
  • Since acidity increases from ethanol to hydroquinone to phenol, the time taken is shortest for phenol and longest for ethanol (rate fastest for phenol, slowest for ethanol) 1
  • The electron-donating alkyl group intensifies the negative charge on the oxygen of the ethoxide ion, so ethanol is the weakest acid 1
  • Both phenoxides have resonance stabilisation due to delocalisation of the negative charge into the benzene ring, so phenol and hydroquinone are stronger acids than ethanol. However, hydroquinone has an additional electron-donating OH group on the ring, which intensifies the negative charge on the O and destabilises its conjugate base; hence phenol is a stronger acid than hydroquinone and gives the greatest rate of \(\ce{H2}\) production 1

Note from the key: you cannot differentiate ethanol from phenol by the volume of gas collected, as both give the same volume (0.010/2 × 24000 = 120 \(\ce{cm^3}\)), so collecting 10 \(\ce{cm^3}\) is feasible. The discriminator is time, not volume.

Ranking: phenol > hydroquinone > ethanol.
You have ranked this backwards twice, confidently. The order is phenol > hydroquinone > ethanol. The trap is assuming a second \(\ce{-OH}\) on the ring must withdraw. It does not — an \(\ce{-OH}\) attached to a benzene ring is electron-DONATING into the ring (its O lone pair delocalises in), which pushes charge back onto the phenoxide O and destabilises the conjugate base. Second trap: this is an experimental-design question, not a theory question. The design is Na metal, effervescence of \(\ce{H2}\), and a timed fixed volume of gas — which is also how you quantify \(\ce{-OH}\) groups, since each \(\ce{-OH}\) gives ½ mol \(\ce{H2}\).
(c) Which reagent will react with neither cyclohexanol nor phenol under suitable conditions? [1]
A  \(\ce{Na2Cr2O7}\)     B  \(\ce{Na}\)     C  \(\ce{NaOH}\)     D  \(\ce{NaHCO3}\)
HCI Prelim · N2023 · P1 · Q23
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
D 1
  • \(\ce{NaHCO3}\) only reacts with acids at least as strong as carbonic acid (i.e. carboxylic acids). Phenol is too weak an acid to liberate \(\ce{CO2}\) from \(\ce{NaHCO3}\), and cyclohexanol is not acidic, so \(\ce{NaHCO3}\) reacts with neither.
  • A — \(\ce{Na2Cr2O7}\) oxidises the secondary alcohol cyclohexanol.
  • B — Na reacts with the O–H of both.
  • C — NaOH reacts with the acidic phenol.
Learn the ladder as four rungs, top to bottom. Na metal reacts with every O–H — alcohol, phenol and carboxylic acid — because it only needs an O–H, not an acid stronger than water. \(\ce{NaOH}\) reacts with phenol and carboxylic acid, never with an alcohol. \(\ce{Na2CO3}\) and \(\ce{NaHCO3}\) react with the carboxylic acid only. A reaction only goes if your acid beats the conjugate acid being formed — phenol cannot beat \(\ce{H2CO3}\), so phenol + \(\ce{Na2CO3}\) is no reaction.

(e) Explain why phenols are more acidic than alcohols such as ethanol. [2]
HCI Prelim · N2025 · P3 · Q2(a)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • When phenol loses its \(\ce{H+}\), the resulting phenoxide ion's negative charge on oxygen is delocalised into the benzene ring (the O lone pair overlaps with the ring π system), spreading/stabilising the charge, so the phenoxide ion is more stable and the equilibrium of dissociation lies further to the right 1
  • In ethoxide the negative charge is localised on oxygen and the ethyl group is electron-donating, destabilising it 1
  • Hence phenol is a stronger acid than ethanol.
Every acid-strength answer runs through the conjugate base. Never argue from the acid molecule itself, never from "it has more O–H groups", never from "it is dibasic". The chain is fixed: \(\ce{HA <=> A- + H+}\) → how well is the negative charge on \(\ce{A-}\) dispersed (delocalisation, or an electron-withdrawing group) → the more stable \(\ce{A-}\), the greater the extent of dissociation, the stronger the acid. Route it there every time, without exception.

HCI Prelim · N2023 · P2 · Q3(d)(ii)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • \(\mathrm{p}K_{a1}\) of succinic acid is lower than 4.76 (succinic acid is a stronger acid for its first ionisation) 1
  • The second \(\ce{-CO2H}\) group is electron-withdrawing (–I inductive effect) and the monoanion is further stabilised by the intramolecular hydrogen bond, both of which stabilise/disperse the negative charge of the conjugate base, making the first proton easier to lose.
"It is dibasic so it dissociates twice" earns nothing. Having two acidic protons tells you the acid has two \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) values; it says nothing about whether the first one is lower. 2   What the –OH does to the ringTotal: 7
(a) Explain why phenol undergoes chlorination more readily than methylbenzene. [2]
HCI Soln · N2022 · P3 · Q1(b)(ii)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • In phenol, the lone pair of electrons on the O atom delocalises into the benzene ring, thereby greatly increasing the electron density of the ring 1
  • In methylbenzene, the methyl group is only weakly electron-donating (inductive effect), increasing electron density to a lesser extent 1
  • Hence the ring in phenol is more activated / more electron rich, more susceptible to electrophilic attack, and undergoes chlorination more readily.
"OH is electron-donating" on its own is not enough for the mark. The mark is for naming the mechanism of the donation — the O lone pair delocalising into the ring. And note the split personality of the same group: attached to a benzene ring the \(\ce{-OH}\) donates (activating, 2,4-directing); attached to an sp³ chain next to a \(\ce{-CO2H}\) it withdraws inductively. Decide by what it is attached to, never by memory of the group alone.
(b) Draw the organic product(s) of:  (1) phenol + \(\ce{Cl2(aq)}\);  (2) methylbenzene + \(\ce{Cl2}\) with an \(\ce{AlCl3(s)}\) catalyst. [2]
HCI Soln · N2022 · P3 · Q1(b)(i)  ·  stem reconstructed from the key
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
How activated the ring is decides how many chlorines go on.
  • Phenol + \(\ce{Cl2(aq)}\) → 2,4,6-trichlorophenol (Cl at the 2, 4 and 6 positions relative to \(\ce{-OH}\)) 1
  • Methylbenzene + \(\ce{Cl2}\), \(\ce{AlCl3(s)}\) → 2-chloromethylbenzene (o-) or 4-chloromethylbenzene (p-) 1
  • The \(\ce{-OH}\) group is highly activating and forms a trisubstituted product with \(\ce{Cl2(aq)}\); since \(\ce{-OH}\) is 2,4-directing, Cl substitutes at 2, 4 and 6. The \(\ce{-CH3}\) group is less activating and gives a mono-substituted product at the 2- or 4-positions.
Aqueous halogen versus halogen in an inert solvent — this is the discriminator you keep collapsing. Aqueous \(\ce{Br2}\) or \(\ce{Cl2}\), room temperature, no catalyst is the phenol reagent: electrophilic SUBSTITUTION, the orange colour is decolourised, and you get a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol. \(\ce{Br2}\) dissolved in \(\ce{CCl4}\) (an inert organic solvent), in the dark, is the alkene reagent: electrophilic ADDITION, the reddish-brown colour is decolourised, and there is no precipitate. A plain benzene or methylbenzene ring is not activated enough for either and needs a halogen carrier catalyst (\(\ce{AlCl3}\), \(\ce{FeBr3}\)).
(c) Explain the role of concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) in the formation of the electrophile in the nitration of benzene. [1]
HCI Prelim · N2021 · P2 · Q2(b)(i)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • \(\ce{H2SO4}\) is a stronger Brønsted acid / proton donor than \(\ce{HNO3}\), hence the protonation of \(\ce{HNO3}\) by \(\ce{H2SO4}\) eventually produces the strong electrophile \(\ce{NO2+}\) 1
  • \(\ce{2H2SO4 + HNO3 -> NO2+ + H3O+ + 2HSO4^{-}}\). \(\ce{HSO4^{-}}\) later deprotonates the intermediate and the catalyst \(\ce{H2SO4}\) is regenerated.
The electrophile is \(\ce{NO2+}\), the nitronium ion. It is never \(\ce{HNO3}\) itself and never \(\ce{NO2}\). Nitric acid is behaving as a base here, which is the counter-intuitive bit — sulfuric acid is the stronger acid, so it protonates the nitric acid, and water then leaves to give \(\ce{NO2+}\). Note the phenol exception too: phenol is so activated that it nitrates with dilute \(\ce{HNO3}\) at room temperature with no \(\ce{H2SO4}\) at all; concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) would over-nitrate it to 2,4,6-trinitrophenol.

(a) B = \(\ce{I(CH2)5OH}\) (a 1° alcohol); C = \(\ce{ICH2CH2CH(OH)CH2CH3}\) (a 2° alcohol); D = \(\ce{ICH2CH2C(CH3)2OH}\) (a 3° alcohol). Distinguish them.
I. Heat each separately with acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) and state the observations.
II. Suggest a reagent (other than 2,4-DNPH) to distinguish the products of the positive tests, with observations. [3]
HCI Prelim · N2024 · P3 · Q2(c)(iii)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Step 1 — acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, heat
  • B (primary) — orange dichromate turns green (oxidised to a carboxylic acid) 1
  • C (secondary) — orange turns green (oxidised to a ketone)
  • D (tertiary) — remains orange (no reaction)
Step 2 — separating the two positives
  • The positive products are a carboxylic acid (from B) and a ketone (from C). Reagent = aqueous \(\ce{Na2CO3}\) (or \(\ce{NaHCO3}\)) 1
  • The carboxylic acid from B gives effervescence (colourless \(\ce{CO2}\)); the ketone from C gives no effervescence / no reaction 1
One test cannot finish this question, and that is the point. Dichromate separates the tertiary alcohol from the other two but leaves B and C both green — so a second reagent is compulsory. The carbonate test is the top rung of the acidity ladder: only an acid at least as strong as carbonic acid liberates \(\ce{CO2}\), so a carboxylic acid effervesces and a phenol does not. That single reagent separates carboxylic acid from phenol from alcohol in one move.

Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • (8) primary alcohol → aldehyde: \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), \(\ce{H2SO4(aq)}\), heat with immediate distillation 1
  • (9) aldehyde → carboxylic acid: \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), \(\ce{H2SO4(aq)}\), heat under reflux or \(\ce{KMnO4}\), \(\ce{H2SO4(aq)}\), heat under reflux 1

Same reagent three times — the condition is the entire answer. Write "acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), heat" with no distillation-or-reflux word and you have answered none of the three. And the ladder itself: 1° → aldehyde → carboxylic acid, 2° → ketone and stops, 3° → no reaction, stays orange, because a tertiary carbon has no H on the carbon bearing the \(\ce{-OH}\).

4   Tri-iodomethane: the test, the equation, the synthesisTotal: 9
(a) Write a balanced equation, with state symbols, for the reaction of ethanal, \(\ce{CH3CHO}\), with alkaline aqueous iodine in the tri-iodomethane (iodoform) test. [2]
HCI Soln · N2017 · P2 · Q3(d)  ·  stem reconstructed from the key
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • Correct species on both sides: \(\ce{CH3CHO + I2 + OH^{-} -> HCO2^{-} + CHI3 + I^{-} + H2O}\) 1
  • Correctly balanced with state symbols: \(\ce{CH3CHO(l) + 3I2(aq) + 4OH^{-}(aq) -> HCO2^{-}(aq) + CHI3(s) + 3I^{-}(aq) + 3H2O(l)}\) 1

Teaching note from the key: the product is a carboxylate ion (alkaline medium) with one carbon less than the original reactant. A common mistake is to write tri-iodomethane as \(\ce{CH3I}\); state symbols are required.

Balance it in this order and it falls out every time. \(\ce{CH3}\) → \(\ce{CHI3}\) needs 3 I atoms in and 3 H out, so 3 \(\ce{I2}\) and 3 \(\ce{OH-}\) to mop up those H as water. Then one more \(\ce{OH-}\) cleaves the C–C bond and takes the remaining fragment, giving 4 \(\ce{OH-}\) in total, \(\ce{HCO2-}\) and 3 \(\ce{H2O}\). Iodine balance: 6 I go in, 3 end up in \(\ce{CHI3}\) and 3 leave as \(\ce{I-}\). The yellow precipitate \(\ce{CHI3}\) is (s), and because the medium is alkaline the other product is the carboxylate ion, not the acid.

The partly-iodinated decoys are the trap, and the ester is the answer. The real discriminator is what the carbonyl belongs to: it must be a ketone or aldehyde. In \(\ce{CI3CO2CH3}\) the C=O is part of an ester linkage, so nothing happens.

(b) Explain why phenol is a weak nucleophile in its reaction (esterification) with a carboxylic acid. [2]

  • Phenol is a weak nucleophile as the lone pair of electrons on oxygen is delocalised into the benzene ring 1
  • and is therefore less available to attack the electron deficient carbon in the carboxylic acid 1
Same fact as Q2(a), pointing the opposite way. The O lone pair delocalising into the ring is what makes phenol's ring unusually reactive towards electrophiles — and, in the same breath, what makes phenol's oxygen a poor nucleophile, because that lone pair is spread out rather than sitting available on the O. One structural fact, two consequences. Say which one the question is asking about before you write.
(c) State the reagents and conditions for the two steps to prepare phenyl ethanoate from phenol. [2]
HCI Soln · N2019 · P2 · Q3(a)(ii)  ·  also HCI Prelim · N2024 · P3 · Q2(c)(iv)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Deprotonate first, acylate second.
  • Step 1: \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\) (or \(\ce{Na(s)}\)) — converts phenol to sodium phenoxide, \(\ce{C6H5O^{-}Na+}\) 1
  • Step 2: ethanoyl chloride, \(\ce{CH3COCl}\) (or ethanoic anhydride, \(\ce{(CH3CO)2O}\)) — acylates the phenoxide to give phenyl ethanoate 1
  • To form a phenyl ester, react phenoxide with an acid chloride to ensure a good yield. Phenoxide is negatively charged and hence a stronger nucleophile than phenol. The acyl carbon in an acid chloride is highly electron deficient as it is attached to two electronegative atoms (O and Cl), so it is more susceptible to nucleophilic attack.
Two routes to an ester, and which one you get is decided by the alcohol half, not the acid half. An alcohol will esterify with a carboxylic acid, conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) catalyst, heat — slow and reversible, but it works. A phenol will not, because its O lone pair is delocalised into the ring, so it needs the acyl chloride at room temperature, no catalyst, with steamy white fumes of HCl. Deprotonating to the phenoxide first makes it better still, because the full negative charge on O beats a delocalised lone pair as a nucleophile.

Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • J is neutral, yet reacts with sodium, hence it must be an alcohol 1
  • J does not react with 2,4-DNPH, so it does not contain an aldehyde or ketone 1
  • J does not react with bromine water, hence it does not contain a C=C double bond 1

"Neutral, yet reacts with sodium" is the whole deduction, and both halves are load-bearing. Sodium fizzes with any O–H — alcohol, phenol or carboxylic acid — so on its own it proves nothing beyond "there is an O–H". The word neutral is what kills the acidic two and leaves the alcohol standing. Never write "reacts with Na so it has an \(\ce{-OH}\)" and stop: say which class, and say what ruled the other two out. Note also that bromine water is doing double duty in this question — a negative result rules out both a C=C and a phenol ring.

Two different starting classes, two different elimination reagents, one product. A halogenoalkane loses HX and needs a base in a non-aqueous solvent — ethanolic NaOH. HCI Prelim · N2024 · P2 · Q4(d)(i)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • Step 1: \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether 1 — reduction of one \(\ce{-CO2H}\) to a primary \(\ce{-CH2OH}\).
  • Step 2: concentrated \(\ce{H3PO4}\) (or concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\)), heat 1 — dehydration / elimination of the alcohol to the alkene.
  • Step 3: methanol (\(\ce{CH3OH}\)) with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) catalyst, heat 1 — esterification.
Read each step off the molecular formula change, then name the reagent. Step 1 swaps a \(\ce{-CO2H}\) for a \(\ce{-CH2OH}\), which is a reduction, and \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) is the only A-level reagent that reduces a carboxylic acid — Reject H₂/Ni, since carboxylic acids, esters and amides are comparatively unreactive with \(\ce{H2}\) gas. Step 2 loses \(\ce{H2O}\) and gains a C=C, so it is dehydration. Step 3 gains a \(\ce{CH3}\) on an oxygen, so it is esterification with methanol. Notice the acid group survives step 1 in one copy and only reacts in step 3 — \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) reducing one of two identical groups is the deliberately awkward part of this scheme.
