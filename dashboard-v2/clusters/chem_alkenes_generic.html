Chemistry — Alkenes

H2 9729 · Topic 11 · Organic · one cluster for the chapter. Grounded in the HCI lecture notes. Highest-yield organic mechanism topic — electrophilic addition is examined almost every year.

Format — teaching cluster: each card is a worked Example (attempt cold, reveal to check the mechanism / product / mark-scheme wording), then a harder Practice that adds parts (c)/(d). The three things examiners reward on every reaction: (1) all reagents AND conditions stated, (2) the two-step mechanism with correctly-placed curly arrows and the rate-determining step labelled, (3) the "most stable carbocation → major product" reasoning written out, not asserted.
The Alkenes toolkit — reagents, conditions, and what each reaction is for
Why reactiveElectron-rich \(\ce{C=C}\); the exposed π-electron cloud attracts electrophiles. The weak π bond breaks, two σ bonds form → electrophilic addition.
+ HX (\(\ce{HCl},\ \ce{HBr},\ \ce{HI}\))HX(g), room temp. → halogenoalkane. Markovnikov.
+ X\(_2\) (\(\ce{Cl2},\ \ce{Br2}\))\(\ce{Cl2}\) or \(\ce{Br2}\) in \(\ce{CCl4}\), room temp, in the dark. → 1,2-dihalide. Br\(_2\) decolourised (test).
+ Bromine water\(\ce{Br2(aq)}\), room temp. → bromohydrin (–OH and –Br adjacent), NOT the dibromide. Yellow-orange decolourised (test).
+ Steam (hydration)\(\ce{H2O(g)}\), \(\ce{H3PO4}\) catalyst, high T & p. → alcohol. Markovnikov.
Reduction\(\ce{H2}\), Ni catalyst (heterogeneous). → alkane. (Margarine from plant oils.)
Mild oxidation\(\ce{KMnO4}\), dilute NaOH (alkaline), cold. → diol. Purple decolourised + brown \(\ce{MnO2}\) ppt.
Oxidative cleavage\(\ce{KMnO4}\), dilute \(\ce{H2SO4}\) (acidified), heat. → cleave \(\ce{C=C}\). Not oxidised by \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\).

Question 1

Example
Propene reacts with hydrogen bromide.
(a) Draw the mechanism, showing curly arrows and the intermediate, and label the rate-determining step. [4]
(b) State the major product and explain, in terms of carbocation stability, why it is the major product. [3]
Solution with mark scheme
Step 1 — (a) Name it, then the slow step
This is electrophilic addition. \(\ce{H-Br}\) is polar (\(\ce{H}\) is \(\delta+\)). M1 mechanism type + first arrows
Step 2 — (a) Fast step
The nucleophile \(\ce{Br-}\) attacks the carbocation. Fast — attraction between oppositely-charged species. Curly arrow from the \(\ce{Br-}\) lone pair to the \(\ce{C+}\). M1 arrow + carbocation shown, A1 correct product
\[ \ce{CH3-\overset{+}{C}H-CH3 + Br^- -> CH3-CHBr-CH3} \]
Step 3 — (b) Major product + reasoning
Markovnikov the marks-earning way. B1 product, M1 carbocation comparison, A1 conclusion
Major product: 2-bromopropane \(\ce{(CH3CHBrCH3)}\). \(\ce{H+}\) adds to the terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) carbon so the \(+\) charge sits on the middle (2°) carbon. The 2° carbocation is more stable than the 1° because the two adjacent alkyl groups are electron-donating and disperse the positive charge. The more stable carbocation forms faster → gives the major product.
Trap: don't just write "H adds to the C with more H's." The mark scheme wants the carbocation-stability chain: electrophile adds to give the most stable (most substituted) carbocation → major product. And label the slow step as rate-determining.

Practice
But-1-ene \(\ce{(CH3CH2CH=CH2)}\) reacts with hydrogen bromide.
(a) State the major product and explain via carbocation stability. [3]
(b) Draw the mechanism for its formation, labelling the rate-determining step. [3]
(c) Explain why the product is formed as a racemic mixture. [2]
(d) But-2-ene also reacts with HBr. Is Markovnikov's rule needed to predict its product? Explain. [2]
Answer with mark scheme
(a) Major product
B1 A1 A1
2-bromobutane \(\ce{(CH3CH2CHBrCH3)}\). \(\ce{H+}\) adds to the terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) → the \(+\) sits on C2, a 2° carbocation, more stable than the 1° at C1 because the adjacent alkyl groups donate electrons and disperse the charge. More stable cation → major product.
(b) Mechanism
M1 slow-step arrows (π→H, H–Br→Br) + 2° carbocation, M1 rds labelled, A1 fast-step arrow Br⁻→C⁺
Same two-step shape as the Example: π electrons attack \(\delta+\) \(\ce{H}\) of \(\ce{H-Br}\) (slow, rds) → \(\ce{CH3CH2\overset{+}{C}HCH3 + Br^-}\); then \(\ce{Br^-}\) attacks the \(\ce{C+}\) (fast) → 2-bromobutane.
(c) Why racemic
M1 chiral centre, A1 both faces
In 2-bromobutane, C2 is bonded to four different groups (\(\ce{Br},\ \ce{H},\ \ce{CH3},\ \ce{C2H5}\)) → a chiral centre. The carbocation is planar (sp², trigonal), so \(\ce{Br^-}\) attacks with equal probability from either face → an equimolar pair of enantiomers → racemic mixture.
(d) But-2-ene
M1 A1
No. But-2-ene \(\ce{(CH3CH=CHCH3)}\) is symmetrical about the \(\ce{C=C}\) — adding \(\ce{H}\) to either carbon gives the same carbocation and the same product, 2-bromobutane. Markovnikov only decides between two different possible carbocations, so it isn't needed here. (It's still formed as a racemic mixture.)
Why harder: (c) tests the planar-cation → racemic logic, and (d) tests that Markovnikov only matters for unsymmetrical alkenes — a symmetrical one needs no rule.

Archetype revealed
Electrophilic addition of HX + Markovnikov via carbocation stability.

Examined almost every year. The two-step mechanism (slow rds forming the carbocation, fast nucleophile attack) plus the "most stable carbocation → major product" reasoning. Racemic mixture whenever the new centre is chiral.

Question 2

Example
Ethene is bubbled into a solution of bromine in tetrachloromethane (\(\ce{CCl4}\)) at room temperature in the dark.
(a) State the reagent, conditions, and the observation. [2]
(b) Draw the mechanism with curly arrows, name it, and state the two bonds broken in the rate-determining step. [4]
Solution with mark scheme
(a) Reagent / conditions / observation
B1 B1
\(\ce{Br2}\) dissolved in \(\ce{CCl4}\), room temperature, in the dark. Observation: reddish-brown \(\ce{Br2}\) is decolourised.
(b) Mechanism — electrophilic addition
\(\ce{Br2}\) is non-polar, so the π cloud must polarise it first. M1 induced dipole + first arrows, M1 rds, A1 carbocation, A1 product
Name + bonds broken
A1
Mechanism: electrophilic addition. The π cloud induces a dipole in \(\ce{Br2}\) (polarises it); in the rds the π bond and the \(\ce{Br-Br}\) bond both break. Product: 1,2-dibromoethane \(\ce{(BrCH2CH2Br)}\).
Trap: \(\ce{Br2}\) is non-polar — you must say the π cloud induces/polarises the dipole before the arrows. And it's in the dark: UV would trigger free-radical substitution of the alkyl H's instead.

Practice
Cyclohexene reacts with bromine in \(\ce{CCl4}\).
(a) Draw the structure of the organic product and state the observation. [2]
(b) Draw the mechanism, labelling the rate-determining step. [3]
(c) Explain why the reaction must be carried out in the absence of UV light. [2]
(d) In practice only the trans 1,2-dibromo product forms, not a cis/trans mixture. What does this tell you about the intermediate? [2]
Answer with mark scheme
(a) Product + observation
A1 A1
trans-1,2-dibromocyclohexane — a cyclohexane ring with \(\ce{Br}\) on two adjacent carbons. Observation: reddish-brown \(\ce{Br2}\) decolourised.
(b) Mechanism
M1 induced dipole + π/Br–Br arrows (rds), M1 rds labelled, A1 Br⁻ attacks carbocation
Same shape as the Example on the ring \(\ce{C=C}\): π cloud polarises \(\ce{Br2}\); π bond + \(\ce{Br-Br}\) break (rds) → cyclic carbocation + \(\ce{Br^-}\); \(\ce{Br^-}\) attacks (fast).
(c) Why no UV
M1 A1
UV light would homolytically break \(\ce{Br2}\) into \(\ce{Br}\) radicals, triggering free-radical substitution of the alkyl \(\ce{C-H}\) bonds — a competing reaction giving a mixture of substitution products. The dark keeps it to clean electrophilic addition.
(d) What trans-only implies
M1 A1
A single stereochemistry (trans) means the two \(\ce{Br}\) added to opposite faces. This implies the intermediate is a cyclic bromonium ion (not an open planar carbocation), which blocks one face, so \(\ce{Br^-}\) must attack from the opposite side → trans only.
Why harder: (c) forces the addition-vs-substitution distinction (why "in the dark" is a condition, not decoration), and (d) reaches to the bromonium-ion refinement that explains the stereochemistry.

Archetype revealed
Halogen addition in \(\ce{CCl4}\) — the LO-named electrophilic addition mechanism.

The one where the electrophile is non-polar and the π cloud must induce the dipole first. Conditions (dark) and the two bonds broken in the rds are the recurring mark-droppers.

Question 3

Example
Ethene is shaken with bromine water, \(\ce{Br2(aq)}\).
(a) State the observation and what it tests for. [2]
(b) Give the structure of the major organic product and explain why it, rather than 1,2-dibromoethane, is the major product. [3]
Solution with mark scheme
(a) Observation
A1 A1
Yellow-orange \(\ce{Br2(aq)}\) is decolourised. This is a distinguishing test for a \(\ce{C=C}\) double bond (unsaturation).
(b) Major product + why
After the carbocation forms, ask which nucleophiles are present. B1 structure, M1 nucleophiles present, A1 water in excess
Major product: 2-bromoethanol (a bromohydrin), \(\ce{HOCH2CH2Br}\) — –OH and –Br on adjacent carbons. Once the carbocation forms, both \(\ce{H2O}\) and \(\ce{Br^-}\) can attack it, but water is present in large excess, so it attacks preferentially → the bromoalcohol dominates over the dibromide.
Trap: bromine water does not give the dibromide as the major product. The step-1 electrophile is still \(\ce{Br2}\); it's step 2 where the abundant water wins.

Practice
Propene is shaken with bromine water that has been contaminated with sodium chloride solution, \(\ce{NaCl(aq)}\).
(a) Draw the structures of the three organic products formed. [3]
(b) State which carbon of propene the \(\ce{Br}\) ends up on, and why. [2]
(c) A student claims 1,2-dichloropropane is also formed. Explain why it cannot be. [2]
(d) State what the colour change of the bromine water tells you. [1]
Answer with mark scheme
(a) Three products
The carbocation can be attacked by \(\ce{H2O}\), \(\ce{Br^-}\), or \(\ce{Cl^-}\) — all present. A1 A1 A1
1-bromopropan-2-ol \(\ce{(CH3CH(OH)CH2Br)}\); 1,2-dibromopropane \(\ce{(CH3CHBrCH2Br)}\); 1-bromo-2-chloropropane \(\ce{(CH3CHClCH2Br)}\).
(b) Where the Br goes
M1 A1
The \(\ce{Br}\) (as the electrophilic \(\ce{Br^{\delta+}}\)) adds to the terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) carbon, so the \(+\) sits on the middle (2°) carbon — the more stable carbocation. The nucleophile then joins the middle carbon, so every product carries \(\ce{Br}\) on C1.
(c) Why not 1,2-dichloropropane
M1 A1
The only electrophile present is \(\ce{Br2}\) — there is no \(\ce{Cl2}\) / \(\ce{Cl^+}\) to start the addition. \(\ce{Cl^-}\) is only a nucleophile (step 2). So every product must contain at least one \(\ce{Br}\); a product with only \(\ce{Cl}\) is impossible.
(d) Colour change
A1
Decolourisation of the yellow-orange \(\ce{Br2(aq)}\) shows the compound is unsaturated (contains a \(\ce{C=C}\)).
Why harder: (a)/(c) test that the electrophile sets the starting halogen (must be Br) while any nucleophile present can finish it — the classic "which ions attack the cation" trap.

Archetype revealed
Bromine water → bromohydrin, and the "which nucleophile attacks the cation" trap.

Electrophile (Br₂) fixes the first halogen; the abundant nucleophile (water, or contaminating ions) finishes the job. Decolourising \(\ce{Br2(aq)}\) is the standard test for \(\ce{C=C}\).

Question 4

Example
2-methylbut-2-ene, \(\ce{(CH3)2C=CHCH3}\), is treated with potassium manganate(VII).
(a) Give the reagents, conditions, observations, and organic product for cold dilute alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\). [3]
(b) Give the reagents, conditions and organic products for hot acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\). [3]
Solution with mark scheme
(a) Mild oxidation → diol
B1 conditions, B1 observation, A1 product
\(\ce{KMnO4}\), dilute \(\ce{NaOH}\) (alkaline), cold. Observation: purple \(\ce{KMnO4}\) decolourised and a brown precipitate of \(\ce{MnO2}\) forms. Product: the diol — \(\ce{-OH}\) added to each former \(\ce{C=C}\) carbon → 2-methylbutane-2,3-diol, \(\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)-CH(OH)CH3}\).
(b) Oxidative cleavage — apply the H-count table
Cut at the \(\ce{C=C}\); each half becomes a product set by how many H it carried. B1 conditions, A1 A1 products
\(\ce{KMnO4}\), dilute \(\ce{H2SO4}\) (acidified), heat. Left carbon \(\ce{(CH3)2C=}\) has 0 H → ketone: propanone \(\ce{(CH3)2C=O}\). Right carbon \(\ce{=CHCH3}\) has 1 H → carboxylic acid: ethanoic acid \(\ce{CH3COOH}\).
Trap: the only thing separating "diol" from "cleavage" is the conditions — cold/alkaline vs hot/acidified. Drop that half of the condition and you get the wrong product. Alkenes are not oxidised by \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\).

Practice
An alkene P, \(\ce{C6H12}\), is warmed with acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\). The only organic products are propanone and propanoic acid.
(a) Deduce the structure and name of P. [3]
(b) A different alkene Q gives, on the same treatment, a single carbonyl compound plus effervescence of a gas that turns limewater milky. What structural feature must Q have, and what is the gas? [3]
(c) Write the balanced equation for the mild oxidation of ethene using [O]. [2]
(d) Why would \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}/\ce{H2SO4}\) be the wrong reagent to test P for a \(\ce{C=C}\)? [1]
Answer with mark scheme
(a) Work backwards from the fragments
Propanone \(\ce{(CH3)2C=O}\) came from a \(\ce{=C(CH3)2}\) (0 H) end; propanoic acid \(\ce{CH3CH2COOH}\) came from a \(\ce{=CHCH2CH3}\) (1 H) end. Re-join the two carbons with a \(\ce{C=C}\). M1 both fragments mapped, A1 structure, A1 name
P = \(\ce{(CH3)2C=CHCH2CH3}\) = 2-methylpent-2-ene. (Check: \(\ce{C6H12}\) ✓.)
(b) Q — the terminal-C=C clue
M1 terminal =CH₂, A1 CO₂, A1 gas test
Q has a terminal \(\ce{C=C}\) (a \(\ce{=CH2}\) end). A carbon with 2 H is oxidised to \(\ce{CO2}\), which gives the effervescence and turns limewater milky. The other end (with fewer H) gives the single carbonyl.
(c) Balanced [O] equation (mild oxidation)
M1 [O] + H₂O, A1 balanced
\[ \ce{CH2=CH2 + H2O + [O] -> HOCH2CH2OH} \]
(d) Wrong reagent
A1
Alkenes are not oxidised by \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) — only by manganate(VII) — so there would be no colour change and the test would fail.
Why harder: (a) is reverse structure-determination (join the cleavage fragments back across a \(\ce{C=C}\)); (b) uses the terminal-\(\ce{=CH2}\) → \(\ce{CO2}\) effervescence as a diagnostic. This is exactly how oxidative cleavage is examined.

Archetype revealed
Oxidation: mild (diol) vs oxidative cleavage, and locating the \(\ce{C=C}\).

Conditions flip the product (cold/alkaline → diol; hot/acidified → cleavage). Cleavage is the tool for structure determination: map each fragment back to its H-count (2H→CO₂, 1H→acid, 0H→ketone).

Question 5

Example
Consider cis- and trans-1,2-dichloroethene, \(\ce{ClCH=CHCl}\).
(a) State which isomer has the higher boiling point, and explain fully in terms of intermolecular forces. [3]
(b) State which isomer has the higher melting point, and explain. [2]
Solution with mark scheme
(a) Higher boiling point — cis
M1 net dipole in cis, M1 dipoles cancel in trans, A1 conclusion
cis has the higher bp. In the cis isomer the two polar \(\ce{C-Cl}\) bond dipoles are on the same side and do not cancel → a net permanent dipole → permanent dipole–permanent dipole (pd–pd) attractions on top of dispersion forces → more energy to separate. In trans the dipoles are opposite and cancel → no net dipole → non-polar → dispersion only → lower bp.
(b) Higher melting point — trans
M1 packing, A1 conclusion
trans has the higher mp. The trans isomer is more symmetrical / straighter, so it packs more regularly and closely into the solid lattice → dispersion forces act more effectively → more energy to melt. cis packs poorly.
Trap: the two answers point opposite ways — cis = higher bp (polarity), trans = higher mp (packing). Don't swap them.

Practice
(a) But-2-ene exists as cis and trans isomers. State which has the higher boiling point, with reasoning. [2]
(b) The bp of cis- and trans-but-2-ene differ by only about 3 °C, far less than for the dichloroethenes. Explain. [2]
(c) State the two conditions a molecule must meet to show cis-trans isomerism. [2]
(d) Does 2-methylbut-2-ene, \(\ce{(CH3)2C=CHCH3}\), show cis-trans isomerism? Explain. [2]
Answer with mark scheme
(a) Higher bp
M1 A1
cis-but-2-ene has the higher bp: the two \(\ce{CH3}\) groups on the same side give a small net dipole → weak pd–pd on top of dispersion; trans is symmetrical, dipoles cancel → dispersion only.
(b) Why the difference is tiny
M1 A1
The \(\ce{CH3}\) group is only very weakly polar (the \(\ce{C-C}\)/\(\ce{C-H}\) bonds are nearly non-polar), so even the cis isomer has only a very small net dipole → the pd–pd contribution is tiny → the two isomers differ little. With \(\ce{C-Cl}\) bonds (strongly polar) the difference is large.
(c) Two conditions
A1 A1
(1) Restricted rotation about the \(\ce{C=C}\); (2) each \(\ce{C=C}\) carbon carries two different groups.
(d) 2-methylbut-2-ene?
M1 A1
No. One \(\ce{C=C}\) carbon carries two identical \(\ce{CH3}\) groups, so the "two different groups on each carbon" criterion fails → no cis-trans isomerism.
Why harder: (b) tests why the polarity effect is small when substituents are barely polar; (d) tests the "two different groups on EACH carbon" criterion on a molecule that fails it.

Archetype revealed
Cis-trans isomerism + the bp/mp reasoning.

cis = higher bp (net dipole, pd–pd); trans = higher mp (symmetry, packing). The size of the bp gap tracks how polar the substituents are. Cis-trans needs restricted rotation + two different groups on each \(\ce{C=C}\) carbon.

Question 6

Example
(a) State the reagents and conditions to convert ethene into ethane, and name the type of catalysis. [2]
(b) Describe a simple chemical test, with reagent and observation, to show that a colourless liquid is an alkene rather than an alkane. [2]
Solution with mark scheme
(a) Reduction
B1 reagents/conditions, B1 catalysis type
\(\ce{H2}\) gas, Ni catalyst (heat; Pt or Pd also work). \(\ce{C=C -> C-C}\), adding one \(\ce{H}\) to each carbon → ethane. This is heterogeneous catalysis (catalyst and reactants in different phases).
(b) Distinguishing test
B1 reagent, B1 observation for the alkene (alkane = no change)
Add \(\ce{Br2(aq)}\) dropwise: the alkene decolourises the yellow-orange bromine water; the alkane gives no change. (Or cold \(\ce{KMnO4}\): alkene decolourises purple + brown \(\ce{MnO2}\); alkane no change.)
Trap: give the observation for both outcomes (change for the alkene, no change for the alkane) — a test answer needs the contrast.

Practice
(a) Describe how you would use oxidative cleavage to distinguish but-1-ene from but-2-ene, giving the key observation. [3]
(b) Explain why, in the bromine-water and \(\ce{KMnO4}\) tests, the reagent must be added dropwise. [2]
(c) State one large-scale industrial use of catalytic hydrogenation of alkenes. [1]
(d) Write the equation for the complete hydrogenation of buta-1,3-diene, \(\ce{CH2=CHCH=CH2}\). [2]
Answer with mark scheme
(a) Distinguish but-1-ene / but-2-ene
Terminal vs internal \(\ce{C=C}\). M1 method, M1 but-1-ene result, A1 but-2-ene result
Warm each with acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\). But-1-ene has a terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) (2 H) → gives \(\ce{CO2}\) — effervescence / gas that turns limewater milky. But-2-ene \(\ce{(CH3CH=CHCH3)}\) has no \(\ce{=CH2}\) → no gas (gives two carboxylic-acid/… fragments, here ethanoic acid). The effervescence distinguishes them.
(b) Why dropwise
M1 A1
If the reagent is added in excess, the colour change (decolourisation) cannot be seen — the solution stays purple / yellow-orange. Adding dropwise lets the alkene consume each drop so the decolourisation is observable.
(c) Industrial use
A1
Hardening unsaturated vegetable/plant oils into margarine (fats).
(d) Equation
Two \(\ce{C=C}\) → add \(\ce{2 H2}\). M1 2H₂, A1 butane
\[ \ce{CH2=CHCH=CH2 + 2H2 ->[\text{Ni}] CH3CH2CH2CH3} \]
Why harder: (a) uses the terminal-\(\ce{=CH2}\) → \(\ce{CO2}\) diagnostic to tell two similar alkenes apart; (d) checks you count one \(\ce{H2}\) per \(\ce{C=C}\) (a diene needs 2).

Hydrogenation (H₂/Ni, heterogeneous); the decolourisation tests and why they're added dropwise; and the terminal-\(\ce{C=C}\) → \(\ce{CO2}\) trick for telling alkenes apart.
