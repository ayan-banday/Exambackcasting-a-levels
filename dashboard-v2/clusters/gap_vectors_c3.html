Question 1 [4]
HCI Tutorial 7C Q2
Find the vector equation of each of the following planes in scalar product form and in Cartesian form. (a) \( \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}+\mu\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\-1\end{pmatrix} \), where \( \lambda,\mu\in\mathbb R \). [2] (b) \( \mathbf r=(1+s-t)\mathbf i+(2-s)\mathbf j+(3-2s+2t)\mathbf k \), where \( s,t\in\mathbb R \). [2]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(a) the two direction vectors are already there — cross them
In \( \mathbf r=\mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf d_1+\mu\mathbf d_2 \), the normal is \( \mathbf n=\mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2 \). \[ \mathbf n=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix} \](1) \[ \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}=2\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y-2z=2 \](1)
(b) split the expression into constant + \(s\)-part + \(t\)-part first
Group the components by which parameter they carry — that recovers \( \mathbf a \), \( \mathbf d_1 \), \( \mathbf d_2 \). \[ \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}+s\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\-2\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\\2\end{pmatrix} \](1) \[ \mathbf n=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\-2\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\\2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} \] \[ \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}=5\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad 2x+z=5 \](1)
The three forms, once and for all.
Parametric: \( \mathbf r=\mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf d_1+\mu\mathbf d_2 \) — a point plus two directions lying in the plane. Two parameters.
Scalar product: \( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=d \) — a normal and a constant. No parameters.
Cartesian: \( n_1x+n_2y+n_3z=d \) — literally the scalar product form written out.
Route between them: parametric \( \xrightarrow{\ \mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2\ } \) scalar product \( \xrightarrow{\ \text{expand}\ } \) Cartesian. Always go via scalar product — never try to jump straight to Cartesian.
Tests: converting between the three forms of a plane equation, including recovering the directions from a plane written as one expression in \(s\) and \(t\).

Question 2 [4]
A-Level N2014 P1 Q9(i)
Planes \(p\) and \(q\) are perpendicular. Plane \(p\) has equation \( x+2y-3z=12 \). Plane \(q\) contains the line \(l\) with equation \( \dfrac{x-1}{2}=\dfrac{y+1}{-1}=\dfrac{z-3}{4} \). The point \(A\) on \(l\) has coordinates \( (1,-1,3) \). Find a Cartesian equation of \(q\). [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
read off what you have, and decide what role each object plays
\(p\) has normal \( \mathbf n_p=(1,2,-3) \). The line \(l\) has direction \( \mathbf d=(2,-1,4) \) and passes through \( A(1,-1,3) \). \(q\) contains \(l\) \( \Rightarrow \) \( \mathbf d \) is a direction in \(q\).   \(q\perp p\) \( \Rightarrow \) \( \mathbf n_p \) is also a direction in \(q\) — not \(q\)'s normal.(1)
cross the two in-plane directions to get \(q\)'s normal
\[ \mathbf n_q=\mathbf n_p\times\mathbf d=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\-3\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}5\\-10\\-5\end{pmatrix}=5\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\-1\end{pmatrix} \](1)(1)
use \( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n \) with the point on \(l\)
\[ \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\-1\end{pmatrix}=1+2-3=0 \] \[ \therefore\ x-2y-z=0 \](1)
This is your gap, in a real A-Level stem. Two planes are perpendicular \( \Longleftrightarrow \) their normals are perpendicular \( \Longleftrightarrow \) each plane's normal lies inside the other plane. So \( \mathbf n_p \) is a direction vector of \(q\), and you must still cross it with something to get \( \mathbf n_q \). Compare with: plane perpendicular to a LINE \(AB\) \( \Rightarrow \) \( \overrightarrow{AB} \) IS the normal, no cross product needed.
Tests: plane containing a given line and perpendicular to a given plane — recognising that the other plane's normal is an in-plane direction, not the normal.

Question 3 [3]
NJC 2009 Prelim P1 Q10(i) · HCI Supplementary Q8
The points \(A\) and \(B\) have position vectors \( -\mathbf i+2\mathbf j-2\mathbf k \) and \( 2\mathbf i-\mathbf j+7\mathbf k \) respectively. The plane \( \pi_1 \) is given by \( \mathbf r\cdot(\mathbf i-\mathbf j)=5 \). The plane \( \pi_2 \) is perpendicular to \( \pi_1 \) and contains the points \(A\) and \(B\). Find the equation of \( \pi_2 \) in the form \( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=d \). [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
find the two directions that lie in \( \pi_2 \)
\( \pi_2 \) contains \(A\) and \(B\), so \( \overrightarrow{AB} \) is one in-plane direction. \( \pi_2\perp\pi_1 \), so \( \mathbf n_1=(1,-1,0) \) is the other in-plane direction. \[ \overrightarrow{AB}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\7\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\-2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\-3\\9\end{pmatrix}=3\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix} \](1)
cross them, simplify, then substitute a known point
\[ \mathbf n_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-3\\-3\\0\end{pmatrix}=-3\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix} \](1) \[ \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\-2\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}=-1+2=1 \] \[ \therefore\ \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}=1 \](1)
Same recognition, third costume. Q2 gave you a line; this gives you two points. Either way the perpendicular plane's normal joins the party as a direction. Notice both simplifications: \( (3,-3,9)\to(1,-1,3) \) and \( (-3,-3,0)\to(1,1,0) \) — factor out before you substitute.
Tests: plane through two points and perpendicular to a given plane — the same "normal becomes a direction" recognition, reached from different given data.

Question 4 [4]
A-Level 2020 P1 Q1
A plane \( \pi_1 \) contains the two vectors \( \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix} \) and \( \begin{pmatrix}1\\-5\\-2\end{pmatrix} \). (i) Find a vector normal to \( \pi_1 \). [2] A plane \( \pi_2 \) has equation \( 4x+5y-6z=0 \). (ii) Find the acute angle between \( \pi_1 \) and \( \pi_2 \). [2]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) "contains the two vectors" means they are in-plane directions
\[ \mathbf n_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}1\\-5\\-2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\2\\-6\end{pmatrix}=-2\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix} \](1) \[ \mathbf n_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{(or any scalar multiple)} \](1)
(ii) angle between planes = angle between normals, with a modulus
Read \( \mathbf n_2=(4,5,-6) \) straight off the Cartesian equation. \( |\mathbf n_1|=\sqrt{11} \), \( |\mathbf n_2|=\sqrt{77} \). \[ \cos\theta=\frac{\left|(1,-1,3)\cdot(4,5,-6)\right|}{\sqrt{11}\,\sqrt{77}}=\frac{|4-5-18|}{\sqrt{847}}=\frac{19}{\sqrt{847}} \](1) \[ \theta=49.2^\circ\ \text{(1 d.p.)} \](1)
Two small things that cost marks. The Cartesian form hands you the normal for free — the coefficients of \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) are \( \mathbf n \). And the angle between planes carries a modulus (acute angle) and uses \( \cos \) — only line-and-plane uses \( \sin \).
Tests: normal from two in-plane vectors; reading a normal off a Cartesian equation; angle between two planes.

Question 5 [4]
NYJC 2009 Prelim P2 Q4 (modified) · HCI Supplementary Q18
The equations of three planes \( p_1 \), \( p_2 \), \( p_3 \) are \( 5x+3y-z=2 \), \( \;x-2y+3z=3 \), \( \;\mu x-y+2z=\rho \) respectively, where \( \mu \) and \( \rho \) are constants. The planes \( p_1 \) and \( p_2 \) intersect in a line \(l\). (i) Find a vector equation of \(l\). [2] (ii) Given that the planes \( p_2 \) and \( p_3 \) intersect at right angles, find \( \mu \). [2]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) direction by cross product, point by inspection
\( \mathbf n_1=(5,3,-1) \), \( \mathbf n_2=(1,-2,3) \). The line lies in both planes, so it is perpendicular to both normals. \[ \mathbf d=\mathbf n_1\times\mathbf n_2=\begin{pmatrix}5\\3\\-1\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}7\\-16\\-13\end{pmatrix} \](1) A point on both: try \( (1,-1,0) \) — \( 5-3-0=2 \) ✓ and \( 1+2+0=3 \) ✓. \[ l:\ \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}7\\-16\\-13\end{pmatrix},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb R \](1)
(ii) planes at right angles \( \Leftrightarrow \) normals at right angles
\( \mathbf n_2=(1,-2,3) \), \( \mathbf n_3=(\mu,-1,2) \). Perpendicular planes means \( \mathbf n_2\cdot\mathbf n_3=0 \). \[ (1)(\mu)+(-2)(-1)+(3)(2)=0\ \Rightarrow\ \mu+2+6=0 \](1) \[ \therefore\ \mu=-8 \](1)
The same fact, used backwards. Q2 and Q3 used "\( p\perp q \)" to supply a direction. Here it is used as an equation: \( \mathbf n_2\cdot\mathbf n_3=0 \). One idea, two jobs — recognise which the question wants.
And part (i): fixing a coordinate to find a point on \(l\) is legitimate because the line is infinite — any consistent choice lands on the same line; only the "starting point" changes, and that was never unique.
Tests: line of intersection of two planes; and the perpendicular-planes condition used in reverse to solve for an unknown coefficient.

Question 6 [5]
HCI Tutorial 7C Q10
The equations of two planes \( \Pi_1 \) and \( \Pi_2 \) are given by \( \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{pmatrix}=1 \) and \( \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}3\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\2\end{pmatrix}+\mu\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} \) respectively, where \( \lambda \) and \( \mu \) are parameters. (i) Show that the two planes are parallel. [2] (ii) Verify that the point \( A(2,0,1) \) lies on \( \Pi_1 \). Hence, or otherwise, find the exact distance between the two planes. [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) you cannot compare the two until they are in the SAME form
\( \Pi_2 \) is parametric, so extract its normal by crossing its two directions. \[ \mathbf n_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\2\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{pmatrix} \](1) This is identical to \( \mathbf n_1=(1,2,-1) \). Parallel planes have parallel normals. \[ \therefore\ \Pi_1\ \text{and}\ \Pi_2\ \text{are parallel.} \](1)
(ii) verify, then project the joining vector onto the unit normal
\( (2,0,1)\cdot(1,2,-1)=2+0-1=1 \) ✓ so \(A\) lies on \( \Pi_1 \).(1) Take \( B(3,2,3) \), a known point on \( \Pi_2 \). \( |\mathbf n|=\sqrt{1+4+1}=\sqrt6 \). \[ \overrightarrow{AB}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix};\qquad \text{distance}=\left|\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\right|=\frac{|1+4-2|}{\sqrt6}=\frac{3}{\sqrt6}=\frac{\sqrt6}{2} \](1)(1)
Why this one is here. The two planes are handed to you in different forms — one scalar product, one parametric — so you must convert before you can compare anything. That is the same three-forms fluency Q1 drilled, now with a consequence attached. If you had tried to compare \( (3,2,3) \) with \( (1,2,-1) \) directly you would have compared a point with a normal.
Tests: extracting a normal from parametric form to compare two planes; the parallel-planes test; distance between two parallel planes by projection onto the unit normal.

Question 7 [3]
HCI Mock M1 Q6 (parts)
The plane \( p_1 \) has equation \( x+2z=-3 \) and the plane \( p_2 \) has equation \( 2x+y+3z=10 \). The point \(Q\) has coordinates \( (-12,2,12) \). The line \(m\) passes through \(Q\) but does not intersect either \( p_1 \) or \( p_2 \). (i) State the angle between \(m\) and \( p_2 \). [1] (ii) Find the Cartesian equation of \(m\). [2]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) "does not intersect a plane" means parallel to it
A line that never meets a plane must be parallel to it. \[ \text{The angle between }m\text{ and }p_2\text{ is }0^\circ. \](1)
(ii) parallel to both planes \( \Rightarrow \) perpendicular to both normals
\( \mathbf n_1=(1,0,2) \) and \( \mathbf n_2=(2,1,3) \). If \(m\) is parallel to each plane then its direction is perpendicular to each normal — and the vector perpendicular to both is their cross product. \[ \mathbf d=\mathbf n_1\times\mathbf n_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\2\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\\1\end{pmatrix} \](1) Through \( Q(-12,2,12) \), using \( (2,-1,-1) \) (the same direction, sign-flipped for tidier signs): \[ \frac{x+12}{2}=\frac{y-2}{-1}=\frac{z-12}{-1},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{x+12}{2}=2-y=12-z \](1)
A third role for a normal. Q2/Q3 used a normal as an in-plane direction; Q5 used it in a dot-product equation; here two normals are crossed to produce a direction perpendicular to both. Ask every time: is this normal acting as a normal, as a direction, or as something to be perpendicular to?
Tests: a line parallel to two planes — direction as the cross product of the two normals; and the angle between a line and a plane it is parallel to.
