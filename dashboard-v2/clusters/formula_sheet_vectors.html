Exam conventions — these are marked, so bake them in.
  • Angles in degrees to 1 d.p. unless told otherwise. Set the calculator to degrees for vectors.
  • Coordinates when a point is asked for: \( (2,3,4) \). Column vector when a position vector is asked for.
  • Write a vector in handwriting as \( \underset{\sim}{a} \) (tilde under the letter) or \( \overrightarrow{AB} \); bold is print-only.
  • Exact form (surds, fractions) when asked; otherwise 3 s.f.
  • Always state \( \lambda\in\mathbb R \) (or \(\mu\), \(t\)…) at the end of a line or plane equation.

1  Basics — magnitude, unit vectors, position & displacement

Position vs displacement vector
\[ \overrightarrow{OA}=\mathbf a \quad\text{(position, always from the origin)};\qquad \overrightarrow{AB}=\overrightarrow{OB}-\overrightarrow{OA}=\mathbf b-\mathbf a \]
Position vector pins a point relative to \(O\). Displacement vector has magnitude and direction but no fixed location — it is "how to get from \(A\) to \(B\)". Nearly every question starts by converting given position vectors into displacement vectors.
Order matters: \( \overrightarrow{AB}=\mathbf b-\mathbf a \) is "end minus start", and \( \overrightarrow{BA}=-\overrightarrow{AB} \). Getting this backwards flips every sign downstream.
Magnitude
\[ |\mathbf v|=\left|\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}\right|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2} \]
Example
\( \mathbf v=(2,1,-5) \Rightarrow |\mathbf v|=\sqrt{4+1+25}=\sqrt{30} \).
Unit vector
\[ \hat{\mathbf v}=\frac{\mathbf v}{|\mathbf v|},\qquad |\hat{\mathbf v}|=1 \]
Strips the length and keeps pure direction. Needed for every projection and distance formula.
Example
\( \mathbf v=(2,1,-5),\ |\mathbf v|=\sqrt{30}\Rightarrow \hat{\mathbf v}=\tfrac1{\sqrt{30}}(2,1,-5) \).
"Find a unit vector" has two answers: \( \pm\hat{\mathbf v} \). Simplify first — \( \tfrac16(2,4,-4)=\tfrac13(1,2,-2) \).
Distance between two points
\[ AB=\left|\overrightarrow{AB}\right|=\left|\mathbf b-\mathbf a\right| \]
Angle between a vector and a coordinate axis
\[ \cos\theta=\frac{\left|\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf e\right|}{|\mathbf v|}\qquad\text{where }\mathbf e=\mathbf i,\ \mathbf j\ \text{or}\ \mathbf k \]
\(\mathbf e\) is the unit vector along the axis: \(x\)-axis \(=(1,0,0)\), \(y\)-axis \(=(0,1,0)\), \(z\)-axis \(=(0,0,1)\). Its magnitude is \(1\), so it disappears from the denominator.
Example
Acute angle between \( (1,2,-2) \) and the \(z\)-axis: \( \cos\theta=\dfrac{|(1,2,-2)\cdot(0,0,1)|}{3}=\dfrac23 \).

2  Ratio theorem & collinearity

Ratio theorem (internal division)
\[ \text{If }AP:PB=\lambda:\mu,\qquad \overrightarrow{OP}=\frac{\mu\,\mathbf a+\lambda\,\mathbf b}{\lambda+\mu} \]
The weight goes with the OPPOSITE endpoint. The \( \lambda \) that sits next to \(A\) in the ratio ends up multiplying \( \mathbf b \). Read it as: the further \(P\) is from a point, the more that point is weighted.
Example
\( AP:PB=2:3\Rightarrow \overrightarrow{OP}=\dfrac{3\mathbf a+2\mathbf b}{5} \).   Midpoint (\(1:1\)): \( \overrightarrow{OM}=\dfrac{\mathbf a+\mathbf b}{2} \).
The classic error is writing \( \dfrac{2\mathbf a+3\mathbf b}{5} \) for \(AP:PB=2:3\). Write the formula out in full every time until it is automatic.
External points / "produced"
No special formula needed. "\(B\) is the midpoint of \(AQ\)" just means \( \mathbf b=\dfrac{\mathbf a+\overrightarrow{OQ}}{2} \Rightarrow \overrightarrow{OQ}=2\mathbf b-\mathbf a \). Set up the relation and rearrange.
Collinearity of \(A\), \(B\), \(C\)
\[ \overrightarrow{AB}=k\,\overrightarrow{AC}\ \text{ for some scalar }k \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}=\mathbf 0 \]
Two conditions in one: the vectors are parallel AND they share the point \(A\). Sharing a point is what upgrades "parallel" to "collinear".
School key's explicit warning: you may not compute \( |\overrightarrow{CP}|=2\sqrt6 \), \( |\overrightarrow{PD}|=3\sqrt6 \) and conclude \(CP:PD=2:3\) — that does not show the points are collinear. You must show \( \overrightarrow{CP}=\tfrac23\overrightarrow{PD} \). And never write \( \dfrac{\overrightarrow{CP}}{\overrightarrow{PD}} \) — there is no division of vectors.

3  Scalar (dot) product

Definition — two equivalent forms
\[ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3 \]
The output is a scalar (a plain number), not a vector. Use the component form to compute, the \( \cos\theta \) form to interpret.
Properties
\[ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf a,\qquad \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b+\mathbf c)=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b+\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c,\qquad \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a=|\mathbf a|^2 \]
\( \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a=|\mathbf a|^2 \) is the workhorse for "find \( \lambda \)" questions: dot an expression with itself to turn vectors into magnitudes.
Example
Given \(3\mathbf b-2\mathbf c=\lambda\mathbf a\) with \(|\mathbf a|=|\mathbf c|=1\), \(|\mathbf b|=4\), \(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c=2\): dot each side with itself — \( 9|\mathbf b|^2-12\,\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c+4|\mathbf c|^2=\lambda^2|\mathbf a|^2 \Rightarrow 144-24+4=\lambda^2\Rightarrow\lambda=\pm2\sqrt{31} \).
What the sign tells you
ValueAngle between the vectors
\( \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b>0 \)acute
\( \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=0 \)perpendicular (for non-zero vectors)
\( \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b<0 \)obtuse
\( \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=0 \) is the single most-used condition in the whole topic: it drives perpendicularity, foot-of-perpendicular, "line lies in plane", and "vector parallel to plane".
Angle between two VECTORS
\[ \cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|}\qquad\text{(NO modulus)} \]
No modulus here — the angle between two vectors can be obtuse, and the school key says explicitly: "DO NOT put absolute sign here as angle between two vectors can be obtuse." The modulus belongs to the acute angle between lines or planes (§10).
Both vectors must diverge from the same point. For \( \angle ABO \) use \( \overrightarrow{BA} \) and \( \overrightarrow{BO} \) — both starting at \(B\). Using \( \overrightarrow{AB} \) instead flips the sign and gives the supplement.

4  Vector (cross) product

Definition & component formula
\[ \mathbf a\times\mathbf b=\begin{pmatrix}a_2b_3-a_3b_2\\a_3b_1-a_1b_3\\a_1b_2-a_2b_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad |\mathbf a\times\mathbf b|=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\sin\theta \]
The output is a vector, perpendicular to both \( \mathbf a \) and \( \mathbf b \) (right-hand rule). That is why it produces normals to planes.
Cover-up method
To get each component, cover that row and cross-multiply the other two: top-left×bottom-right − top-right×bottom-left. The middle component is \( a_3b_1-a_1b_3 \) — note the reversed order.
Check your cross product by dotting the result with each original vector: both must give \(0\). The school key recommends this every time; it costs seconds and catches sign slips.
Properties
\[ \mathbf a\times\mathbf b=-(\mathbf b\times\mathbf a),\qquad \mathbf a\times\mathbf a=\mathbf 0,\qquad \mathbf a\times(\mathbf b+\mathbf c)=\mathbf a\times\mathbf b+\mathbf a\times\mathbf c \]
Anti-commutative — swapping the order flips the sign. This is the main thing that breaks algebraic expansions.
What \( \mathbf a\times\mathbf b=\mathbf 0 \) means
\[ |\mathbf a|=0\quad\text{or}\quad|\mathbf b|=0\quad\text{or}\quad\mathbf a\parallel\mathbf b \]
A complete answer must include the degenerate cases. "They are parallel" alone is an incomplete deduction and drops a mark.
Dot vs cross — the one-line summary. The dot product returns a number and answers "how are these two oriented?" — zero means perpendicular. The cross product returns a vector and answers "what is perpendicular to both?" — zero means parallel. Dot for angles and perpendicularity; cross for normals and areas.

5  Projections

The shadow picture. Shine a light straight down onto the line of \( \mathbf b \). The length of the shadow of \( \mathbf a \) is the scalar projection; the shadow itself (an arrow along \( \mathbf b \)) is the projection vector; the height above the shadow is the perpendicular component.
Scalar projection — the LENGTH of the shadow
\[ \text{length of projection of }\mathbf a\text{ onto }\mathbf b=\left|\mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}\right|=\frac{|\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b|}{|\mathbf b|} \]
Why it works: \( \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta \), and the shadow length is \( |\mathbf a|\cos\theta \) — so divide by \( |\mathbf b| \) to cancel the unwanted factor. That is exactly what \( \hat{\mathbf b} \) does.
Has a modulus — a length is never negative.
Projection vector — the shadow ITSELF
\[ \text{projection vector of }\mathbf a\text{ onto }\mathbf b=\left(\mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}\right)\hat{\mathbf b}=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{|\mathbf b|^2}\,\mathbf b \]
No modulus here. The school key states it explicitly: "There is no modulus sign for projection vector as we need to retain the sign to give the direction of the projection vector." A negative coefficient means the shadow points backwards along \( \mathbf b \).
Reverse use
If you are told the projection vector of \( \mathbf b \) onto \( \mathbf a \) is \( \tfrac34\mathbf a \) and \( \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=4 \), then \( \dfrac{4}{|\mathbf a|^2}=\dfrac34\Rightarrow|\mathbf a|^2=\dfrac{16}{3}\Rightarrow|\mathbf a|=\dfrac{4\sqrt3}{3} \).
Perpendicular component (the "height")
\[ \left|\mathbf a\times\hat{\mathbf b}\right|=|\mathbf a|\sin\theta \qquad\text{or by Pythagoras}\qquad \sqrt{|\mathbf a|^2-\left(\mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}\right)^2} \]
This is the point-to-line distance formula (§11) — same quantity, different name.
Geometrical meanings you may be asked to state
ExpressionWhat to write
\( \left|\mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\right| \)the length of projection of \( \mathbf a \) onto \( \mathbf n \)
\( \left|\mathbf a\times\hat{\mathbf n}\right| \)the perpendicular distance from the tip of \( \mathbf a \) to the line along \( \mathbf n \)
\( \left|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\right| \)the area of the parallelogram with sides \( \mathbf a \) and \( \mathbf b \)
\( \tfrac12\left|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\right| \)the area of the triangle with sides \( \mathbf a \) and \( \mathbf b \)

6  Areas

Triangle and parallelogram
\[ \text{Area of }\triangle ABC=\tfrac12\left|\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}\right| \qquad\qquad \text{Area of parallelogram}=\left|\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AD}\right| \]
Both vectors must come from the same vertex — that is where the enclosed angle lives. Any two sides work: \( \overrightarrow{BA} \) and \( \overrightarrow{BC} \) give the same triangle area.
Take the magnitude. The cross product is a vector; the area is its length. Stopping at \( (1,5,-2) \) instead of \( \sqrt{30} \) throws away the answer mark. And the \( \tfrac12 \) belongs to the triangle only — a parallelogram has no half.
Alternative route
If you already know a base and the perpendicular height (e.g. from a projection), \( \text{Area}=\tfrac12\times\text{base}\times\text{height} \) is often faster.

7  Equations of a LINE

Vector form
\[ l:\ \mathbf r=\mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf d,\qquad \lambda\in\mathbb R \]
\( \mathbf a \) = position vector of any known point on the line. \( \mathbf d \) = any direction vector parallel to the line. Neither is unique — any point on the line and any scalar multiple of \( \mathbf d \) give the same line.
Through two points
\( \mathbf d=\overrightarrow{AB}=\mathbf b-\mathbf a \), then \( \mathbf r=\mathbf a+\lambda(\mathbf b-\mathbf a) \). Simplify \( \mathbf d \): \( (-8,-16,-8)\to(1,2,1) \).
Cartesian form & conversion
\[ \frac{x-a_1}{d_1}=\frac{y-a_2}{d_2}=\frac{z-a_3}{d_3}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\\a_3\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}d_1\\d_2\\d_3\end{pmatrix} \]
Read the point off the numerators and the direction off the denominators. To convert the other way, set each fraction equal to \( \lambda \) and solve for \(x\), \(y\), \(z\).
Sign traps in the numerator. \( \dfrac{10-x}{3}=\dfrac{x-10}{-3} \), so the direction component is \(-3\), not \(3\). Likewise \( -z-1=\dfrac{z+1}{-1} \). Always rewrite as \( \dfrac{x-a}{d} \) first.
Zero direction component. If \( d_2=0 \) you cannot divide — write it as a separate condition, e.g. \( \dfrac{x}{3}=\dfrac{z}{5},\ y=4 \).
Is a point on the line?
Substitute the point and solve for \( \lambda \) from one component; it must give the same \( \lambda \) in all three. One mismatch means the point is not on the line.
Point on a line at a given distance
Write the general point \( \mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf d \), set \( |\,\cdot\,|^2=k^2 \), and solve the quadratic in \( \lambda \) — normally two answers.

The decision procedure
StepTestConclusion

9  Equations of a PLANE

Three forms
\[ \text{Parametric: }\ \mathbf r=\mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf d_1+\mu\mathbf d_2,\quad\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb R \] \[ \text{Scalar product: }\ \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n=d \qquad\qquad \text{Cartesian: }\ n_1x+n_2y+n_3z=d \]
\( \mathbf d_1,\mathbf d_2 \) = two non-parallel directions lying in the plane. \( \mathbf n \) = a normal, obtained as \( \mathbf n=\mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2 \). \( d \) = the constant \( \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n \).
Route
Almost always: find two in-plane directions → cross them for \( \mathbf n \) → simplify \( \mathbf n \) → use \( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n \) → read off the Cartesian form. Scalar-product form first, Cartesian second — it is easier that way.
Parametric form is not unique, and the two directions must be non-parallel.
Getting the two in-plane directions — by situation
GivenDirections to use
Three points \(A\), \(B\), \(C\)\( \overrightarrow{AB} \) and \( \overrightarrow{AC} \) — both from the same point
A line, plus a point off itthe line's \( \mathbf d \), and \( \overrightarrow{AB} \) from a point of the line to the given point
The origin, plus a linethe line's \( \mathbf d \), and the position vector of a point on the line (then \(d=0\))
Two intersecting linesthe two direction vectors; use the common point as \( \mathbf a \)
A point and a normal \( \mathbf n \)find any two non-parallel \( \mathbf v \) with \( \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n=0 \), by inspection
A line, and perpendicular to plane \( \Pi \)the line's \( \mathbf d \), and \( \Pi \)'s normal (it lies inside your plane)
Read what "perpendicular" attaches to.
• Plane perpendicular to a LINE \(AB\)  ⟹  \( \overrightarrow{AB} \) IS the normal \( \mathbf n \).
• Plane perpendicular to a PLANE \( \Pi \)  ⟹  \( \Pi \)'s normal is a direction inside your plane (so cross it with your other direction to get your normal).
These are opposite roles for the same word. Deciding which one applies is the whole first mark.
Always simplify the normal
\[ (-8,0,16)=-8(1,0,-2)\ \longrightarrow\ \text{use }(1,0,-2);\qquad (-3,3,3)=3(-1,1,1)\ \longrightarrow\ \text{use }(-1,1,1) \]
Any scalar multiple of a normal is an equally valid normal. Factor the common scalar out before computing \( d \) — it keeps every later distance and angle calculation clean.
Checks worth doing
Dot your normal with each in-plane direction: both must be \(0\). Then verify a known point satisfies your final equation.

11  Distances

Point to a PLANE
\[ \text{distance}=\left|\overrightarrow{BA}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\right|=\frac{\left|\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n-d\right|}{|\mathbf n|} \]
\(A\) = the point you are measuring from. \(B\) = any known point on the plane. \( \mathbf n \) = the plane's normal, read straight off \( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=d \). \(d\) = the plane's constant.
Example
\(A(1,2,3)\), plane \( \mathbf r\cdot(-1,2,2)=2 \), \(B(2,1,1)\) on it. \( \overrightarrow{BA}=(-1,1,2) \), \( |\mathbf n|=3 \). Distance \( =\tfrac13|1+2+4|=\tfrac73 \).
Point to a LINE
\[ \text{distance}=\left|\overrightarrow{BA}\times\hat{\mathbf d}\right| \qquad\text{or}\qquad \sqrt{\left|\overrightarrow{BA}\right|^2-\left(\overrightarrow{BA}\cdot\hat{\mathbf d}\right)^2} \]
\(B\) = any known point on the line, \( \mathbf d \) = the line's direction. The second form is Pythagoras: (hypotenuse)² − (projection)².
Example
\(A(1,2,3)\), line through \(B(2,1,1)\) with \( \mathbf d=(0,-1,1) \). \( \overrightarrow{BA}=(-1,1,2) \); \( \overrightarrow{BA}\times\mathbf d=(3,1,1) \); distance \( =\dfrac{\sqrt{11}}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt{\tfrac{11}{2}} \).
Two parallel planes / a line parallel to a plane
\[ \text{distance}=\left|\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\right|\quad\text{with }A\text{ on one, }B\text{ on the other}\qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{|d_1-d_2|}{|\mathbf n|} \]
For the \( \dfrac{|d_1-d_2|}{|\mathbf n|} \) form the two normals must be written identically (same scalar multiple) before you compare the constants.
Only TWO distance formulas to choose between. Point→plane uses the dot with \( \hat{\mathbf n} \); point→line uses the cross with \( \hat{\mathbf d} \). Nothing else. Dot for planes, cross for lines.

And note: the distance from \(A\) to a line lying in a plane is not generally the distance from \(A\) to the plane — they are equal only if the line passes through the foot of the perpendicular.

12  Foot of perpendicular — the two procedures

Side by side
To a LINE \( \mathbf r=\mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf d \)To a PLANE \( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=d \)
1. Write the foot\( \overrightarrow{OF}=\mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf d \)  (general point on the line)\( \overrightarrow{OF}=\overrightarrow{OP}+\lambda\mathbf n \)  (travel from \(P\) along the normal)
2. Impose\( \overrightarrow{PF}\cdot\mathbf d=0 \)  (perpendicular to the direction)substitute \( \overrightarrow{OF} \) into the plane equation
3. Solvefor \( \lambda \), then substitute backfor \( \lambda \), then substitute back
The distinction that keeps catching you. To a line there is nothing to substitute into — you impose a dot-product-zero condition instead. To a plane there is no perpendicularity to impose — the normal direction is already known, so you substitute into the plane. Line → dot with direction. Plane → sub into equation.
Alternative (projection route)
Foot on a line: \( \overrightarrow{OF}=\mathbf a+\left(\overrightarrow{AP}\cdot\hat{\mathbf d}\right)\hat{\mathbf d} \). Foot on a plane: \( \overrightarrow{OF}=\overrightarrow{OP}-\left(\overrightarrow{QP}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\right)\hat{\mathbf n} \) for any \(Q\) on the plane.

13  Reflections

The master relation
\[ \overrightarrow{OP'}=2\,\overrightarrow{OF}-\overrightarrow{OP} \]
\(P\) = the original point, \(P'\) = its image, \(F\) = the foot of the perpendicular from \(P\). It follows from \(F\) being the midpoint of \(PP'\).
One formula covers reflection in a line and in a plane — only the way you find \(F\) changes (§12).
Reflecting a LINE
Reflect a point of it, then join. If the line meets the mirror at \(A\), that \(A\) is fixed — so the image line is \(A\) joined to \(P'\): \( \mathbf r=\mathbf a+\mu\,\overrightarrow{AP'} \).

14  Intersections

Line ∩ plane
Substitute the line's general point into \( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=d \), solve for \( \lambda \), substitute back. Three outcomes:
AlgebraGeometry
a unique \( \lambda \)the line cuts the plane at one point
identity, true for all \( \lambda \)the line lies in the plane
contradiction, no \( \lambda \)the line is parallel to and outside the plane
To show a line LIES IN a plane
Either substitute and get an identity in \( \lambda \); or show both \( \mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n=0 \) (parallel) and one point of the line satisfies the plane.
Plane ∩ plane — the line of intersection
\[ \mathbf d=\mathbf n_1\times\mathbf n_2 \]
The line lies in both planes, so it is perpendicular to both normals — which is exactly what the cross product gives. Then find one point on both planes.
Why you may set a coordinate to zero. The line of intersection is infinite. Fixing \(z=0\) just picks out the single point where the line crosses the \(xy\)-plane. Any consistent choice lands on the same line — only the "starting point" \( \mathbf a \) changes, and \( \mathbf a \) was never unique anyway. (If \(z=0\) gives no solution, the line is parallel to that plane — try \(x=0\) or \(y=0\) instead.)
Fastest in practice
Find a common point by inspection — try small integers in both equations. Or solve the 2×3 system on the GC and read off its general solution.
The pencil of planes through a line
\[ (n_1x+n_2y+n_3z-d_1)+k(m_1x+m_2y+m_3z-d_2)=0 \]
Every point of the line of intersection makes both brackets zero, so it satisfies this for any \(k\). Choose \(k\) to force one extra condition — e.g. to pass through a given point.
Three planes
Solve the 3×3 system on the GC. Unique solution → one common point. No solution → no common point (parallel, or forming a triangular prism). Infinitely many → they share a common line.

If \( \alpha\mathbf a+\beta\mathbf b=\gamma\mathbf a+\delta\mathbf b \) and \( \mathbf a \), \( \mathbf b \) are non-parallel, then \( \alpha=\gamma \) and \( \beta=\delta \). State the non-parallel condition — it is a marked step, and the deduction is false without it.

17  Excluded from 9758 — do not use

These are out of syllabus. Reaching for one wastes time and signals a method the examiner is not asking for.
  • Triple products \( \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) \) and \( \mathbf a\times(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) \).
  • Shortest distance between two skew lines, and the common perpendicular to two skew lines. (The formula \( \frac{|(\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1)\cdot(\mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2)|}{|\mathbf d_1\times\mathbf d_2|} \) is excluded twice over — it is also a triple product.)
If a stem looks like skew-line distance, the real ask is point-to-line minimisation. Note that a question may still scaffold you through a common-perpendicular construction step by step — that is fine, because each step is in syllabus; what is excluded is quoting the closed formula.
