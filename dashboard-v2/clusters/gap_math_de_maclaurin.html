Question 1 [5]
CJC 2015 Tutorial Q7 · HCI Ch9 Supplementary Q1
In a favourable environment the number of bacteria increases at a rate proportional to the number present. If \( 1\,000\,000 \) bacteria are present at a certain time and \( 2\,000\,000 \) are present an hour later, find the number of bacteria present four hours after the first measurement. [5]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
form and solve the DE — general solution first
"Rate of increase proportional to the number present" with \(N\) the number at time \(t\) hours. \[ \frac{\mathrm dN}{\mathrm dt}=kN,\qquad k>0 \](1) \[ \int\frac1N\,\mathrm dN=\int k\,\mathrm dt\ \Rightarrow\ \ln N=kt+c\ \Rightarrow\ N=A e^{kt},\quad A=e^{c} \](1)
FIRST data point gives \(A\)
Take \(t=0\) at the first measurement: \(N=1\,000\,000\). \[ 1\,000\,000=Ae^{0}\ \Rightarrow\ A=10^{6} \](1)
SECOND data point gives \(k\) — this is the step that is easy to skip
At \(t=1\), \(N=2\,000\,000\). \[ 2\times10^{6}=10^{6}e^{k}\ \Rightarrow\ e^{k}=2\ \Rightarrow\ k=\ln 2 \](1) \[ \therefore\ N=10^{6}e^{(\ln 2)t}=10^{6}\cdot 2^{\,t} \]
now answer what was asked
\[ t=4:\qquad N=10^{6}\times 2^{4}=16\,000\,000 \](1)
The exact miss from the cooling question. A particular solution has two unknowns — the constant of integration \(A\) and the proportionality constant \(k\). You need two pieces of data: the initial condition pins \(A\), a second reading pins \(k\). If a stem gives you two data points, that is why. Finding \(A\) and stopping leaves \(k\) unknown and the question unanswerable.
Tests: forming an exponential-growth DE, and using both given data points — one for \(A\), one for \(k\) — before evaluating.

Question 2 [8]
A-Level 2016 P1 Q8
A stone is released on the surface of a pond and falls vertically. The distance fallen in \(t\) seconds is \(x\) metres, and \(x=0\) and \( \dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}=0 \) when \(t=0\). The motion is modelled by \( \dfrac{\mathrm d^2x}{\mathrm dt^2}+2\dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}=10 \). (i)(a) By substituting \( y=\dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt} \), show that the differential equation can be written as \( \dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dt}=10-2y \). [1] (i)(b) Find \(y\) in terms of \(t\), and hence find \(x\) in terms of \(t\). [6] (ii) The pond is \(5\) metres deep. Find the time the stone takes to reach the bottom, giving your answer to 2 decimal places. [1]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i)(a) the substitution drops the order by one
\( y=\dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt} \Rightarrow \dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dt}=\dfrac{\mathrm d^2x}{\mathrm dt^2} \). \[ \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dt}+2y=10\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dt}=10-2y\ \text{(shown)} \](1)
(i)(b) separate and solve for \(y\)
\[ \int\frac{1}{10-2y}\,\mathrm dy=\int\mathrm dt\ \Rightarrow\ -\tfrac12\ln|10-2y|=t+c \](1) \[ 10-2y=Ae^{-2t}\ \Rightarrow\ y=5-\tfrac{A}{2}e^{-2t} \](1) \( y=0 \) when \( t=0 \): \( 0=5-\tfrac A2 \Rightarrow \tfrac A2=5 \). \[ y=5\left(1-e^{-2t}\right) \](1)
now INTEGRATE \(y\) to get \(x\) — a second integration, so a second constant
\( y=\dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt} \), so \( x=\displaystyle\int y\,\mathrm dt \). \[ x=\int 5\left(1-e^{-2t}\right)\mathrm dt=5t+\tfrac52 e^{-2t}+D \](1) \( x=0 \) when \( t=0 \): \( 0=0+\tfrac52+D \Rightarrow D=-\tfrac52 \). \[ x=5t-\tfrac52+\tfrac52 e^{-2t} \](1)(1)
(ii) solve for the time
\[ 5t-\tfrac52+\tfrac52 e^{-2t}=5\ \Rightarrow\ t=1.47\ \text{s (2 d.p.)} \](1) (No exact rearrangement — solve numerically on the GC, but show the equation you solved.)
Distance is never velocity ÷ time here. The velocity is changing, so you must integrate: \( x=\int v\,\mathrm dt \). Differentiate for acceleration, integrate for displacement. Note also the two separate constants — \(A\) from solving for \(y\), then a fresh \(D\) from integrating again. Two integrations, two letters.
Tests: reducing a second-order equation with \( y=\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt} \); solving for velocity; then integrating velocity to displacement with a second independent constant; and a numerical solve.

Question 3 [8]
SAJC 2016 Prelim P1 Q8 (modified) · HCI Tutorial 9 Q6
The variables \(w\), \(x\) and \(y\) are connected by the differential equations \[ \frac{\mathrm dw}{\mathrm dx}=-\tfrac32 w-2\quad\text{(A)},\qquad\qquad \frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=w\quad\text{(B)}. \] (i) Solve equation (A) to find \(w\) in terms of \(x\). [3] (ii) Hence find \(y\) in terms of \(x\). [2] (iii) Different arbitrary constants found in part (ii) yield different solution curves. Some of the solution curves are straight lines. Write down the equation of one of these lines. On a single diagram, sketch your line together with a non-linear solution curve that has your line as an asymptote, indicating clearly any axes intercepts. [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) separate, integrate, keep the constant alive
\[ \frac{\mathrm dw}{\mathrm dx}=-\frac{3w+4}{2}\ \Rightarrow\ \int\frac{2}{3w+4}\,\mathrm dw=-\int\mathrm dx \](1) \[ \tfrac23\ln|3w+4|=-x+c\ \Rightarrow\ \ln|3w+4|=-\tfrac32x+c' \](1) Exponentiate; \( \pm e^{c'} \) collapses into a single new constant \(B\). \[ 3w+4=Be^{-\frac32x}\ \Rightarrow\ w=\tfrac13 Be^{-\frac32x}-\tfrac43 \](1)
(ii) integrate again — and use a NEW letter for the new constant
\[ y=\int w\,\mathrm dx=\int\left(\tfrac13 Be^{-\frac32x}-\tfrac43\right)\mathrm dx=-\tfrac29 Be^{-\frac32x}-\tfrac43x+D \](1) Write \( C=-\tfrac29B \) to tidy it. \(C\) and \(D\) are independent arbitrary constants. \[ y=Ce^{-\frac32x}-\tfrac43x+D \](1)
(iii) the family — and which member is a straight line
Different \((C,D)\) give different members of the family. The curve is a straight line exactly when the exponential term vanishes, i.e. \( C=0 \). \[ C=0:\qquad y=-\tfrac43x+D. \quad\text{Taking }D=0:\ \ \boxed{y=-\tfrac43x} \](1) For a non-linear member take \(C\neq0\), say \(C=1\), \(D=0\): \( y=e^{-\frac32x}-\tfrac43x \). As \(x\to\infty\), \( e^{-\frac32x}\to0 \), so this curve approaches the line \(y=-\tfrac43x\) from above — the line is its asymptote. As \(x\to-\infty\) the exponential blows up, so the curve rises steeply. It cuts the \(y\)-axis at \( y=1 \). \[ \text{Sketch: the line }y=-\tfrac43x\text{, and the curve }y=e^{-\frac32x}-\tfrac43x\text{ hugging it as }x\to\infty,\ \text{with }y\text{-intercept }1. \](1)(1)
"Family of solution curves" — what the question actually wants. The general solution contains arbitrary constants; each choice of constants is one member of the family. Two things earn the marks: (1) name a specific member (here, kill the exponential with \(C=0\) to get the straight line); (2) sketch a second, non-linear member and show the shared asymptotic behaviour — because the decaying exponential dies, every member converges onto the same line. Mark axes intercepts.
Tests: keeping the arbitrary constant through exponentiation; a second integration introducing a second independent constant; and reading a family of solution curves off the general solution.

Question 4 [5]
NYJC 2020 Prelim P1 Q7 · HCI Tutorial 9 Q5
The curve \(C\), where \(y\ge 0\), satisfies the differential equation \[ \left(1+x^2\right)y\,\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}+2xy^2=0, \] where \(y=1\) when \(x=0\). By using the substitution \( z=y^2 \), solve the differential equation and find the value of \(y\) when \(x=1\). [5]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
differentiate the substitution, then substitute BOTH things
\( z=y^2 \). Differentiate implicitly with respect to \(x\): \( \dfrac{\mathrm dz}{\mathrm dx}=2y\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx} \), so \( y\dfrac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}=\tfrac12\dfrac{\mathrm dz}{\mathrm dx} \). Now replace both \( y\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx} \) and \( y^2 \) in the equation. \[ \left(1+x^2\right)\cdot\tfrac12\frac{\mathrm dz}{\mathrm dx}+2xz=0\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{\mathrm dz}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{-4xz}{1+x^2} \](1)(1)
separate — the right side is an \(f'/f\) log
\[ \int\frac1z\,\mathrm dz=-2\int\frac{2x}{1+x^2}\,\mathrm dx\ \Rightarrow\ \ln|z|=-2\ln\left(1+x^2\right)+c \](1) \[ z=\frac{A}{\left(1+x^2\right)^{2}} \]
return to \(y\), then apply the condition
\( z=y^2 \) and \( y\ge0 \), so take the positive root. \[ y=\frac{\sqrt A}{1+x^2}. \qquad y=1\ \text{when}\ x=0\ \Rightarrow\ \sqrt A=1 \] \[ y=\frac{1}{1+x^2} \](1) \[ x=1:\qquad y=\tfrac12 \](1)
Substitution DEs — the two-substitution rule you worked out yourself. Whatever the substitution, you always do two replacements: (1) differentiate the substitution to convert the derivative, (2) replace the original variable wherever it appears. Here \(z=y^2\) handles both \(y\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx}\) and \(y^2\). Miss either and you are left with a mixed equation you cannot separate.
Tests: a substitution differential equation of a different flavour from \(z=x-y\) — here \(z=y^2\) — plus an \(f'/f\) integral and returning to the original variable before applying the condition.

Question 6 [8]
A-Level 2011 P1 Q4
(i) Use the first three non-zero terms of the Maclaurin series for \( \cos x \) to find the Maclaurin series for \( \mathrm g(x) \), where \( \mathrm g(x)=\cos^6 x \), up to and including the term in \( x^4 \). [3] (ii)(a) Use your answer to part (i) to give an approximation for \( \displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\mathrm g(x)\,\mathrm dx \) in terms of \(a\), and evaluate this approximation in the case where \( a=\tfrac{\pi}{4} \). [3] (ii)(b) Use your calculator to find an accurate value for \( \displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\mathrm g(x)\,\mathrm dx \). Why is the approximation in part (ii)(a) not very good? [2]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) raise the series to the sixth power, keeping only up to \(x^4\)
\( \cos x=1-\tfrac{x^2}{2}+\tfrac{x^4}{24}-\cdots \). Write \( u=-\tfrac{x^2}{2}+\tfrac{x^4}{24} \) and use \( (1+u)^6=1+6u+15u^2+\cdots \) — terms beyond \(u^2\) start at \(x^6\), so they are not needed. \[ 6u=-3x^2+\tfrac{x^4}{4};\qquad 15u^2=15\left(\tfrac{x^4}{4}\right)=\tfrac{15}{4}x^4\ \ \left(\text{only the }x^4\text{ part of }u^2\right) \](1)(1) \[ \cos^6x=1-3x^2+\left(\tfrac14+\tfrac{15}{4}\right)x^4+\cdots=1-3x^2+4x^4+\cdots \](1)
(ii)(a) integrate the series term by term
\[ \int_{0}^{a}\mathrm g(x)\,\mathrm dx\approx\int_{0}^{a}\left(1-3x^2+4x^4\right)\mathrm dx=\left[x-x^3+\tfrac45x^5\right]_{0}^{a}=a-a^3+\tfrac45a^5 \](1)(1) \[ a=\tfrac{\pi}{4}:\qquad \tfrac{\pi}{4}-\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)^3+\tfrac45\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)^5=0.540\ (3\ \text{s.f.}) \](1)
(ii)(b) compare, then explain WHY
\[ \int_{0}^{\pi/4}\cos^6x\,\mathrm dx=0.475\ (3\ \text{s.f.}) \](1) The approximation \(0.540\) is well off the accurate \(0.475\). \[ \text{Because }a=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\approx0.785\ \text{is \textbf{not small}, so the truncated series } 1-3x^2+4x^4 \] \[ \text{deviates significantly from }\cos^6x\ \text{over much of the interval of integration.} \](1)
"Why is the approximation not good?" — the answer you were missing. Two things to check, in order:
1. Is \(x\) inside the validity range? If the expansion is invalid there, that alone answers it.
2. If it is valid, is \(x\) small? A Maclaurin series is built at \(x=0\) and only hugs the function near \(0\). Here \( \tfrac{\pi}{4}\approx0.785 \) is far from \(0\), so the truncated series drifts.
Say the number, say the comparison, and say why — a bare "it's not accurate" scores nothing.
Tests: building a series for a power of a standard series; term-by-term integration as an approximation; and justifying the quality of an approximation by how far the point is from the expansion centre.
