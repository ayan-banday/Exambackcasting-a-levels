Exam conventions for vectors — these are marked, so follow them:
  • Angles in degrees to 1 decimal place unless the question says otherwise. Set your calculator to degrees for this sheet.
  • Use \( \cos\theta = \dfrac{|\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b|}{|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|} \) with the modulus for the acute angle between lines/planes — but no modulus for the angle between two vectors, which may be obtuse.
  • Give coordinates when asked for a point, and a column vector when asked for a position vector.
  • Exact form (surds, fractions) when asked; otherwise 3 significant figures.
  • Out of syllabus in 9758, so never reach for them: triple products \( \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c) \), and the shortest distance between two skew lines.

Question 1 [9]
A-Level 2007 P1 Q6
Referred to the origin \(O\), the position vectors of the points \(A\) and \(B\) are \( \mathbf i-\mathbf j+2\mathbf k \) and \( 2\mathbf i+4\mathbf j+\mathbf k \) respectively. (i) Show that \(OA\) is perpendicular to \(OB\). [2] (ii) Find the position vector of the point \(M\) on the line segment \(AB\) such that \(AM:MB=1:2\). [3] (iii) The point \(C\) has position vector \( -4\mathbf i+2\mathbf j+2\mathbf k \). Use a vector product to find the exact area of triangle \(OAC\). [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) perpendicular \(\Leftrightarrow\) scalar product zero
\[ \overrightarrow{OA}\cdot\overrightarrow{OB}=(1)(2)+(-1)(4)+(2)(1)=2-4+2=0 \](1) Since \( \overrightarrow{OA}\cdot\overrightarrow{OB}=0 \) and neither vector is zero, \(OA\) is perpendicular to \(OB\).(1)
(ii) ratio theorem
\(M\) divides \(AB\) with \(AM:MB=\lambda:\mu=1:2\). In \( \overrightarrow{OM}=\dfrac{\mu\,\mathbf a+\lambda\,\mathbf b}{\lambda+\mu} \) the weight sits with the opposite endpoint, so \(\mathbf a\) carries the \(2\). \[ \overrightarrow{OM}=\frac{2\,\overrightarrow{OA}+\overrightarrow{OB}}{3}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2+2\\-2+4\\4+1\end{pmatrix}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}4\\2\\5\end{pmatrix} \](1)(1)(1)
(iii) area of a triangle from a vector product
Two sides from the same corner \(O\): \( \overrightarrow{OA}=(1,-1,2) \) and \( \overrightarrow{OC}=(-4,2,2) \). \[ \overrightarrow{OA}\times\overrightarrow{OC}=\begin{pmatrix}(-1)(2)-(2)(2)\\(2)(-4)-(1)(2)\\(1)(2)-(-1)(-4)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-6\\-10\\-2\end{pmatrix} \](1)(1) \[ \text{Area}=\tfrac12\left|\overrightarrow{OA}\times\overrightarrow{OC}\right|=\tfrac12\sqrt{36+100+4}=\tfrac12\sqrt{140}=\sqrt{35}\ \text{units}^2 \](1)(1)
Mark-loser: the area is \( \tfrac12\big|\mathbf a\times\mathbf c\big| \) — you must take the magnitude of the cross product. Stopping at the vector \((-6,-10,-2)\) scores the method mark and loses the answer mark.
Tests: perpendicularity by scalar product; ratio theorem (weight on the opposite endpoint); area of a triangle as half the magnitude of a vector product.

Question 2 [4]
A-Level 2021 P1 Q7(a)
The line \(l_1\) has equation \( \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}3\\2\\0\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}2\\-3\\1\end{pmatrix} \), where \(\lambda\) is a parameter. Find a Cartesian equation of the plane containing \(l_1\) and the point \( (1,-3,-1) \). [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
two directions in the plane, then a normal
The plane contains \(l_1\), so its direction \( \mathbf d=(2,-3,1) \) lies in the plane. A second direction: join a known point of \(l_1\), \(A(3,2,0)\), to the given point \(B(1,-3,-1)\). \[ \overrightarrow{AB}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-3\\-1\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}3\\2\\0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\-5\\-1\end{pmatrix} \](1) \[ \mathbf n=\overrightarrow{AB}\times\mathbf d=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\-5\\-1\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}2\\-3\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-8\\0\\16\end{pmatrix}=-8\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-2\end{pmatrix} \](1)
use \( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n \), then read off the Cartesian form
Take the simplified normal \( \mathbf n=(1,0,-2) \) and the known point \( \mathbf a=(3,2,0) \) on the plane. \[ \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\2\\0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-2\end{pmatrix}=3 \](1) \[ \therefore\ x-2z=3 \](1)
The move worth drilling: \((-8,0,16)=-8(1,0,-2)\). Always factor the common scalar out of a normal before using it — any scalar multiple is an equally valid normal, and the small one keeps the arithmetic clean. The same simplification is why \(3(-1,1,1)\) becomes \((-1,1,1)\).
Tests: building a plane from a line plus an external point — two in-plane directions, normal by vector product, simplify the normal, then \( \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n \) converted to Cartesian form.

Question 3 [12]
A-Level J93 P1 Q14 · HCI Tutorial 7B Q10
The equation of a straight line \(l\) is \( \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix} \), \(t\in\mathbb R\). The point \(A\) on \(l\) is given by \(t=0\), and \(O\) is the origin of position vectors. (i) Calculate the acute angle between \(OA\) and \(l\), giving your answer correct to the nearest degree. [2] (ii) Find the position vector of the point \(P\) on \(l\) such that \(OP\) is perpendicular to \(l\). [3] (iii) A point \(Q\) on \(l\) is such that the length of \(OQ\) is \(5\) units. Find the two possible position vectors of \(Q\). [4] (iv) The points \(R\) and \(S\) on \(l\) are given by \(t=\lambda\) and \(t=2\lambda\) respectively. Show that there is no value of \(\lambda\) for which \(OR\) and \(OS\) are perpendicular. [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) acute angle between a vector and a line
\(t=0\) gives \( \overrightarrow{OA}=(1,2,3) \); the line's direction is \( \mathbf d=(-1,1,1) \). Modulus because an acute angle is required. \[ \cos\theta=\frac{\left|(1,2,3)\cdot(-1,1,1)\right|}{\sqrt{14}\,\sqrt{3}}=\frac{4}{\sqrt{42}} \](1) \[ \theta=52^\circ\ \text{(nearest degree)} \](1)
(ii) foot of perpendicular from a point to a LINE
\(P\) lies on \(l\), so write the general point and impose that \( \overrightarrow{OP} \) is perpendicular to the direction of \(l\). \[ \overrightarrow{OP}=\begin{pmatrix}1-k\\2+k\\3+k\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for some }k\in\mathbb R \](1) \[ \overrightarrow{OP}\cdot\mathbf d=0:\ -(1-k)+(2+k)+(3+k)=0\ \Rightarrow\ 3k+4=0\ \Rightarrow\ k=-\tfrac43 \](1) \[ \overrightarrow{OP}=\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}7\\2\\5\end{pmatrix} \](1)
(iii) point on a line at a given distance — quadratic in the parameter
\[ \left|\overrightarrow{OQ}\right|^2=(1-t)^2+(2+t)^2+(3+t)^2=25 \](1) \[ 3t^2+8t-11=0\ \Rightarrow\ (3t+11)(t-1)=0\ \Rightarrow\ t=-\tfrac{11}{3}\ \text{or}\ t=1 \](1)(1) \[ \overrightarrow{OQ}=\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}14\\-5\\-2\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{or}\quad\overrightarrow{OQ}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\3\\4\end{pmatrix} \](1)
(iv) discriminant argument
\( \overrightarrow{OR}=(1-\lambda,\,2+\lambda,\,3+\lambda) \) and \( \overrightarrow{OS}=(1-2\lambda,\,2+2\lambda,\,3+2\lambda) \). Suppose they were perpendicular. \[ \overrightarrow{OR}\cdot\overrightarrow{OS}=6\lambda^2+12\lambda+14=0\ \Rightarrow\ 3\lambda^2+6\lambda+7=0 \](1)(1) Discriminant \( =6^2-4(3)(7)=-48<0 \), so this quadratic has no real roots. \[ \therefore\ \text{there is no value of }\lambda\text{ for which }OR\perp OS. \](1)
Tests: angle between a vector and a line; foot of perpendicular to a line by the dot-with-direction method; locating points on a line at a fixed distance; a discriminant argument to prove a configuration is impossible.

Question 4 [5]
A-Level 2014 P1 Q3
(i) Given that \( \mathbf a\times\mathbf b=\mathbf 0 \), what can be deduced about the vectors \( \mathbf a \) and \( \mathbf b \)? [2] (ii) Find a unit vector \( \mathbf n \) such that \( \mathbf n\times(\mathbf i+2\mathbf j-2\mathbf k)=\mathbf 0 \). [2] (iii) Find the cosine of the acute angle between \( \mathbf i+2\mathbf j-2\mathbf k \) and the \(z\)-axis. [1]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) what a zero vector product means
\( |\mathbf a\times\mathbf b|=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\sin\theta \), so the product vanishes when a magnitude is zero or when \( \sin\theta=0 \). \[ |\mathbf a|=0\ \text{ or }\ |\mathbf b|=0\ \text{ or }\ \mathbf a\text{ is parallel to }\mathbf b. \](1)(1)
(ii) parallel, and of unit length
By (i), \( \mathbf n \) must be parallel to \( (1,2,-2) \), whose magnitude is \( \sqrt{1+4+4}=3 \). \[ \mathbf n=\pm\tfrac13\big(\mathbf i+2\mathbf j-2\mathbf k\big) \](1)(1)
(iii) angle with a coordinate axis
The \(z\)-axis has direction \( \mathbf k=(0,0,1) \); modulus for the acute angle. \[ \cos\theta=\frac{\left|(1,2,-2)\cdot(0,0,1)\right|}{3\times 1}=\frac{2}{3} \](1)
Don't lose the \(\pm\): "a unit vector" parallel to a given direction has two answers. And note (i) must include the degenerate cases \( |\mathbf a|=0 \) or \( |\mathbf b|=0 \) — "parallel" alone is an incomplete deduction.
Tests: the geometric meaning of a zero vector product; unit vectors; the angle between a vector and a coordinate axis.

Question 5 [18]
HCI Tutorial 7C Q4
The point \(A\) has position vector \( \mathbf i+2\mathbf j+3\mathbf k \); the line \(l_1\) has equation \( \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}0\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix} \), \(t\in\mathbb R\); the line \(l_2\) has equation \( \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\\2\end{pmatrix}+s\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix} \), \(s\in\mathbb R\); and the plane \( \Pi \) has equation \( \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix}=2 \). (a) Show that \(l_1\) lies on \( \Pi \). [3] (b) Find the perpendicular distance from \(A\) to \( \Pi \). [3] (c) Find the perpendicular distance from \(A\) to \(l_1\). [3] (d) Find the coordinates of the point of intersection between \(l_2\) and \( \Pi \). [3] (e) Find the position vector of the foot of perpendicular from \(A\) to \(l_2\). [3] (f) Find the position vector of the foot of perpendicular from \(A\) to \( \Pi \). [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(a) a line lies in a plane — two things to show
Substitute the general point of \(l_1\) into the plane equation. \( \mathbf n=(-1,2,2) \) is the plane's normal. \[ (2,\,1-t,\,1+t)\cdot(-1,2,2)=-2+2-2t+2+2t=2 \](1)(1) This holds for every \(t\), so every point of \(l_1\) satisfies \( \Pi \). \[ \therefore\ l_1\ \text{lies on}\ \Pi. \](1)
(b) distance from a point to a PLANE — project onto the unit normal
Take \(B(2,1,1)\), a known point on \( \Pi \). \( \hat{\mathbf n}=\tfrac13(-1,2,2) \) since \( |\mathbf n|=3 \). \[ \overrightarrow{BA}=(1,2,3)-(2,1,1)=(-1,1,2) \] \[ \text{distance}=\left|\overrightarrow{BA}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\right|=\tfrac13\left|1+2+4\right|=\frac73\ \text{units} \](1)(1)(1)
(c) distance from a point to a LINE — cross with the unit direction
Same \(B\) on \(l_1\); \( \hat{\mathbf d}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(0,-1,1) \). \[ \text{distance}=\left|\overrightarrow{BA}\times\hat{\mathbf d}\right|=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\left|(-1,1,2)\times(0,-1,1)\right|=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\left|(3,1,1)\right|=\sqrt{\tfrac{11}{2}}\ \text{units} \](1)(1)(1)
(d) line meets plane — substitute the general point
\[ (3+s,\,4+s,\,2-2s)\cdot(-1,2,2)=2\ \Rightarrow\ -3-s+8+2s+4-4s=2\ \Rightarrow\ s=\tfrac73 \](1)(1) \[ \text{Coordinates }\left(\tfrac{16}{3},\ \tfrac{19}{3},\ -\tfrac83\right) \](1)
(e) foot of perpendicular to a LINE — dot with the direction
\(P\) on \(l_2\): \( \overrightarrow{OP}=(3+s,4+s,2-2s) \); impose \( \overrightarrow{AP}\perp\mathbf d_2 \). \[ (2+s,\,2+s,\,-1-2s)\cdot(1,1,-2)=0\ \Rightarrow\ 6s+6=0\ \Rightarrow\ s=-1 \](1)(1) \[ \overrightarrow{OP}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\\4\end{pmatrix} \](1)
(f) foot of perpendicular to a PLANE — travel from \(A\) along the normal
The foot \(N_1\) lies on the line through \(A\) in the direction of \( \mathbf n \); substitute that line into \( \Pi \). \[ \mathbf r=(1-\lambda,\,2+2\lambda,\,3+2\lambda);\quad (1-\lambda,2+2\lambda,3+2\lambda)\cdot(-1,2,2)=2\ \Rightarrow\ 9\lambda+9=2\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda=-\tfrac79 \](1)(1) \[ \overrightarrow{ON_1}=\tfrac19\begin{pmatrix}16\\4\\13\end{pmatrix} \](1)
This is the whole method-selection test in one question. Line versus plane are different procedures:
• To a PLANE — distance \( =|\overrightarrow{BA}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}| \); foot \( =A+\lambda\mathbf n \), then substitute into the plane.
• To a LINE — distance \( =|\overrightarrow{BA}\times\hat{\mathbf d}| \); foot \( =\mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf d \), then impose \( \overrightarrow{AF}\cdot\mathbf d=0 \).
Key note from the school key: the distance from \(A\) to \(l_1\) is not generally the same as the distance from \(A\) to \( \Pi \), even when \(l_1\) lies in \( \Pi \) — here \( \tfrac73 \) versus \( \sqrt{11/2} \). They coincide only if the line passes through the foot of the perpendicular.
Tests: showing a line lies in a plane; point-to-plane and point-to-line distance; line-plane intersection; and both feet of perpendicular — all six interleaved so you must pick the method each time.

Question 6 [9]
ACJC 2023 Promo Q6 · HCI Supplementary Q1
Referred to the origin \(O\), the points \(P\) and \(Q\) have position vectors \( \mathbf p \) and \( \mathbf q \) respectively, where \( \mathbf p \) and \( \mathbf q \) are not parallel. Point \(A\) lies between \(P\) and \(Q\), such that \( PA:AQ=\lambda:(1-\lambda) \), where \( 0<\lambda<1 \). (i) Express \( \overrightarrow{OA} \) in terms of \( \mathbf p \), \( \mathbf q \) and \( \lambda \). [1] (ii) The point \(B\) lies between \(O\) and \(Q\), such that \( OB:OQ=1:3 \). Given that \(AB\) is parallel to \( (\mathbf q-4\mathbf p) \), find the value of \( \lambda \). [4] (iii) It is now given that \( \mathbf p \) is a unit vector which is perpendicular to \( \mathbf q \), and \( |\mathbf q|=2 \). Find the area of triangle \(OAQ\), in terms of \( \lambda \). [4]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) ratio theorem with a parameter
\(PA:AQ=\lambda:(1-\lambda)\), and the two parts sum to \(1\), so the denominator is \(1\). \[ \overrightarrow{OA}=\frac{(1-\lambda)\mathbf p+\lambda\mathbf q}{\lambda+(1-\lambda)}=(1-\lambda)\mathbf p+\lambda\mathbf q \](1)
(ii) parallel means "a scalar multiple of"
\( \overrightarrow{OB}=\tfrac13\mathbf q \). Then \( \overrightarrow{AB}=\overrightarrow{OB}-\overrightarrow{OA} \). \[ \overrightarrow{AB}=\tfrac13\mathbf q-(1-\lambda)\mathbf p-\lambda\mathbf q=-(1-\lambda)\mathbf p+\left(\tfrac13-\lambda\right)\mathbf q \](1) \( AB \parallel (\mathbf q-4\mathbf p) \) means \( \overrightarrow{AB}=m(\mathbf q-4\mathbf p) \) for some scalar \(m\). Since \( \mathbf p \) and \( \mathbf q \) are not parallel, their coefficients may be compared. \[ \mathbf p:\ -(1-\lambda)=-4m \qquad \mathbf q:\ \tfrac13-\lambda=m \](1) \[ 1-\lambda=4\left(\tfrac13-\lambda\right)\ \Rightarrow\ 1-\lambda=\tfrac43-4\lambda\ \Rightarrow\ 3\lambda=\tfrac13\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda=\tfrac19 \](1)(1)
(iii) area via a vector product, using the given magnitudes
\( \text{Area}=\tfrac12\left|\overrightarrow{OA}\times\overrightarrow{OQ}\right| \) with \( \overrightarrow{OQ}=\mathbf q \). \[ \overrightarrow{OA}\times\mathbf q=\big[(1-\lambda)\mathbf p+\lambda\mathbf q\big]\times\mathbf q=(1-\lambda)(\mathbf p\times\mathbf q)+\lambda(\mathbf q\times\mathbf q) \](1) \( \mathbf q\times\mathbf q=\mathbf 0 \). And \( |\mathbf p\times\mathbf q|=|\mathbf p||\mathbf q|\sin 90^\circ=(1)(2)(1)=2 \) since \( \mathbf p\perp\mathbf q \) and \( |\mathbf p|=1 \). \[ \text{Area}=\tfrac12\left|(1-\lambda)\right|\left|\mathbf p\times\mathbf q\right|=\tfrac12(1-\lambda)(2)=1-\lambda\ \text{units}^2 \](1)(1)(1) (\(1-\lambda>0\) because \(0<\lambda<1\), so the modulus can be dropped.)
Tests: ratio theorem in parametric form; using "parallel" to compare coefficients of two non-parallel vectors; expanding a vector product with \( \mathbf q\times\mathbf q=\mathbf 0 \) and evaluating \( |\mathbf p\times\mathbf q| \) from magnitudes and the angle.

Question 7 [3]
A-Level N2000 P1 Q3 · HCI Tutorial 7B Q3(a)
Equations of two lines are given below: \( l_1:\ \mathbf r=-2\mathbf i+\mathbf j+9\mathbf k+\lambda(2\mathbf i+5\mathbf j+4\mathbf k) \), \( \lambda\in\mathbb R \); \( l_2:\ \mathbf r=11\mathbf i+8\mathbf j+3\mathbf k+\mu(3\mathbf i-\mathbf j+5\mathbf k) \), \( \mu\in\mathbb R \). Determine if the lines are parallel, intersecting or skew, giving a reason for your answer. [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
step 1 — test parallel first
\( (2,5,4)\neq k(3,-1,5) \) for any scalar \(k\). \[ \therefore\ \text{the two lines are not parallel.} \](1)
step 2 — assume they intersect and look for a contradiction
\[ -2+2\lambda=11+3\mu\ \cdots(1);\qquad 1+5\lambda=8-\mu\ \cdots(2);\qquad 9+4\lambda=3+5\mu\ \cdots(3) \] Solve any two, then test in the third. From (1) and (2): \( \lambda=2,\ \mu=-3 \). \[ \text{In (3): LHS}=9+8=17,\quad \text{RHS}=3-15=-12,\quad 17\neq-12 \](1) \[ \therefore\ \text{the two lines do not intersect.} \]
step 3 — conclude
\[ \text{Non-parallel and non-intersecting}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{the lines are \textbf{skew}.} \](1)
Both halves are needed. "No solution" on its own does not prove skew — parallel lines also fail to intersect. You must state non-parallel and non-intersecting. Always solve two equations and check in the third.
Tests: classifying two lines as parallel, intersecting or skew — the two-part argument and the consistency check in the unused equation.

Question 8 [12]
A-Level 2009 P1 Q10
The planes \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) have equations \( \mathbf r\cdot(2\mathbf i+\mathbf j+3\mathbf k)=1 \) and \( \mathbf r\cdot(-\mathbf i+2\mathbf j+\mathbf k)=2 \) respectively, and meet in a line \(l\). (i) Find the acute angle between \(p_1\) and \(p_2\). [3] (ii) Find a vector equation of \(l\). [4] (iii) The plane \(p_3\) has equation \( 2x+y+3z-1+k(-x+2y+z-2)=0 \). Explain why \(l\) lies in \(p_3\) for any constant \(k\). Hence, or otherwise, find a Cartesian equation of the plane in which both \(l\) and the point \( (2,3,4) \) lie. [5]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) angle between two planes = angle between their normals
\( \mathbf n_1=(2,1,3) \), \( \mathbf n_2=(-1,2,1) \); modulus for the acute angle. \[ \cos\theta=\frac{\left|(2,1,3)\cdot(-1,2,1)\right|}{\sqrt{14}\,\sqrt6}=\frac{3}{\sqrt{84}} \](1)(1) \[ \theta=70.9^\circ\ \text{(1 d.p.)} \](1)
(ii) line of intersection — direction by cross product, point by inspection
The line lies in both planes, so it is perpendicular to both normals; that is exactly what \( \mathbf n_1\times\mathbf n_2 \) gives. \[ \mathbf n_1\times\mathbf n_2=(2,1,3)\times(-1,2,1)=(-5,-5,5)=-5(1,1,-1) \](1)(1) A point on both: try \( (0,1,0) \) — \( (0,1,0)\cdot(2,1,3)=1 \) ✓ and \( (0,1,0)\cdot(-1,2,1)=2 \) ✓. \[ l:\ \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb R \](1)(1)
(iii) the pencil of planes through \(l\)
Every point of \(l\) lies in both planes, so it satisfies \( 2x+y+3z-1=0 \) and \( -x+2y+z-2=0 \) simultaneously. \[ \text{Then}\quad 0+k(0)=0\quad\text{for any }k, \] so every point of \(l\) satisfies the equation of \(p_3\) whatever \(k\) is. \[ \therefore\ l\ \text{lies in}\ p_3\ \text{for all }k. \](1)(1) Now choose \(k\) so that \( (2,3,4) \) also satisfies it. \[ (4+3+12-1)+k(-2+6+4-2)=0\ \Rightarrow\ 18+6k=0\ \Rightarrow\ k=-3 \](1) \[ 2x+y+3z-1-3(-x+2y+z-2)=0\ \Rightarrow\ 5x-5y+5=0 \](1) \[ \therefore\ x-y+1=0 \](1)
Why you may fix a coordinate to find a point on \(l\): the line of intersection is infinite, so setting one coordinate to a convenient value just picks out the single point where the line meets that coordinate plane. Any consistent choice lands on the same line — only the "starting point" \( \mathbf a \) in \( \mathbf r=\mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf d \) changes, and that is not unique anyway.
Tests: angle between two planes; line of intersection of two planes (cross-product direction plus a point); and the pencil-of-planes trick for the plane through a given line and a given point.

Question 9 [9]
MJC 2009 Prelim P1 Q5 · HCI Supplementary Q12
With respect to an origin \(O\), the points \(A\) and \(B\) have position vectors \( \mathbf a \) and \( \mathbf b \) respectively, where \( \mathbf a \) and \( \mathbf b \) are not parallel. (i) It is given that \(B\) lies on the line segment \(AC\), such that \( \overrightarrow{BC}=2\mathbf b-k\mathbf a \). State the value of the constant \(k\). Hence find \( \overrightarrow{OC} \) in terms of \( \mathbf a \) and \( \mathbf b \). [2] (ii) \( \overrightarrow{ON} \) is the projection vector of \( \overrightarrow{OB} \) onto \( \overrightarrow{OA} \), where \( \overrightarrow{ON}=\tfrac34\mathbf a \) and \( \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=4 \). Show that the length of \(OA\) is \( \dfrac{4\sqrt3}{3} \). [2] (iii) Given that \( \mathbf b=2\mathbf i-2\mathbf j+2\mathbf k \), find the angle between \(OA\) and \(OB\). [2] (iv) Given that \(OADC\) is a parallelogram and \(E\) lies on the line segment \(AC\) such that \( AE:AC=2:3 \), find \( \overrightarrow{DE} \). [3]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(i) \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) collinear
\( \overrightarrow{AB}=\mathbf b-\mathbf a \). Since \(B\) lies on \(AC\), \( \overrightarrow{BC} \) must be parallel to \( \overrightarrow{AB} \); \( 2\mathbf b-k\mathbf a=2(\mathbf b-\mathbf a) \) requires \(k=2\). \[ k=2 \](1) \[ \overrightarrow{OC}=\overrightarrow{OB}+\overrightarrow{BC}=\mathbf b+2\mathbf b-2\mathbf a=3\mathbf b-2\mathbf a \](1)
(ii) projection VECTOR — and what its coefficient tells you
The projection vector of \( \mathbf b \) onto \( \mathbf a \) is \( \left(\mathbf b\cdot\hat{\mathbf a}\right)\hat{\mathbf a}=\dfrac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{|\mathbf a|^2}\,\mathbf a \). \[ \frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{|\mathbf a|^2}\,\mathbf a=\tfrac34\mathbf a\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{4}{|\mathbf a|^2}=\frac34\ \Rightarrow\ |\mathbf a|^2=\frac{16}{3} \](1) \[ |\mathbf a|=\frac{4}{\sqrt3}=\frac{4\sqrt3}{3}\ \text{(shown)} \](1)
(iii) angle between two vectors
\( |\mathbf b|=\sqrt{4+4+4}=2\sqrt3 \), and \( \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=4 \), \( |\mathbf a|=\tfrac{4}{\sqrt3} \). \[ \cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|}=\frac{4}{\left(\tfrac{4}{\sqrt3}\right)\left(2\sqrt3\right)}=\frac48=\frac12 \](1) \[ \theta=60^\circ \](1)
(iv) work round the parallelogram
In parallelogram \(OADC\) the vertices are in order, so \( \overrightarrow{OD}=\overrightarrow{OA}+\overrightarrow{OC}=\mathbf a+(3\mathbf b-2\mathbf a)=3\mathbf b-\mathbf a \). \( \overrightarrow{AC}=\overrightarrow{OC}-\overrightarrow{OA}=3\mathbf b-3\mathbf a \), and \( \overrightarrow{AE}=\tfrac23\overrightarrow{AC}=2\mathbf b-2\mathbf a \), so \( \overrightarrow{OE}=\mathbf a+2\mathbf b-2\mathbf a=2\mathbf b-\mathbf a \).(1)(1) \[ \overrightarrow{DE}=\overrightarrow{OE}-\overrightarrow{OD}=(2\mathbf b-\mathbf a)-(3\mathbf b-\mathbf a)=-\mathbf b=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\2\\-2\end{pmatrix} \](1)
Scalar vs vector projection: the projection vector is \( (\mathbf b\cdot\hat{\mathbf a})\hat{\mathbf a} \) — a vector, and it keeps its sign so it carries direction. The length of projection is \( |\mathbf b\cdot\hat{\mathbf a}| \) — a number, with a modulus. Part (ii) only works because you compare the vector form coefficient with \( \tfrac34 \).

[2]
Before you reveal — say it out loud:  Confidence:  high  /  medium  /  guessed
Solution
(a) plane through the origin containing a line
Both \(O\) and the point \( (2,0,-1) \) lie in the plane, so \( (2,0,-1) \) is a direction in the plane; the line's own direction \( (3,4,-1) \) is the second. \[ \text{Parametric: }\ \mathbf r=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}+\mu\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\\-1\end{pmatrix},\quad\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb R \](1) \[ \mathbf n=\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\\-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\-1\\8\end{pmatrix};\qquad \text{Scalar product: }\ \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}4\\-1\\8\end{pmatrix}=0 \](1) The right-hand side is \(0\) because the plane passes through the origin.
(b) point and normal — then run it backwards
\[ \text{Scalar product: }\ \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix}=2+1-1=2 \](1) For the parametric form you need two non-parallel vectors perpendicular to the normal, i.e. any \( \mathbf v \) with \( \mathbf v\cdot(1,-1,1)=0 \). By inspection \( (1,1,0) \) and \( (0,1,1) \) both work. \[ \text{Parametric: }\ \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}+\mu\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb R \](1)

The given plane \( 2x+3y+4z=5 \) has normal \( (2,3,4) \). If our plane is perpendicular to it, then that normal lies inside our plane — so it serves as a direction vector. The second direction is the line's own \( (-1,2,-1) \). \[ \text{Parametric: }\ \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\-1\end{pmatrix}+\mu\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\\4\end{pmatrix},\quad\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb R \](1) \[ \mathbf n=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\-1\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\\4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}11\\2\\-7\end{pmatrix};\quad \mathbf r\cdot\begin{pmatrix}11\\2\\-7\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}11\\2\\-7\end{pmatrix}=6 \](1)
The recognition to lock: "plane perpendicular to plane \( \Pi \)" \( \Rightarrow \) \( \Pi \)'s normal is a direction inside your plane. Solution
(i) direction from two points, then simplify it
\[ \overrightarrow{AB}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-8\\1\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}7\\8\\9\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-8\\-16\\-8\end{pmatrix}=-8\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\1\end{pmatrix} \](1)(1) \[ l:\ \mathbf r=\begin{pmatrix}7\\8\\9\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb R \](1)

Solution
(i) plane through three points — two vectors from the SAME point
Call the points \(A(4,-1,-3)\), \(B(-2,-5,2)\), \(C(4,-3,-2)\).
