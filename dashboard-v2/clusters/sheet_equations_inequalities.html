1Rational inequalities — the sign-diagram method
The procedure that earns “algebraically” marks
When a question says “without using a calculator” or “solve algebraically”, a GC answer scores zero. Use this:

1. Move everything to one side so you have \(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\ \square\ 0\). Never cross-multiply — you do not know the sign of the denominator, so the inequality may flip.
2. Combine into a single fraction over a common denominator.
3. Factorise the numerator and denominator fully.
4. Mark every critical value (zeros of numerator and denominator) on a number line and build a sign diagram.
5. Read off the intervals. Zeros of the denominator are always excluded, even when the inequality is \(\ge\) or \(\le\).
A useful factorisation
A difference of two squares appears constantly here:
\((x-1)^2-(x-2)^2=\big[(x-1)-(x-2)\big]\big[(x-1)+(x-2)\big]=(1)(2x-3)=2x-3\).
Spotting this turns a messy quadratic expansion into a linear factor.
Example
Solve algebraically \(\dfrac{x-1}{x-2}\ \ge\ \dfrac{x-2}{x-1}\).

\(\dfrac{x-1}{x-2}-\dfrac{x-2}{x-1}\ge 0\)
\(\dfrac{(x-1)^2-(x-2)^2}{(x-2)(x-1)}\ge 0\)
\(\dfrac{\big[(x-1)-(x-2)\big]\big[(x-1)+(x-2)\big]}{(x-2)(x-1)}\ge 0\)
\(\dfrac{2x-3}{(x-2)(x-1)}\ge 0\)

Critical values \(1,\ \tfrac32,\ 2\). Sign diagram gives \(-\ +\ -\ +\) across the four intervals.
Answer: \(12\).
\(x=\tfrac32\) is included (numerator zero); \(x=1\) and \(x=2\) are excluded (denominator zero).
Trap Cross-multiplying. \(\dfrac{a}{b}\ge\dfrac{c}{d}\) does not give \(ad\ge bc\) unless you know \(bd>0\). It is the single most common way to lose all the marks on this question type.
Practice — do this before revealing
(i) Solve algebraically the inequality \(\dfrac{x-1}{x-2}\ \ge\ \dfrac{x-2}{x-1}\).
(ii) Given that \(\mathrm{f}(x)=\dfrac{x-1}{x-2}\), state the value of \(a\) such that \(\mathrm{f}(x-a)=\dfrac{2x-3}{2x-5}\).
(iii) Hence solve \(\dfrac{2x-3}{2x-5}\ \ge\ \dfrac{2x-5}{2x-3}\).
HCI 2022 Prelim Paper 1 Q1 · [3] [1] [2] · answer verbatim from the HCI mark scheme, re-verified symbolically
(i) As the example: \(\dfrac{2x-3}{(x-2)(x-1)}\ge0\), giving \(\boxed{12}\).

(ii) Write \(\mathrm{f}(x)=\dfrac{x-1}{x-2}=1+\dfrac{1}{x-2}\). Replacing \(x\) by \(x-a\):
\(\mathrm{f}(x-a)=1+\dfrac{1}{x-a-2}\). We need \(\dfrac{2x-3}{2x-5}=1+\dfrac{2}{2x-5}=1+\dfrac{1}{x-\frac52}\).
Matching, \(a+2=\tfrac52\), so \(\boxed{a=\tfrac12}\).

(iii) The new inequality is the old one with \(x\) replaced by \(x-\tfrac12\). So take the answer to (i) and shift it:
\(12\ \Rightarrow\ x>\tfrac52\).
\(\tfrac32\tfrac52\).

The move to internalise: “Hence” means do not start again. Identify the substitution, then translate your existing solution set through it. Re-solving from scratch usually costs more time than the whole question is worth.
2The “hence / deduce” pattern
How the second half of these questions works
Part (i) gives you a solved inequality in \(x\). Part (ii) presents a disguised copy of it and says hence or deduce.

Step 1. Identify what has replaced \(x\). Common substitutions: \(x\to x-a\), \(x\to \sqrt x\), \(x\to e^x\), \(x\to \ln x\), \(x\to \cos x\), \(x\to\dfrac1x\).
Step 2. Write your part (i) answer with that expression in place of \(x\).
Step 3. Solve each resulting piece, respecting the range of the substitution. This is where the marks are:
• \(\sqrt x\ge 0\) and \(e^x>0\) — any branch demanding a negative value is rejected;
• \(-1\le\cos x\le 1\) — same;
• a stated domain such as \(-\pi
Example
Given that \(x<-2\) or \(x>3\) solves an inequality in \(x\), deduce the solution of the same inequality with \(x\) replaced by \(e^{2t}\).

Replace: \(e^{2t}<-2\) or \(e^{2t}>3\).
\(e^{2t}<-2\) is impossible because \(e^{2t}>0\) always — reject that branch and say so.
\(e^{2t}>3\Rightarrow 2t>\ln 3\Rightarrow t>\tfrac12\ln 3\).
Answer: \(t>\tfrac12\ln 3\).
The rejected branch is worth a mark on its own if you state why it is rejected.
Trap Carrying a branch through without checking the substitution's range. If the substituted expression can never be negative, the branch demanding it is negative must be explicitly discarded, not silently dropped.

3Modulus inequalities
The two relations, and what they mean
\(|x-a| — “within \(b\) of \(a\)”, one interval.
\(|x-a|>b \iff xa+b\) — “further than \(b\) from \(a\)”, two pieces.

Read \(|x-a|\) as the distance from \(x\) to \(a\). That reading makes both relations obvious and stops you memorising them backwards.

For a modulus on both sides, square both sides — legitimate because both are non-negative:
\(|A|\ge|B|\iff A^2\ge B^2\iff A^2-B^2\ge0\iff (A-B)(A+B)\ge 0\).
Example
Solve \(|2x-1|\le|x+3|\).

Both sides non-negative, so square:
\((2x-1)^2\le(x+3)^2\Rightarrow (2x-1)^2-(x+3)^2\le0\).
Difference of squares: \(\big[(2x-1)-(x+3)\big]\big[(2x-1)+(x+3)\big]\le0\)
\((x-4)(3x+2)\le 0\).
Critical values \(-\tfrac23\) and \(4\), and the parabola opens upwards, so the expression is \(\le0\) between them.
Answer: \(-\tfrac23\le x\le 4\).
Trap Squaring when one side can be negative. \(|A|\ge B\) cannot be squared if \(B\) might be negative — split into cases instead. Squaring is only safe when both sides are non-negative, which is guaranteed when both carry a modulus.
Practice — do this before revealing
Find the exact solution of the inequality \(\left|\dfrac{2x-1}{x+2}\right|\le 1\).
Built on the exact-solution modulus inequality in HCI 2020 Prelim Paper 1
\(\left|\dfrac{2x-1}{x+2}\right|\le1 \iff |2x-1|\le|x+2|\) (valid for \(x\ne-2\); multiplying by \(|x+2|>0\) is safe).
Square both sides:
\((2x-1)^2\le(x+2)^2\Rightarrow (2x-1)^2-(x+2)^2\le0\)
\(\big[(2x-1)-(x+2)\big]\big[(2x-1)+(x+2)\big]\le0\)
\((x-3)(3x+1)\le 0\).
Upward parabola, roots \(-\tfrac13\) and \(3\), so the solution is between them.
\(-\dfrac13\le x\le 3\), and \(x=-2\) is not in this interval so no exclusion is needed.

Check the endpoints: at \(x=3\), \(\left|\dfrac{5}{5}\right|=1\) ✓. At \(x=-\tfrac13\), \(\left|\dfrac{-\frac53}{\frac53}\right|=1\) ✓. Both satisfy \(\le\), so both are included.
4Systems of equations, and graphical methods
Forming and solving systems
Real-world stems (pricing, mixtures, investments across accounts) become a system of linear equations. Define your variables explicitly in words first — that is often a mark.

On the TI-84: APPS → 5:PlySmlt2 → 2:SIMULT EQN SOLVER. Set the number of equations and unknowns, key the coefficient matrix, then SOLVE.
For a polynomial equation use PlySmlt2 → 1:POLY ROOT FINDER.

When the question does not forbid a calculator, use it — but if the answer must be exact, do the algebra.
Graphical method
To solve \(f(x)>g(x)\) graphically: plot both, find the intersection \(x\)-values, and read off where \(f\) lies above \(g\).

State the intersection values to 3 significant figures unless exact form is demanded, and write the answer as an interval in \(x\) — not as a list of intersection points.
Example
A shop sells three drink sizes. Two smalls and one large cost $8.10; one small and three larges cost $13.30; three smalls and two larges cost $14.90. Are these consistent?

Let \(s\) and \(l\) be the prices. The three statements give:
\(2s+l=8.10\), \(s+3l=13.30\), \(3s+2l=14.90\).
From the first two: \(s=2.36\), \(l=3.38\).
Check in the third: \(3(2.36)+2(3.38)=7.08+6.76=13.84\ne 14.90\).
The system is inconsistent — no pair of prices satisfies all three.

That consistency check is the whole point of a three-equations-two-unknowns question.
Trap With three equations and two unknowns, solving two of them and stopping. The third equation is the question — substitute back and state explicitly whether it is satisfied.
Practice — do this before revealing
The curve \(y=\dfrac{x^2+1}{x-1}\) and the line \(y=2x+k\) intersect at exactly one point. Using a graphical or algebraic method, find the possible values of \(k\).
Discriminant condition on an intersection — the standard 9758 pairing of Topic 1.3 with curve sketching
Set them equal: \(\dfrac{x^2+1}{x-1}=2x+k\).
Multiply by \((x-1)\), valid since \(x\ne1\):
\(x^2+1=(2x+k)(x-1)=2x^2+kx-2x-k\)
\(0=x^2+(k-2)x-(k+1)\).

Exactly one intersection means this quadratic has a repeated root, so the discriminant is zero:
\((k-2)^2+4(k+1)=0\)
\(k^2-4k+4+4k+4=0\)
\(k^2+8=0\).
This has no real solution, so there is no value of \(k\) giving a repeated root.

The real answer needs the other case. A line can also meet this curve exactly once if one of the two roots is \(x=1\), which the curve excludes. Put \(x=1\) into the quadratic: \(1+(k-2)-(k+1)=-2\ne0\), so that never happens either.
Conclusion: no value of \(k\) works — every line \(y=2x+k\) meets this curve either twice or not at all.

Why this question is worth doing: “exactly one intersection” is not always “discriminant zero”. With a rational curve you must also check whether a root has been lost to an excluded \(x\)-value.
