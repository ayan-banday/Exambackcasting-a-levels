A · Vectors — planes, lines, distances

← situation · formula →
Angle between two planes$$\cos\theta=\frac{|\,\mathbf{n_1}\cdot\mathbf{n_2}\,|}{|\mathbf{n_1}|\,|\mathbf{n_2}|}$$
Angle between a line and a plane$$\sin\theta=\frac{|\,\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,|}{|\mathbf{d}|\,|\mathbf{n}|}$$ (sin, not cos — the line's direction vs the normal)
Line of intersection of two planesDirection $\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{n_1}\times\mathbf{n_2}$. Then set one coordinate (e.g. $z=0$) and solve the two plane equations for a point on the line.
Distance from point $A$ to plane $\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n}=d$$$\frac{|\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{n}-d\,|}{|\mathbf{n}|}$$
Distance from point $A$ to a line $\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{b}+\lambda\mathbf{d}$$$\frac{|\,\overrightarrow{AB}\times\mathbf{d}\,|}{|\mathbf{d}|}$$

Whenever a question says "distance", reach for a normal (planes) or a direction vector (lines) — it's always one of these two.

Cartesian ↔ vector conversion (the one no one showed you)

Cartesian line → vector
$\dfrac{x-a}{l}=\dfrac{y-b}{m}=\dfrac{z-c}{n}$		$$\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\\c\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}l\\m\\n\end{pmatrix}$$ The numbers you subtract are the point; the denominators are the direction.
Cartesian plane → vector / normal
$ax+by+cz=d$		normal $\mathbf{n}=\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\\c\end{pmatrix}$, and $\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n}=d$.
Vector plane → Cartesian
$\mathbf{r}\cdot(a,b,c)=d$		just write $ax+by+cz=d$.

Worked check: the line \((x-1)/2=(y+1)/{-1}=(z-3)/4\) is \(\mathbf{r}=(1,-1,3)+\lambda(2,-1,4)\). Sign of the point flips out of the bracket; the direction is read straight off.

B · Integration — trig powers & standard forms

Powers of secant

  1. Odd power (\(\int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta\)): cyclic IBP. Let \(I=\int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta\); take \(u=\sec\theta,\ dv=\sec^2\theta\,d\theta\Rightarrow v=\tan\theta\). You get \(I=\sec\theta\tan\theta-\int\sec\theta\tan^2\theta\,d\theta\); replace \(\tan^2=\sec^2-1\); the \(\int\sec^3\) reappears \(=I\); solve for \(I\).
  2. Even power (\(\int\sec^4\theta\,d\theta\)): split off \(\sec^2\theta\), use \(\sec^2=1+\tan^2\), then substitute \(u=\tan\theta\ (du=\sec^2\theta\,d\theta)\). So \(\int(1+u^2)\,du\).

MF27 standard forms — know these on sight

$$\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}\,dx=\sin^{-1}\!\frac{x}{a}$$$$\int\frac{1}{a^2+x^2}\,dx=\frac{1}{a}\tan^{-1}\!\frac{x}{a}$$
$$\int\frac{1}{a^2-x^2}\,dx=\frac{1}{2a}\ln\left|\frac{a+x}{a-x}\right|$$$$\int\frac{1}{x^2-a^2}\,dx=\frac{1}{2a}\ln\left|\frac{x-a}{x+a}\right|$$
The MF27 reflex: can't integrate directly → try a substitution / by parts → still stuck → it's a standard form in MF27. You lost a whole part because you didn't spot \(1/\sqrt{4-x^2}\) was \(\sin^{-1}(x/2)\). Scan MF27 before forcing it.

C · Discipline — the careless leaks

1. Change the limits. Definite integral + substitution → convert the \(x\)-limits into the new variable's limits immediately. You flagged this twice yourself.

2. Calculator in RADIANS. Any inverse-trig or exact-area answer. The degrees slip turned \(\pi/3\) into "60". Set it once at the start.

3. "Show that" a relation → differentiate the relation, not the mess. Find \(dy/dx\), form a clean relation containing \(y\), then differentiate that again. Never differentiate the ugly explicit form twice.
