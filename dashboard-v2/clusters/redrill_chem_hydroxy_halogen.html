(b) The mechanism involves two steps: step 1, protonation of the \(\ce{-OH}\) group of methanol by HI; step 2, attack of the intermediate by iodide ion. Draw the mechanism, showing lone pairs, dipoles, curly arrows, charges and the intermediate. [2]
HCI Prelim · N2025 · P3 · Q4(b)(ii)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
Mechanism — one mark per correct step.
  • Step 1: a lone pair on the –OH oxygen of \(\ce{CH3OH}\) attacks the δ+ H of H–I (curly arrow from O to H; curly arrow from the H–I bond to I), giving the protonated alcohol (oxonium) intermediate \(\ce{CH3-O+H2}\) and \(\ce{I-}\) 1
  • Step 2: a lone pair on \(\ce{I-}\) attacks the δ+ carbon of \(\ce{CH3-O+H2}\) (curly arrow from \(\ce{I-}\) to C; curly arrow from the C–O bond to O, expelling \(\ce{H2O}\) as the good leaving group), forming \(\ce{CH3I + H2O}\) 1
This is the whole point of the question. \(\ce{-OH}\) is a terrible leaving group — hydroxide never just walks off a carbon. Every alcohol-to-halogenoalkane route exists to fix that: HX protonates the oxygen so the leaving group becomes neutral water; \(\ce{PCl5}\), \(\ce{PCl3}\) and \(\ce{SOCl2}\) convert \(\ce{-OH}\) into a phosphorus- or sulfur-based leaving group instead. Reject conc H₃PO₄ — that is the dehydration reagent and gives you an alkene, not a halogenoalkane.

HCI Soln · N2022 · P3 · Q4(f)(i)  ·  stem reconstructed from the key
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
SN2 on a primary halogenoalkane.

Two things you dropped last time, and they are separate marks. (1) Name the attacking species. With \(\ce{NaCN}\) or \(\ce{NaOH}\) the \(\ce{Na+}\) is a spectator ion — it takes no part in the mechanism and must never appear in it. 3   What actually sets the rateTotal: 10
(a) Three primary halogenoalkanes M, N and O react with NaOH. N contains a C–I bond; M and O both contain C–Br; O has a highly branched, bulky alkyl group. Arrange them in order of increasing rate of hydrolysis and explain your order. [3]
HCI Soln · N2022 · P3 · Q4(f)(iii)  ·  stem reconstructed from the key
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • The relative rate increases from O to M to N (i.e. O < M < N) 1
  • All three are primary halogenoalkanes undergoing nucleophilic substitution via \(\mathrm{S_N}2\). The C–I bond in N is weaker than the C–Br bond in M and O, so N has a lower activation energy and the fastest rate 1
  • The highly branched alkyl group in O is bulkier than in M, causing greater steric hindrance for nucleophilic attack, so O has the slowest rate 1
Bond energy, not electronegativity. Reject "iodine is less electronegative so the carbon is less δ+" — that argument predicts the wrong order. Quote Data Booklet bond energies if you want the mark nailed down.

HCI Prelim · N2023 · P2 · Q2(f)(i)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
The acyl chloride Cl (Cl–C=O) is far more reactive than the alkyl Cl (\(\ce{CH2-Cl}\)) toward nucleophiles.

Say "electron-deficient", not "electron-donating/withdrawing" in the abstract. The whole comparison is about how δ+ the attacked carbon is. "It is dibasic so it dissociates twice" earns nothing. Having two acidic protons tells you the acid has two \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) values; it does not tell you whether the first one is lower. 5   Phenol: the ranking, the sodium experiment, and the ringTotal: 11
(a) Write an equation for the reaction of hydroquinone (benzene-1,4-diol) with excess \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\). [1]
HCI Soln · N2020 · P2 · Q4(a)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
\(\ce{HO-C6H4-OH + 2OH^{-} -> ^{-}O-C6H4-O^{-} + 2H2O}\) 1
Phenol is acidic, hence in excess strong base \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\) both phenolic groups are deprotonated.
The ladder, cold. An alcohol does not react with \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\) at all — it is a weaker acid than water. A phenol does react with \(\ce{NaOH}\), but not with \(\ce{Na2CO3}\)/\(\ce{NaHCO3}\). A carboxylic acid reacts with both. Sodium metal is the odd one out: it reacts with all three, because it only needs an O–H, not an acid stronger than water — which is exactly why Na is the reagent of choice for detecting \(\ce{-OH}\).
(b) Describe an experiment to compare the acidity of hydroquinone, phenol and ethanol, and explain the order of acidity. [4]
HCI Soln · N2020 · P2 · Q4(b)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • Add Na metal to each and record the time taken for a fixed volume (e.g. 10 \(\ce{cm^3}\)) of \(\ce{H2}\) gas to be collected for each compound 1
  • Since acidity increases from ethanol to hydroquinone to phenol, the time taken is shortest for phenol and longest for ethanol (rate fastest for phenol, slowest for ethanol) 1
  • The electron-donating alkyl group intensifies the negative charge on the oxygen of the ethoxide ion, so ethanol is the weakest acid 1

Note from the key: you cannot differentiate ethanol from phenol by the volume of gas collected, as both give the same volume (0.010/2 × 24000 = 120 \(\ce{cm^3}\)), so collecting 10 \(\ce{cm^3}\) is feasible. The discriminator is time, not volume.

You ranked this backwards last time, confidently. The order is phenol > hydroquinone > ethanol. The trap is assuming a second \(\ce{-OH}\) on the ring must withdraw. It does not — an \(\ce{-OH}\) attached to a benzene ring is electron-DONATING into the ring (its O lone pair delocalises in), which pushes charge back onto the phenoxide O and destabilises it. Second trap: this is an experimental-design question. The design is Na metal, effervescence of \(\ce{H2}\), and a timed fixed volume of gas — which is also how you quantify \(\ce{-OH}\) groups, since each \(\ce{-OH}\) gives ½ mol \(\ce{H2}\).
(c) Explain why phenol undergoes chlorination more readily than methylbenzene. [2]
HCI Soln · N2022 · P3 · Q1(b)(ii)  ·  also Cambridge N2022 P3 Q1(b)(ii)
Confidence: high / medium / guessed
  • In phenol, the lone pair of electrons on the O atom delocalises into the benzene ring, thereby greatly increasing the electron density of the ring 1
  • In methylbenzene, the methyl group is only weakly electron-donating (inductive effect), increasing electron density to a lesser extent 1
  • Hence the ring in phenol is more activated / more electron rich, more susceptible to electrophilic attack, and undergoes chlorination more readily.
"OH is electron-donating" on its own is not enough. The mark is for naming the mechanism of the donation — the O lone pair delocalising into the ring. Note the split personality: into a benzene ring the \(\ce{-OH}\) group donates (activating, 2,4-directing); attached to an sp³ chain next to a \(\ce{-CO2H}\) it is inductively withdrawing. Same group, opposite behaviour — decide by what it is attached to.

C  butanoyl chloride + phenol

Confidence: high / medium / guessed
C 1. Phenyl butanoate is \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2C(=O)O-C6H5}\) — the acyl half comes from butanoic acid / butanoyl chloride and the O half comes from phenol.
  • A is wrong: there will be no condensation (nucleophilic acyl substitution) due to phenol being a weak nucleophile. Because the lone pair on the O of phenol is delocalised into the benzene ring, it is less available to attack the electron-deficient carboxyl carbon of the carboxylic acid.

Read an ester backwards to name it. In \(\ce{R-C(=O)-O-R'}\), the acyl side R–CO– gives the -anoate stem and the R' on the single-bonded oxygen gives the first word. So "phenyl butanoate" = phenol supplied the O, butanoyl supplied the C=O. And remember the two routes: alcohol + carboxylic acid, conc \(\ce{H2SO4}\) catalyst, heat (slow, reversible) works only for alcohols; a phenol needs the acyl chloride at room temperature, no catalyst, white fumes of HCl.
