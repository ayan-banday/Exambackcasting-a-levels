# Alkenes — Study Sheet (study this BEFORE the drill dashboard)

> **Teach-first sheet.** Understand Layer 1 + Layer 2 here, then attempt the drill (with drawn structures) on the dashboard. Reasoning is lifted from your HCI notes; key phrases are bolded. Alkenes is the **highest-yield organic mechanism topic** — the mechanism draw, Markovnikov, and the reactions table come up almost every year.

---

## Layer 1 — the four big ideas

1. **An alkene is an unsaturated hydrocarbon with a C=C double bond.** The C=C is **one σ + one π bond**; each carbon is **sp² hybridised, trigonal planar, 120°**, and the **π electron cloud lies exposed above and below the plane**.
2. **That exposed, electron-rich π cloud is why alkenes are reactive** — even though a C=C is stronger than a C–C, the π cloud **attracts electrophiles**, so the defining reaction is **electrophilic addition**.
3. **Every C=C reaction is one of three types:** **addition** (electrophilic addition of HX, X₂, Br₂(aq), water), **reduction** (H₂/Ni → alkane), or **oxidation** (mild → diol; cleavage → carbonyls/CO₂). Each has **specific reagents AND conditions** that must all be stated.
4. **Product prediction rests on two things: carbocation stability (Markovnikov)** and the **exact conditions** (which flip diol vs cleavage, and dibromide vs bromohydrin).

---

## Layer 2 — the framework

### The C=C and cis-trans isomerism
- Cis-trans needs **two things: restricted rotation about the C=C, and two different groups on each C=C carbon.** If either carbon carries two identical groups, there is no cis-trans.
- **cis has the higher boiling point:** the bond dipoles **do not cancel**, giving a net dipole and **permanent dipole–permanent dipole forces on top of dispersion**. In **trans** the dipoles **cancel**, so it is non-polar with dispersion only.
- **trans has the higher melting point:** it is **more symmetrical and straighter, so it packs better** in the lattice.

### Why alkenes react with electrophiles
The **π electron cloud is exposed on the surface of the molecule and is electron-rich**, so it **attracts electrophiles**. The weaker π bond breaks and two new σ bonds form, leaving the carbons joined by a single bond.

### The electrophilic addition mechanism (two steps)
- **Step 1 is slow and rate-determining.** The **π electrons of the C=C attack the electrophile**; the π bond breaks and the electrophile's bond undergoes **heterolytic fission**, forming a **positively charged carbocation intermediate**.
- **Step 2 is fast.** The **nucleophile** (the anion just generated) attacks the carbocation to give the stable product, fast because it is attraction between oppositely charged species.

### Carbocation stability (the examiner's favourite)
- The carbocation carbon is **positive, sp², trigonal planar, with an empty p orbital**.
- **Stability order: 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl.** **Alkyl groups are electron-donating, so they disperse the positive charge and stabilise the cation**; electron-withdrawing groups intensify the charge and destabilise it.

### Markovnikov (highest-yield)
- The mark-scheme reason: **the electrophile adds so as to form the MOST STABLE carbocation, which gives the MAJOR product.** So H⁺ adds to the **less-substituted** carbon, leaving the positive charge on the **more-substituted (more stable)** carbon. (Do **not** just quote "H adds to the carbon with more H" — explain via carbocation stability.)
- **Racemic mixture:** if the addition makes the carbocation carbon a **new chiral centre**, the **planar cation is attacked equally from both faces**, giving an **equimolar pair of enantiomers**.

### The reactions table (state all reagents + conditions + observation)

| Reaction | Reagents & conditions | Product | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Add HX | HCl / HBr / HI **(g)**, room temp | halogenoalkane (Markovnikov) | — |
| Add halogen | **Cl₂ or Br₂ in CCl₄**, room temp, **in the dark** | 1,2-dihalide | **Br₂ decolourised** |
| Bromine water | **Br₂(aq)**, room temp | **bromohydrin** (–OH + –Br on adjacent C) | **yellow-orange decolourised** |
| Hydration (industrial) | **steam, H₃PO₄ catalyst, high T & P** | alcohol (Markovnikov) | — |
| Reduction | **H₂, Ni catalyst** | alkane | — |
| Mild oxidation | **cold dilute alkaline KMnO₄** | **diol** (–OH on each C) | **purple decolourised + brown MnO₂** |
| Oxidative cleavage | **hot acidified KMnO₄** | carbonyls / acids / CO₂ | purple decolourised (+ CO₂ if terminal) |

- **Halogen addition is in the dark** because UV would trigger **free-radical substitution** of the alkyl groups instead. Non-polar Br₂ is **polarised (an induced dipole) by the π cloud** before it reacts.
- **Bromine water gives the bromohydrin, not the dibromide**, because **water is in large excess** and attacks the carbocation preferentially. The **only electrophile is Br₂**, so every product contains at least one Br.

### Oxidative cleavage — the product table (memorise)

| H atoms on that C=C carbon | Becomes |
|---|---|
| **2** (terminal =CH₂) | **CO₂ + H₂O** (lost as gas → effervescence) |
| **1** (=CHR) | **carboxylic acid, –COOH** |
| **0** (=CR₂) | **ketone, C=O** |

Use it to **locate the C=C** (work backwards from the fragments) and to **distinguish a terminal C=C** (gives CO₂ effervescence). **Alkenes are NOT oxidised by K₂Cr₂O₇** — only manganate(VII).

### Distinguishing tests
- **Br₂(aq)** decolourises, and **cold alkaline KMnO₄** decolourises with a **brown MnO₂ precipitate** — both are tests for C=C. Add the reagent **dropwise**, because in excess the colour change is masked and cannot be observed.
