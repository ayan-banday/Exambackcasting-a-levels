Reduction (\(\ce{H2}\)/Ni)\(\ce{H2}\), Ni catalyst. → alkane. Obs (closed vessel): pressure gauge needle indicates lowered pressure (less \(\ce{H2}\) gas after reaction).

Cleavage product rule=CH\(_2\) (2 H) → \(\ce{CO2}\); =CHR (1 H) → carboxylic acid; =CR\(_2\) (0 H) → ketone.
Carbocation stability3° > 2° > 1° > methyl. Verbatim: electron-donating alkyl groups stabilise the carbocation by reducing its positive charge; electron-withdrawing groups destabilise by intensifying its positive charge.

Question 1

Example — HCI Q1(a)/(b)
Consider the addition of \(\ce{HBr}\) to but-1-ene and to propene. In each case two carbocation intermediates are possible. State which is more stable and explain why, in terms of the alkyl groups present. [2]
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
More stable carbocation + reason
The one with more alkyl groups on the positive carbon. M1 identify, A1 wording
The presence of more electron-donating alkyl groups stabilises the carbocation by reducing its positive charge.
Wording: "reducing its positive charge" (or "dispersing the positive charge" — both appear in the HCI key). NOT your own "disperses/spreads." Alkyl groups are electron-donating.

Practice — HCI Q1(c)/(d), harder
Now the two alkenes carry an electron-withdrawing halogen.
(c) A chlorine sits on a carbon adjacent to the positive centre. [2]
(d) A fluorine sits one carbon further away from the positive centre. Explain the effect on carbocation stability in each case. [2]
Answer — verbatim HCI wording
(c) Chlorine
M1 A1
The presence of electron-withdrawing chlorine destabilises the other carbocation by intensifying its positive charge.
(d) Fluorine, one carbon away
M1 A1
The presence of electron-withdrawing fluorine destabilises the other carbocation even though it is one carbon away.
Why harder: (d) tests that the inductive electron-withdrawing effect still reaches through one extra carbon — the destabilisation isn't only for a directly-attached group.

Archetype
Carbocation stability — the reason phrased the HCI way.

Every Markovnikov / major-product question rests on this. "Electron-donating alkyl → reduces/disperses the + charge → stabilises"; "electron-withdrawing halogen → intensifies the + charge → destabilises," even one carbon away.

Question 2

Example — HCI Q2(a)
Hex-3-ene reacts with bromine to form 3,4-dibromohexane.
Name the mechanism and draw it fully, showing curly arrows, partial charges, the carbocation intermediate, and labelling the slow and fast steps. [4]
Solution — mechanism + the HCI mark-scheme checklist
The HCI mark scheme — "Things to take note"
This box is the exact checklist the marker uses. [4]
Describe mechanism for electrophilic addition
  • Indicate "electrophilic addition" as name of mechanism
  • Correct structures (ambiguous formula NOT acceptable)
Slow step:
  • Indicate "slow" for first step (formation of carbocation)
  • Partial charges on electrophile \(\ce{Br2}\)
  • 2 arrows for slow step (one from \(\ce{C=C}\) bond to Br, the other from \(\ce{Br-Br}\) bond to Br)
Fast step:
  • Lone pair on nucleophile \(\ce{Br^-}\)
  • 1 arrow for fast step from lone pair on \(\ce{Br^-}\) to C (not to +)
Two mark-droppers: the arrow in the fast step goes to the carbon, not to the "+" symbol; and \(\ce{Br2}\) needs the \(\delta+/\delta-\) partial charges even though it starts non-polar (the π cloud induces the dipole).

Practice — HCI Q2(b), the racemic part
3,4-dibromohexane can be formed as a pair of enantiomers.
Explain, with reference to the intermediate, why the two enantiomers are formed in equal amounts. [2]
Answer — verbatim HCI wording
Why a racemic (1:1) mixture
M1 planar cation, A1 equal chances
The carbocation is planar with respect to the electron deficient carbon, and has equal chances of being attacked from the top or bottom by the bromide ion in the second step of the reaction. Therefore, there are equal chances of producing the 2 enantiomers.
Wording: this is the exact HCI answer — "planar with respect to the electron deficient carbon," "equal chances of being attacked from the top or bottom," "equal chances of producing the 2 enantiomers." Not "equal probability from either face → racemic."

Question 3

Example — HCI Q3
But-1-ene is reacted with bromine, \(\ce{Br2}\), dissolved in methanol (\(\ce{CH3OH}\)) as solvent.
(a) Give the structures of the major and minor organic products. [2]
(b) Explain why the products form from the secondary, not the primary, carbocation, and why the methoxy product is the major one. [3]
Solution — verbatim HCI wording
(a) Products
A1 A1
Major: 1-bromo-2-methoxybutane \(\ce{(BrCH2CH(OCH3)CH2CH3)}\). Minor: 1,2-dibromobutane \(\ce{(BrCH2CHBrCH2CH3)}\). (Both have \(\ce{Br}\) on C1 — the electrophile \(\ce{Br^{\delta+}}\) added there.)
(b) Why secondary carbocation, why methoxy major
M1 stability, M1 both nucleophiles, A1 solvent excess
The secondary carbocation is more likely to be formed than the primary carbocation because it is more stable than the primary carbocation due to presence of two electron-donating alkyl groups on positive carbon than one that stabilises the carbocation by dispersing the positive charge.

Since both nucleophiles \(\ce{CH3OH}\) and \(\ce{Br^-}\) can attack the secondary carbocation intermediate, a mixture of 1-bromo-2-methoxybutane and 1,2-dibromobutane will be obtained.

1-bromo-2-methoxybutane is the major product because methanol (as a solvent) is present in a much higher concentration compared to the bromide ion and therefore attacks the intermediate preferentially in the second step of the reaction.
Why harder: two competing nucleophiles (like bromine water, but with methanol as the solvent). The "2-bromo-1-methoxybutane is not formed" proves the mechanism goes via the secondary — not primary — carbocation.

Practice — bromine water variant, harder (c)/(d)
Propene is shaken with bromine water contaminated with \(\ce{NaCl(aq)}\).
(c) Give the three organic products. [3]
(d) Explain why 1,2-dichloropropane can never be one of them. [2]
Answer
(c) Three products (Br on C1 each)
The carbocation can be attacked by \(\ce{H2O}\), \(\ce{Br^-}\), or \(\ce{Cl^-}\). A1 A1 A1
1-bromopropan-2-ol, 1,2-dibromopropane, 1-bromo-2-chloropropane.
(d) No 1,2-dichloropropane
M1 A1
The only electrophile present is \(\ce{Br2}\) (no \(\ce{Cl2}\) / \(\ce{Cl^+}\) to start the addition); \(\ce{Cl^-}\) is only a nucleophile in step 2. So every product must contain at least one \(\ce{Br}\).
Why harder: same "which nucleophile attacks the cation" logic as Q3, but the electrophile fixes the first halogen — a product with only Cl is impossible.
Answer hidden — click Reveal
Archetype
Addition where more than one nucleophile competes for the carbocation.

Electrophile (Br₂) fixes the first halogen and the more stable (2°) carbocation; the nucleophile in largest concentration (solvent methanol / excess water) gives the major product.

Question 4

Example — HCI Q4 (observations)
A terpene alkene is treated separately with each reagent below. For each, state the observation in full.
(a) \(\ce{Br2}\) in \(\ce{CCl4}\)   (b) \(\ce{Br2(aq)}\)   (c) \(\ce{H2}\)/Ni in a closed vessel   (d) cold alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\)   (e) hot acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) on a terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\). [5]
Solution — verbatim HCI observations
(a) reddish-brown liquid \(\ce{Br2}\) decolourised.
(b) yellow-orange aqueous \(\ce{Br2}\) decolourised (major product is the bromohydrin).
(c) pressure gauge needle indicates lowered pressure after a while (because after reaction, there is less hydrogen gas).
(d) purple \(\ce{KMnO4}\) decolourised and brown ppt of \(\ce{MnO2}\) formed (mild oxidation; \(\ce{MnO2}\) is formed in alkaline medium).
(e) purple \(\ce{KMnO4}\) decolourised, effervescence of (colourless and odourless) \(\ce{CO2}\) gas (which gave a white precipitate of \(\ce{CaCO3}\) when bubbled in limewater \(\ce{Ca(OH)2}\)).
Marks leak here: (a) says "liquid," (b) says "aqueous" — state the colour that matches the medium; (d) needs the brown \(\ce{MnO2}\) ppt, not just decolourisation; (e) needs the full \(\ce{CO2}\)/limewater line for the terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\).

Practice — products, harder
2-methylbut-2-ene, \(\ce{(CH3)2C=CHCH3}\), is treated with (a) cold alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\), then (b) hot acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\). Give the organic product(s) of each, with reagents/conditions. [4]
Answer
(a) Mild → diol
\(\ce{KMnO4}\), \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\), cold. B1 A1
2-methylbutane-2,3-diol, \(\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)CH(OH)CH3}\).
(b) Cleavage — apply the H-count
\(\ce{KMnO4}\), dilute \(\ce{H2SO4}\), heat. Left \(\ce{(CH3)2C=}\) has 0 H → ketone; right \(\ce{=CHCH3}\) has 1 H → acid. B1 A1
propanone \(\ce{(CH3)2C=O}\) + ethanoic acid \(\ce{CH3COOH}\).
Why harder: the conditions flip the product entirely — cold/alkaline → diol, hot/acidified → cleavage. Lose the "cold/alkaline" or "hot/acidified" and you lose the mark.

Question 5

Example — HCI Q7 (structural elucidation)
An alkene X has molecular formula \(\ce{C6H10}\). It reacts with liquid bromine in the dark to form \(\ce{C6H10Br4}\). On heating with acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\), \(\ce{CO2}\) is evolved and the products are \(\ce{CH3CO2H}\), \(\ce{CH3COCO2H}\) and \(\ce{CO2}\). Deduce the structure of X, explaining your reasoning. [4]
Solution — verbatim HCI deductions
Deductions (state the reasoning, not just the answer)
M1 M1 (each deduction), A1 structure
• X reacts with liquid bromine in the dark to form \(\ce{C6H10Br4}\) ⟹ X has 2 \(\ce{C=C}\) double bonds and undergoes electrophilic addition reaction with liquid bromine (\(\ce{C6H10 + 2Br2 -> C6H10Br4}\)).

• Upon heating with acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) solution, carbon dioxide gas was formed ⟹ X is a terminal alkene; X undergoes oxidative cleavage (\(\ce{C6H10 + 8[O] -> CH3CO2H + CH3COCO2H + CO2 + H2O}\)).
Structure of X
A1
2-methylpenta-1,3-diene or 3-methylpenta-1,3-diene (re-join the cleavage fragments across the two \(\ce{C=C}\); check the carbon count is \(\ce{C6}\)).
Method note (state this): In questions involving structural elucidation, you are required to explain qualitatively how you deduced the structures, by stating the type of reaction (e.g. electrophilic addition, oxidative cleavage) and the type (and number) of functional groups responsible.

Practice — HCI Q5-style retrosynthesis, harder
An alkene \(\ce{C9H16}\) with two \(\ce{C=C}\) bonds is oxidatively cleaved (hot acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\)) to give propanone, \(\ce{CH3COCH2CH2CO2H}\) (a keto-acid), and \(\ce{CO2}\). Work backwards to deduce a structure, and say how the \(\ce{CO2}\) tells you about the \(\ce{C=C}\) positions. [4]
Answer
Map each fragment back to its C=C carbon
Retrosynthesis — think backwards from products to starting material. M1 fragments, M1 rejoin
Propanone \(\ce{(CH3)2C=O}\) came from a \(\ce{=C(CH3)2}\) end (0 H → ketone); the keto-acid's \(\ce{-CO2H}\) came from a \(\ce{=CHR}\) end (1 H → acid); the \(\ce{CO2}\) came from a terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) (2 H). Re-join to a \(\ce{C9H16}\) diene, e.g. \(\ce{(CH3)2C=CH-CH2CH2-C(=CH2)-CH3}\) (2,6-... skeleton — check 9 C, 2 C=C).
What the CO₂ tells you
A1 A1
Effervescence of \(\ce{CO2}\) shows a terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) was present (a carbon with 2 H is lost as \(\ce{CO2}\)); count carbons before and after cleavage to fix the positions.
Why harder: three fragments (ketone + acid + \(\ce{CO2}\)) to re-assemble, and the carbon count must balance — the classic mark-loser is forgetting the carbon lost as \(\ce{CO2}\).
Answer hidden — click Reveal
Archetype
Structural elucidation via oxidative cleavage (retrosynthesis).

Work backwards: each carbonyl/acid/\(\ce{CO2}\) fragment maps to a \(\ce{C=C}\) carbon by its H-count; rejoin across the \(\ce{C=C}\)(s); balance the carbons. Always state the reaction types and functional groups you used.

Question 6

Example — HCI Q6(a)
Describe a chemical test to distinguish cyclohexane from cyclohexene. Give reagents, conditions, and the observation for each compound. [3]
Solution — verbatim HCI (any one option)
Reagents/conditions: \(\ce{Br2}\) in \(\ce{CCl4}\), absence of UV light (to prevent free radical substitution).
• cyclohexane: reddish brown \(\ce{Br2}\) not decolourised.
• cyclohexene: reddish brown \(\ce{Br2}\) decolourised.

OR aqueous \(\ce{Br2}\) → cyclohexane: yellow-orange \(\ce{Br2(aq)}\) not decolourised; cyclohexene: decolourised.
OR \(\ce{KMnO4}\), dilute \(\ce{H2SO4}\), heat → cyclohexane: purple \(\ce{KMnO4}\) not decolourised; cyclohexene: decolourised.
OR \(\ce{KMnO4}\), \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\), cold → cyclohexene: decolourised and brown ppt of \(\ce{MnO2}\).
A test answer needs BOTH outcomes — the change for the alkene AND "not decolourised" for the alkane. State reagent + conditions together.

Practice — HCI Q6(b), harder (terminal vs internal)
Describe how to distinguish but-1-ene from but-2-ene, giving reagents, conditions and the key observation for each. [3]
Answer — verbatim HCI
Reagents/conditions: \(\ce{KMnO4}\), dilute \(\ce{H2SO4}\), heat.
• but-1-ene: purple \(\ce{KMnO4}\) decolourised, effervescence of (colourless and odourless) \(\ce{CO2}\) gas (which gave a white precipitate of \(\ce{CaCO3}\) when bubbled in limewater \(\ce{Ca(OH)2}\)).
• but-2-ene: purple \(\ce{KMnO4}\) decolourised but no gas evolved.
Why harder: both are alkenes, so a plain decolourisation test can't separate them — you need oxidative cleavage so the terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) of but-1-ene gives \(\ce{CO2}\) effervescence and but-2-ene doesn't.

Distinguishing tests — alkene vs alkane, and terminal vs internal C=C.
