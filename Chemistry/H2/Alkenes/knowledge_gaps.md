## 2026-08-05 — diagnostic test (Q4 HBr addition, Q5 structure determination, Q7 distinguishing)

Big, productive session. He called Q4 and Q7 "such a f***ing good question" and worked most of it out loud. Lock these:

**Q4 — pent-1-ene + HBr (electrophilic addition, Markovnikov)**

1. **Markovnikov — the full mark-scheme keyword chain for the major product (2-bromopentane).** Had the idea, kept missing the exact words. LOCK: **adding H⁺ to C1 generates a secondary carbocation rather than a primary one**; the secondary carbocation has **more electron-donating alkyl groups, which disperse the positive charge and stabilise it**; a more stable carbocation has a **lower activation energy of formation** and is therefore **preferentially formed** → 2-bromopentane is major. Miss any of {2° vs 1° / alkyl groups disperse charge / stabilise / lower Ea / preferentially formed} and you drop marks. (ACTIVE — this Q "always comes out")

2. **Racemic-mixture trap — "is the product optically active?"** Nearly fell for it. LOCK: the product has a chiral carbon **but is optically INACTIVE**, because the reaction goes through a **planar (trigonal) carbocation that is attacked by Br⁻ with equal probability from the top and bottom faces**, giving a **racemic mixture (50:50) of the two enantiomers whose rotations cancel**. His own rule: **whenever an alkene electrophilic addition creates a new chiral centre, expect a racemic mixture → optically inactive.** (ACTIVE, recurring — ties to Organic_Foundations #19/#22)

3. **Why HBr adds readily to pent-1-ene but HF does not.** First wrong instinct was "HF is too small", then self-corrected. LOCK: the **H–F bond is very strong (high bond energy), so HF does not undergo heterolytic fission** to release the H⁺ electrophile. (FRAGILE)

**Q5 — combustion → degrees of unsaturation → oxidative cleavage → deduce structure**

4. **Molecular formula from combustion.** For CₓH_y, the mole ratio (hydrocarbon : H₂O) gives **y/2 = mol H₂O per mol hydrocarbon**, so y = 2 × that. "This is always y/2." Got C₇H₁₂. (FRAGILE)

5. **Degrees of unsaturation, then narrowing with Br₂ uptake.** (2C + 2 − H)/2; C₇H₁₂ → **2 degrees**. Two degrees = 2 C=C **or** 1 C≡C **or** 1 C=C + 1 ring. Because **A adds only ONE Br₂ (product is Br₂-, not Br₄-substituted) → only one C=C → the second degree must be a RING.** This ring deduction was the step he initially missed (assumed 2 C=C). (ACTIVE)

6. **Oxidative cleavage — conditions + the product table (the core method).** Conditions: **hot acidified KMnO₄ (KMnO₄ + dilute H₂SO₄ + heat)**; contrast mild oxidation = cold dilute alkaline KMnO₄ → diol. Work backwards from each product fragment: **=CR₂ (0 H on the alkene carbon) → ketone R₂C=O · =CHR (1 H) → carboxylic acid RCOOH · =CH₂ (2 H, terminal) → CO₂ (+ H₂O)**. His words: "0H → ketone, 1H → carboxylic acid, 2H → CO₂… this is so important to remember." A **single** product from the cleavage means the alkene was **in a ring** (the ring opens to one chain). (ACTIVE, recurring — extends the 28 Jul structure-determination gap #3; still slow + error-prone)

**Q7 — distinguishing tests, bromine water, isomer counting**

7. **Bromine water gives up to THREE products (and why no dichloro).** With Br₂(aq) (+ NaCl present): **1,2-dibromoalkane, the bromohydrin (1-bromo-2-ol, from H₂O/OH⁻), and 1-bromo-2-chloro (from Cl⁻)**. Only the **Br₂ can act as the electrophile** (it is polarised / forms the induced dipole that initiates and makes the carbocation); **Cl⁻, OH⁻ and H₂O are nucleophiles** that only attack the carbocation in step 2 → **no dichloro product forms** (Cl⁻ cannot initiate). (ACTIVE — the "three-product trap")

8. **Distinguishing tests.** hex-1-ene vs cyclohexane: **Br₂ in CCl₄** (reddish-brown decolourised by the alkene only) or **acidified KMnO₄**. hex-1-ene vs hex-2-ene: **hot acidified KMnO₄ oxidative cleavage** — the terminal =CH₂ of hex-1-ene gives **CO₂, which turns limewater milky**; hex-2-ene gives no CO₂. (FRAGILE — mostly there)

9. **Isomer-counting routine.** Chain isomers → position isomers; **cis-trans counts as one extra isomer; a chiral carbon counts as one extra.** Drawing convention he set for himself: "put all the double bonds on the inside." (FRAGILE)

3. **Why \(\ce{H2O}\) is a nucleophile, not an electrophile** (his question). \(\ce{H2O}\) has lone pairs → it's a **nucleophile**; it's a poor/weak electrophile (the \(\ce{O-H}\) bond is strong/hard to break heterolytically). This is why mild oxidation (adding OH across C=C) is **not** classed as electrophilic addition — there's no electrophile. Worth a one-line note next session. (concept)
