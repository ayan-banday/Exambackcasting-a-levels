## 1. Graphite — the π system and the dispersion forces are TWO different things

This is the exact spot you got tangled. Here is the clean version, straight from your notes.

- In graphite, each carbon is **sp² hybridised** and covalently bonded to **three** other carbons, forming flat two-dimensional layers of hexagonal carbon rings.
- Each carbon has a **2p orbital containing one electron** that is **not used in forming the C–C σ bonds** (the unhybridised 2p orbital).
- **Within each layer:** these 2p orbitals **overlap side-on** with one another to form a **delocalised π bonding system** (like the delocalisation of π electrons in benzene). The electrons in this π system are **delocalised and free to move along the layer**, so they act as **mobile charge carriers**. This is why graphite conducts **along the layers**.
- **Between the layers:** there are **weak dispersion forces** (because of the large surface area).

**The mix-up to kill:** you said the 2p side-on overlap "forms the dispersion forces." It does not.
- The 2p orbitals overlapping side-on form the **π bonding system**, which is what gives graphite its **conductivity** along the layers.
- The **dispersion forces are the weak forces *between* the layers**, which are what make graphite **soft and slippery** (the layers slide over one another).

They are two separate features. One is inside a layer (π system, conduction); the other is between the layers (dispersion, softness).

**Why it conducts along the layers but not at right angles to them:** the delocalised electrons are free to move **within** a layer, but they **cannot jump from one layer to the next** (the layers are held only by dispersion forces, with no electron pathway between them). So graphite does not conduct in the direction at right angles to the layers.

**Full mark-scheme answer for "why does graphite conduct along its layers but not across them":**
Each carbon in graphite is covalently bonded to three other carbons, and its unhybridised 2p orbital (containing one electron) overlaps side-on within the layer to form a delocalised π bonding system. The delocalised electrons are free to move along the layer and act as mobile charge carriers, so graphite conducts along the layers. The delocalised electrons cannot jump from one layer to the next, so it does not conduct in the direction at right angles to the layers.

## 2. Metallic vs ionic melting point — don't assume

You hesitated on where magnesium (metallic) and sodium chloride (ionic) sit. There is **no fixed rule** that ionic always beats metallic — it depends on the actual bonding strengths. Let the charges, ionic sizes and any given data decide the order. For this set the melting points settle it: magnesium 650 °C is lower than sodium chloride 801 °C, so here metallic is below ionic. Reason from the specific substances, never from a blanket "ionic is higher than metallic."
