## The one thing to hold: it's ONE chain

Everything in this chapter is a single causal chain. Nothing here is a standalone fact:

### **VSEPR → Shape → Polarity → Intermolecular force → Physical property**

| # | Stage | What you actually do | What comes out |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **VSEPR** | count the electron pairs on the central atom | the electron arrangement |
| 2 | **Shape** | name it by atom positions (lone pairs bend it) | shape + bond angle |
| 3 | **Polarity** | check if the bond dipoles cancel by symmetry | polar or non-polar molecule |
| 4 | **IMF** | polarity + electron count pick the force | dispersion / pd–pd / H-bond |
| 5 | **Property** | the IMF strength sets the behaviour | mp, bp, solubility, volatility, conductivity |

Read any exam question by asking: **where on this chain am I?** A bond-angle question is at the start. A boiling-point or solubility question is at the end but you have to walk the whole chain to answer it. The marks live in walking the chain out loud, not in naming the endpoint.

**The single most tested skill:** explain a physical property (usually mp/bp) from structure and bonding. That is the whole chapter compressed into one command word: *explain*.

## LAYER 1 — Shape (VSEPR)

**Two principles, that's the entire theory:**
1. Electron pairs/groups around the central atom get **as far apart as possible** (minimise repulsion).
2. Repulsion strength: **lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair.** (A lone pair is held by one nucleus only, so it spreads out and pushes harder.)

**Two rules that trip people:**
- Count a **double or triple bond as ONE** electron group.
- Name the shape by **atom positions only**. Lone pairs bend the shape but are invisible in its name.

**The shape chart (know these cold):**

| e⁻ pairs | bond : lone | Shape | Angle | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 2 : 0 | linear | 180° | CO₂, BeCl₂, HCN |
| 3 | 3 : 0 | trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃, CO₃²⁻, SO₃ |
| 3 | 2 : 1 | bent | ~118° | SO₂, NO₂⁻, O₃ |
| 4 | 4 : 0 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄, NH₄⁺, SO₄²⁻ |
| 4 | 3 : 1 | trigonal pyramidal | 107° | NH₃, PCl₃, H₃O⁺ |
| 4 | 2 : 2 | bent | 104.5° | H₂O, H₂S, OF₂ |
| 5 | 5 : 0 | trigonal bipyramidal | 90°, 120° | PCl₅ |
| 5 | 4 : 1 | see-saw | — | SF₄ |
| 5 | 3 : 2 | T-shape | — | ClF₃, BrF₃ |
| 5 | 2 : 3 | linear | 180° | XeF₂, I₃⁻ |
| 6 | 6 : 0 | octahedral | 90° | SF₆ |
| 6 | 4 : 2 | square planar | 90° | XeF₄, ICl₄⁻ |

**5-pair rule:** lone pairs always go **equatorial** (fewest 90° lp–lp clashes). That single rule explains see-saw, T-shape, and linear XeF₂.

### Layer 2 — the bond-angle "explain" mark

There are **two different reasons** an angle changes. Pick the right one or lose the mark.

**Reason A — lone pairs (same central atom, different lone-pair count):**
CH₄ 109.5° → NH₃ 107° → H₂O 104.5°. Each added lone pair repels the bond pairs harder, squeezing them closer, so the angle shrinks.

**Reason B — electronegativity (same shape, different atoms):**
- H₂O (104.5°) > H₂S. O is more electronegative than S → pulls bond pairs closer to itself → more bond-pair–bond-pair repulsion → wider angle.
- NH₃ (107°) > NF₃. H is less electronegative than F → bond pair sits closer to N → more repulsion near N → wider angle.

The trap: reaching for lone pairs when the question is actually about electronegativity, or vice versa.

## LAYER 1 — Polarity (shape decides it)

**Polar bond ≠ polar molecule.** Two separate things.
- **Polar bond:** two atoms of different electronegativity → dipole (δ+ / δ−). C–H is treated as non-polar.
- **Polar molecule:** the bond dipoles **don't cancel** once you account for the shape.

### Layer 2 — the cancellation logic
- Symmetric shape → dipoles cancel → **non-polar molecule even though the bonds are polar.** CO₂ (linear), BF₃ (trigonal planar), CCl₄ (tetrahedral).
- Asymmetric shape (usually a lone pair breaks the symmetry) → dipoles don't cancel → **polar.** H₂O (bent), NH₃ (pyramidal), CHCl₃.

This is why Layer 1 (shape) has to come first: you cannot decide polarity without the shape. BF₃ vs NH₃ is the classic pair — same "three bonds to a central atom," opposite polarity, purely because of shape (planar cancels, pyramidal doesn't).

## LAYER 1 — Intermolecular forces (polarity + electrons decide which)

Three forces. All weak vs actual bonds. All electrostatic.

| IMF | Between what | Strength driven by |
|---|---|---|
| **id–id (dispersion)** | ALL molecules (the only force for non-polar) | (1) number of electrons, (2) surface area / shape |
| **pd–pd** | polar molecules | size of the permanent dipole |
| **Hydrogen bond** | H–(F/O/N) **and** a lone pair on an F/O/N | electronegativity of the F/O/N (strength) + number of H-bonds per molecule (extent) |

**The strength ladder (Engine B):**
ionic / metallic / giant covalent (all strong, lots of energy) ≫ **hydrogen bond > pd–pd > id–id** (all weak intermolecular).

### Layer 2 — the three things that actually get tested

**1. Dispersion scales with electrons.** More electrons → bigger, more polarisable electron cloud → stronger instantaneous dipoles → stronger dispersion. This is why I₂ is a solid but F₂ is a gas, and why **HI boils higher than HBr** even though HBr has the bigger permanent dipole — dispersion from HI's extra electrons beats the dipole. (Your logged confusion: *more polarisable = more electrons = stronger dispersion.* Yes. That's the mechanism.)

**2. Hydrogen bonding needs BOTH halves.** An H attached to F/O/N (to give a big enough δ+) **and** a lone pair on an F/O/N to receive it. Miss either half and there's no H-bond.
- **Strength** rises with electronegativity of the atom H is bonded to: HF's H-bond is stronger than NH₃'s.
- **Extent** rises with how many H-bonds each molecule can form. H₂O forms ~2 per molecule (2 δ+ H + 2 lone pairs). HF and NH₃ manage only ~1 each.
- So **bp: H₂O > HF > NH₃** — H₂O wins on extent, not strength.

**3. Dispersion exists in polar molecules too.** Polar molecules have pd–pd *and* dispersion. Dispersion is never switched off. (Your logged confusion — yes, dispersion is always present.)

## LAYER 1 — Physical properties (the payoff)

### Layer 2 — the two mark-scheme engines

**Engine A — any melting/boiling point question, four moves:**
1. Name the **structure** of each substance (giant ionic / giant metallic / giant covalent / simple molecular).
2. Name the **specific attraction broken** on melting (ionic bond / metallic bond / covalent bond / H-bond / pd–pd / dispersion).
3. Compare **strength**, quoting the deciding factor (charge, radius, number of electrons, number of H-bonds).
4. Make the **energy statement**: *"more energy is needed to overcome the stronger ___, hence higher mp/bp."*

Move 4 is mark-bearing. Strength alone doesn't score — you must say energy.

**Engine B** is the strength ladder above (which attraction, how strong).

### The property table

| Structure | On melting you break | mp/bp | Conducts? | Soluble in water? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Giant ionic | strong ionic attraction (whole lattice) | high | only molten/aqueous (ions mobile), not solid | usually yes |
| Giant metallic | metallic bond (cations + e⁻ sea) | high | yes, solid & molten (delocalised e⁻) | no |
| Giant covalent | many strong covalent bonds | very high | only graphite (see below) | no |
| Simple molecular | **weak IMF only** | low | no | only if it can H-bond / is ionic-forming |

### The two facts you keep logging as confusing

**Simple molecular melting breaks IMF, NOT covalent bonds.** When ice or I₂ or CO₂ melts, only the weak intermolecular forces break. The O–H, I–I, C=O covalent bonds inside each molecule stay intact. Saying "covalent bonds break when ice melts" is a 0-mark answer. (This is exactly the misconception you flagged in your own Mistakes file — "bp affected by covalent bond." It isn't, for simple molecular.)

**Graphite vs diamond — same element, opposite properties, all from hybridisation:**
- **Diamond:** each C is **sp³**, bonded to 4 C in a 3-D lattice. All 4 outer electrons locked in σ bonds → no mobile charge → **doesn't conduct**; breaking it means breaking the whole lattice → **very hard**.
- **Graphite:** each C is **sp²**, bonded to 3 C in flat layers. The leftover unhybridised p electron forms a **delocalised π system within each layer** → mobile electrons → **conducts along the layers**. Layers are held to each other only by **weak dispersion forces** → they slide → **soft, slippery**.
- Your logged question — *why conduct within a layer but not across?* Because the delocalised π electrons are free to move inside a layer, but between layers there's only a dispersion gap with no electron pathway, so charge can't cross.

**SiO₂ vs diamond** (your logged confusion): both giant covalent, but SiO₂'s Si–O bonds are longer/weaker than diamond's C–C, so SiO₂ melts a bit lower. It is still very high — nothing like a simple molecular solid.

### Solubility — "like dissolves like," stated properly
A substance dissolves when the **solute–solvent** attraction formed is at least as strong as the **solute–solute + solvent–solvent** attractions you have to break.
- Polar / H-bonding solutes dissolve in water (new H-bonds pay for the ones broken).
- Non-polar solutes (hexane) don't dissolve in water: weak solute–solvent forces can't repay water's H-bonds.
- Layer-2 phrasing: name all three interaction types and compare. That's the answer structure.

## Quick recall — the spine on one page

Walk the chain top to bottom. Each step feeds the next:

1. **VSEPR** — two principles: pairs get as far apart as possible; repulsion lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp.
2. **Shape** — count pairs, multiple bond counts as one group, name by atoms.
3. **Bond angle** — explained by lone pairs *or* electronegativity. Pick one, not both.
4. **Polarity** — do the bond dipoles cancel by symmetry? BF₃ planar cancels (non-polar); NH₃ pyramidal doesn't (polar).
5. **IMF** — dispersion scales with electrons; pd–pd for polar molecules; H-bond needs H–(F/O/N) **and** a lone pair on F/O/N.
6. **Property** — Engine A: structure → attraction broken → strength → **energy statement**.

**Two non-negotiables at the property end:**
- Simple molecular melting breaks **IMF only, never the covalent bonds**.
- Graphite conducts (sp², delocalised π); diamond doesn't (sp³, all σ).

**Strength ladder:** ionic / metallic / giant covalent ≫ **H-bond > pd–pd > dispersion**.

**Must-say phrases for marks:**
- "more energy is needed to overcome the stronger ___"
- "only the intermolecular forces are broken, not the covalent bonds" (simple molecular)
- "the bond dipoles cancel due to the symmetrical shape" (non-polar despite polar bonds)
- "delocalised electrons act as mobile charge carriers" (conductivity)
