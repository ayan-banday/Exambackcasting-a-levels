## Q1
(a) BF₃ trigonal planar 120°; NH₃ trigonal pyramidal 107°; SF₄ see-saw; XeF₂ linear 180°. [4]
(b) In CH₄ there are 4 bond pairs only. In NH₃ one bond pair is replaced by a **lone pair**; **lone pair–bond pair repulsion is greater than bond pair–bond pair repulsion**, so the bonding pairs are pushed closer together and the angle decreases to 107°. In H₂O there are **two lone pairs**, giving even greater repulsion (lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp), so the angle decreases further to 104.5°. [3]
(c) F is **more electronegative** than H, so in NF₃ the **bonding electrons are drawn away from N** towards F. This reduces the bond-pair–bond-pair repulsion around N, so the bonding pairs move closer together and the angle is smaller than in NH₃ (where H is less electronegative and the bond pair sits closer to N). [2]

## Q2
(a) In CO₂ (linear) the two C=O **bond dipoles are equal and opposite and cancel** → net dipole = 0 → non-polar. In SO₂ (bent, because of the lone pair on S) the **bond dipoles do not cancel** → net dipole ≠ 0 → polar. [3]
(b) BF₃ trigonal planar → dipoles cancel → **non-polar**. NH₃ trigonal pyramidal → dipoles do not cancel → **polar**. [2]

## Q3
(a) **Instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (dispersion)** and **permanent dipole–permanent dipole**. [1]
(b) From HCl to HI the **number of electrons increases**, so the electron cloud is **more polarisable** and the **instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (dispersion) forces become stronger**. This increase outweighs the decrease in permanent dipole–permanent dipole attraction, so **more energy is needed to overcome the stronger dispersion forces** and the boiling point rises. [3]

## Q4
(a) O is more electronegative and smaller than S, so **H₂O forms hydrogen bonds** whereas H₂S has only weaker permanent dipole–permanent dipole (and dispersion) forces. **More energy is needed to overcome the hydrogen bonds**, so H₂O boils higher. [2]
(b) Each H₂O molecule has **2 δ+ H atoms and 2 lone pairs**, so it forms on average **~2 hydrogen bonds per molecule** — the greatest **extent** of hydrogen bonding. HF (1 δ+ H) and NH₃ (1 lone pair) can each form only ~1 per molecule. More hydrogen bonds per molecule → more energy to overcome → H₂O has the highest bp. [3]
(c) In ice each H₂O is hydrogen-bonded to **4 others in an open tetrahedral lattice** with empty spaces, so the molecules are held **further apart** than in liquid water. Lower mass per volume → ice is less dense. [2]

## Q5
(a) Sodium — giant metallic; SiO₂ — giant covalent (molecular); iodine — simple molecular; NaCl — giant ionic. [4]
(b) Increasing mp: **iodine < sodium < sodium chloride < silicon(IV) oxide**. Iodine (simple molecular) — only weak **dispersion** forces broken, little energy. Sodium (metallic) — moderately strong metallic bonding. NaCl (ionic) — strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. SiO₂ (giant covalent) — many **strong covalent bonds** broken, most energy. Award for correct order + energy comparison at each step. [5]
(c) On melting iodine, **only the weak intermolecular (dispersion) forces are overcome — the strong I–I covalent bonds are not broken**, so little energy is needed and the mp is low. [2]

## Q6
(a) In graphite each C is **sp² hybridised, bonded to 3 others**, leaving one electron per C in a **delocalised π system**; these delocalised electrons are **mobile charge carriers** → conducts. In diamond each C is **sp³, bonded to 4 others**, all four outer electrons in localised σ bonds → **no mobile charge carriers** → does not conduct. [3]
(b) Graphite has a **layered structure held together by weak dispersion forces**; the layers **slide over each other easily** → soft/lubricant. Diamond has a **3-D network of strong covalent bonds** in all directions → very hard. [3]

## Q7
(a) Lattice energy is the **energy released when one mole of a solid ionic compound is formed from its constituent gaseous ions**. (Accept: enthalpy change when 1 mol of ionic solid forms from gaseous ions.) [2]
(b) MgO has ions of **higher charge (Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ vs Na⁺ and Cl⁻)** and **smaller ionic radii**. Since lattice energy ∝ (q⁺q⁻)/(r⁺+r⁻), the **larger charge product and smaller inter-ionic distance** give MgO a much larger (more exothermic) lattice energy. [3]
(c) **MgO** — larger lattice energy → stronger ionic bonding → higher mp. [1]

## Q8
NaCl dissolves in water because the **ion–dipole attractions between the ions and water** release enough energy to compensate for breaking the **ionic lattice (solute–solute)** and the **hydrogen bonds between water molecules (solvent–solvent)**. In hexane the only solute–solvent interactions are **weak ion–induced dipole**, too weak to overcome the strong ionic lattice, so NaCl does not dissolve. [4]

## Q9 (indicative content — this is the extended-method capstone)
A strong answer runs the whole chain and then qualifies it:
- **Electronegativity difference → bond type**: small Δ → covalent; large Δ → ionic; intermediate → polar covalent / ionic with covalent character (Fajans).
- **Electronegativity → bond polarity → (with shape) → molecular polarity**: decides pd–pd vs dispersion-only.
- **→ intermolecular forces → physical properties**: e.g. hydrogen bonding (needs the high electronegativity of N/O/F) explains the anomalous bp of H₂O/HF/NH₃.
- **Counter (for the judgement)**: electronegativity is **not** the only factor — **number of electrons / molecular size** drives dispersion (HI > HBr), **shape** decides whether dipoles cancel (CO₂ non-polar despite polar bonds), **ionic charge and radius** drive lattice energy and mp. So electronegativity is *a* major factor, especially for bond type and hydrogen bonding, but structure and electron count are equally decisive for physical properties. Reach a judgement, don't sit on the fence. [10]
