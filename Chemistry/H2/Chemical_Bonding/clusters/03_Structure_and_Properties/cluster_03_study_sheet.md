# Cluster 3 — Structure → Physical Properties (the reasoning map)

> **Layer 1 + Layer 2, and every sub-argument carries its reasoning.** Layer 1 is the four structures. Layer 2 is, for each structure, every property **and the *why* behind it** — because the reasoning is the whole point; a bare property you can't explain is worthless in an exam. What's cut is worked examples and specific data, not the reasoning. Study this to understand the arguments; the drill turns that understanding into exam-precise answers.

---

## The one move behind every question

Every structure-to-property question is answered the same way: **identify the structure, identify the particles and the force holding them, then reason to the property.**

For melting or boiling point the mark-scheme engine is: name the structure, name the attraction broken, compare its strength, then state that **more energy is needed to overcome the stronger attraction**.

---

## Layer 1 — the four structures

| Structure | Particles | Held together by |
|---|---|---|
| Giant ionic | cations and anions | strong electrostatic attraction across the whole lattice |
| Giant metallic | metal cations + delocalised electrons | metallic bonding across the whole lattice |
| Giant covalent | atoms | many strong covalent bonds across the whole lattice |
| Simple molecular | molecules | weak intermolecular forces between molecules |

---

## Layer 2 — each property, with the reasoning

### Giant ionic
- **Melting point — high.** There are many strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely charged ions across the whole lattice, so a large amount of energy is needed to overcome them.
- **Conductivity — only when molten or aqueous.** In the solid the ions are held in fixed positions and cannot move; when molten or dissolved the ions are free to move and act as mobile charge carriers.
- **Solubility — usually soluble.** The polar water molecules are attracted to the ions (ion-dipole attraction) and pull them out of the lattice.
- **Mechanical — hard and rigid but brittle.** Hard because the electrostatic attractions are strong. Brittle because shearing the lattice pushes ions of like charge together, and the repulsion between them shatters the lattice.

### Giant metallic
- **Melting point — high.** There is a strong electrostatic attraction between the metal cations and the sea of delocalised electrons, so a lot of energy is needed to overcome the metallic bonding.
- **Conductivity — conducts as a solid and when molten.** The delocalised electrons are free to move and carry charge in both states.
- **Solubility — insoluble.** The metallic bonding cannot be broken by any interaction with water.
- **Mechanical — malleable and ductile.** When a force is applied the layers of cations slide over one another; the sea of delocalised electrons prevents the cations from repelling each other, so the metallic bonding stays intact and the metal deforms instead of shattering.

### Giant covalent
- **Melting point — very high.** A very large number of strong covalent bonds extend throughout the lattice and must be broken, which needs a great deal of energy.
- **Conductivity — non-conductor, except graphite.** The electrons are held in localised covalent bonds, so there are no mobile charge carriers. Graphite is the exception: each carbon bonds to only three others, leaving one delocalised electron per atom that can move along the layers.
- **Solubility — insoluble.** No interaction with a solvent is strong enough to break the covalent lattice.
- **Mechanical — very hard (graphite soft).** Very hard because of the rigid three-dimensional network of strong covalent bonds. Graphite is soft because its layers are held together only by weak forces and slide over each other.

### Simple molecular
- **Melting point — low.** Only the weak intermolecular forces between the molecules are overcome, not the covalent bonds inside the molecules, so little energy is needed.
- **Conductivity — non-conductor.** There are no ions and no delocalised or mobile electrons.
- **Solubility — soluble only if polar or able to hydrogen-bond.** The solute-solvent attractions formed must be able to replace the attractions broken, so "like dissolves like."
- **Mechanical — soft.** The weak intermolecular forces are easily overcome.

---

## Cross-cutting points (where the marks hide)

1. **Conductivity always comes down to mobile charge carriers** — free ions (molten/aqueous ionic) or delocalised electrons (metals, graphite). No mobile carriers means no conduction.
2. **Simple molecular melting breaks only the intermolecular forces**, never the covalent bonds inside the molecules. Saying "covalent bonds break" here loses the mark.
3. **The Period 3 melting-point trend is the synthesis** of all four structures in one line: sodium, magnesium, aluminium rise (giant metallic), silicon is the peak (giant covalent), then phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine, argon are low (simple molecular).

## Quick recall — the map

1. Four structures: giant ionic, giant metallic, giant covalent, simple molecular. Each has its own particles and its own force.
2. Every property is reasoned from that force: melting point from its strength, conductivity from whether there are mobile charge carriers, solubility from whether new attractions can replace the ones broken, mechanical behaviour from how the structure responds to a force.
3. The engine for melting/boiling: structure, attraction broken, strength comparison, energy statement.
