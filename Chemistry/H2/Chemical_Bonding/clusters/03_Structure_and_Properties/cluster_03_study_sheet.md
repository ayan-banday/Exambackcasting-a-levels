# Cluster 3 — Structure → Physical Properties (the reasoning map)

> **Lean argument map — Layer 1 + Layer 2 only, no worked examples.** Clusters 1 and 2 already gave you the pieces (the property table, graphite vs diamond, lattice energy, the intermolecular forces). This cluster is the **framework that ties them together**: given any substance, how you argue from its structure to its properties. Target: see an "explain the physical properties of X" question and immediately know the shape of the answer. The detail and examples live in the drill.

---

## The one move behind every question

Every structure-to-property question is answered the same way:

**Identify the structure, identify the particles and the force holding them, then reason to the property.**

For melting or boiling point, the mark-scheme engine is: name the structure, name the attraction that is broken, compare its strength (quote the deciding factor), then state that more energy is needed to overcome the stronger attraction.

---

## Layer 1 — the four structures (each argues differently)

| Structure | Particles | Held together by |
|---|---|---|
| Giant ionic | cations and anions | strong electrostatic attraction across the whole lattice |
| Giant metallic | metal cations + delocalised electrons | metallic bonding across the whole lattice |
| Giant covalent | atoms | many strong covalent bonds across the whole lattice |
| Simple molecular | molecules | weak intermolecular forces between molecules |

---

## Layer 2 — how each structure argues each property

| Structure | Melting/boiling point | Electrical conductivity | Solubility (water) | Mechanical |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Giant ionic | high — strong electrostatic attraction | only when molten or aqueous (ions become mobile), not solid | usually soluble | hard but brittle |
| Giant metallic | high — strong metallic bonding | conducts as solid and molten (delocalised electrons) | insoluble | malleable and ductile |
| Giant covalent | very high — many strong covalent bonds broken | non-conductor (except graphite) | insoluble | very hard (graphite soft) |
| Simple molecular | low — only weak intermolecular forces broken | non-conductor | soluble only if it can hydrogen-bond / is polar | soft |

---

## The cross-cutting sub-arguments (where the marks hide)

These are the "argument" points examiners reward on top of the table:

1. **Conductivity depends on mobile charge carriers.** Ionic conducts only when the ions are free to move (molten/aqueous). Metallic conducts through delocalised electrons. Graphite conducts through a delocalised electron system; diamond and silicon dioxide do not (no mobile charge carriers).
2. **Simple molecular melting breaks only the intermolecular forces**, never the covalent bonds inside the molecules. Stating "covalent bonds break" for a simple molecular substance loses the mark.
3. **The Period 3 melting-point trend is the synthesis** of all four structures in one line: sodium, magnesium, aluminium rise (giant metallic), silicon is the peak (giant covalent), then phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine, argon are low (simple molecular).
4. **Volatility down a group** (for simple molecular): more electrons give stronger dispersion forces, so boiling point rises down the group.
5. **Ice is less dense than water** because hydrogen bonding holds the molecules in an open lattice with spaces.

## Quick recall — the map

1. Four structures: giant ionic, giant metallic, giant covalent, simple molecular. Each has its own particles and its own force.
2. Every property comes from that force: melting point from its strength, conductivity from whether there are mobile charge carriers, solubility from whether new attractions can replace the ones broken, mechanical behaviour from how the lattice responds to force.
3. The engine for melting/boiling: structure, attraction broken, strength comparison, energy statement.
4. The Period 3 trend ties all four structures together in a single sequence.
