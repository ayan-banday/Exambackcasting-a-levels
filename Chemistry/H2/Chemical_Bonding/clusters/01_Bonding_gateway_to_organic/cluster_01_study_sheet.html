Toolkit — the bonding rules organic sits on
1 · Shapes (VSEPR)

Count bonding pairs + lone pairs on the central atom → electron geometry → molecular shape. Repulsion order: lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp; each lone pair squeezes bond angles ~2–2.5°. Base shapes: 2→linear 180°, 3→trigonal planar 120°, 4→tetrahedral 109.5°, 5→trigonal bipyramidal, 6→octahedral 90°. Lone pairs on 4 domains: 1 lp → pyramidal (\(\ce{NH3}\), 107°), 2 lp → bent (\(\ce{H2O}\), 104.5°).

2 · Electronegativity & polarity

Electronegativity = pull on bonding electrons; ↑ across a period, ↓ down a group. A difference makes a bond polar (\(\delta+ / \delta-\)). A molecule with polar bonds can still be non-polar if symmetric (dipoles cancel: \(\ce{CO2}\), \(\ce{CCl4}\), \(\ce{BF3}\)).

3 · σ and π bonds

σ = head-on overlap (every single bond; allows free rotation). π = sideways p-orbital overlap, present in multiple bonds and locks rotation → cis-trans isomerism. \(\ce{C=C}\) = 1σ+1π; \(\ce{C#C}\) = 1σ+2π; \(\ce{C=O}\) = 1σ+1π.

4 · Dative (coordinate) bond

A covalent bond where both electrons come from one atom (a lone pair donated into an empty orbital): \(\ce{NH4+}\), \(\ce{H3O+}\), \(\ce{Al2Cl6}\).

5 · Intermolecular forces (weakest → strongest)

id–id (dispersion; grows with number of electrons / \(M_r\) / contact area) < pd–pd (permanent dipoles) < hydrogen bonding (H bonded to N/O/F, attracted to a lone pair on N/O/F). IMF set boiling/melting point, solubility, viscosity — not covalent bond strength.

6 · Gateway to organic

Bond polarity sets reactivity: a \(\delta+\) carbon (in \(\ce{C-X}\), \(\ce{C=O}\), \(\ce{C-OH}\)) is attacked by nucleophiles; π electron density (\(\ce{C=C}\), benzene) is attacked by electrophiles. Shapes (109.5° sp³, 120° sp², 180° sp) and restricted rotation about \(\ce{C=C}\) come straight from this bonding.

Questions — attempt cold
Question 1[7]
(a) By reference to electron-pair repulsion, explain why the bond angle decreases in the order \(\ce{CH4} > \ce{NH3} > \ce{H2O}\), quoting an approximate value for each. [4]
(b) Predict and explain the shape of, and bond angle in, the \(\ce{ClF3}\) molecule. [3]
Reveal worked solution

(a) All three have four electron domains around the central atom, so the base geometry is tetrahedral.

Moleculebp / lpAngleReason
\(\ce{CH4}\)4 bp, 0 lp109.5°only bp–bp repulsion
\(\ce{NH3}\)3 bp, 1 lp≈107°1 lp; lp–bp > bp–bp squeezes the angle
\(\ce{H2O}\)2 bp, 2 lp≈104.5°2 lp; even greater lp–lp / lp–bp repulsion

The key idea markers reward: a lone pair repels more strongly than a bonding pair (it sits closer to the central atom, not shared), so each added lone pair pushes the bonding pairs closer together. A1 for the ordering + reason, A1 per correct angle set.

(b) \(\ce{ClF3}\): Cl has 7 valence e⁻; 3 are used bonding to F, leaving 3 bonding pairs + 2 lone pairs = 5 domains → trigonal bipyramidal electron geometry.

The 2 lone pairs occupy equatorial positions (least repulsion). Molecular shape = T-shaped, with F–Cl–F angles slightly less than 90° (≈87.5°) because the equatorial lone pairs repel the axial bonding pairs. A1 shape, A1 angle, A1 reason (lp in equatorial).

Archetype & trap

Archetype: VSEPR shape from a lone-pair count, plus the lp>bp repulsion argument for angle differences.

Trigger: asked for shape/angle of a species that has lone pairs, or to compare angles across a series → always count bp + lp first, then apply the repulsion order.

Trap: quoting angles without the reason (no marks), forgetting the 5-domain expanded octet on Cl (many write "trigonal planar"), and placing lone pairs axially instead of equatorially.

Question 2[5]
In the vapour phase aluminium chloride exists as the dimer \(\ce{Al2Cl6}\).
(a) Describe the bonding in \(\ce{Al2Cl6}\), stating which bonds are dative and explaining why they form. [3]
(b) State and explain the shape around each aluminium atom in the dimer. [2]
Reveal worked solution

(a) In \(\ce{AlCl3}\), Al has only 6 electrons in its valence shell — it is electron-deficient with an empty orbital. Two \(\ce{AlCl3}\) units join: a lone pair on a Cl of one unit is donated into the empty orbital of the Al on the other unit. This forms two bridging dative (coordinate) bonds, one in each direction. A1 electron deficiency, A1 lone pair donated to empty orbital, A1 two bridging dative bonds.

(b) Each Al now has 4 bonding pairs (2 terminal Cl + 2 bridging Cl), 0 lone pairs → tetrahedral, ≈109.5°. A1 tetrahedral, A1 "4 bonding pairs, no lone pairs."

Archetype & trap

Archetype: dative bonding driven by electron deficiency (empty orbital) + lone-pair donor.

Trigger: a species with an atom short of an octet (Al, B) or a positive ion that gained a bond (\(\ce{NH4+}\), \(\ce{H3O+}\)) → look for the empty orbital and the lone-pair donor.

Trap: saying "Al shares electrons with Cl" without naming where both electrons come from; forgetting that the shape around Al changes from trigonal planar (monomer) to tetrahedral (dimer).

Question 3[5]
(a) \(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{SO2}\) both contain polar bonds, yet \(\ce{CO2}\) has no overall dipole moment while \(\ce{SO2}\) does. Explain. [3]
(b) State, with reasoning, which of \(\ce{CHCl3}\) and \(\ce{CCl4}\) has the larger dipole moment. [2]
Reveal worked solution

(a) \(\ce{CO2}\) is linear (2 domains on C, no lone pairs): the two \(\ce{C=O}\) bond dipoles are equal and point in exactly opposite directions, so they cancel → no net dipole. \(\ce{SO2}\) is bent (S has a lone pair, 3 domains): the two bond dipoles do not cancel and there is a net dipole. A1 \(\ce{CO2}\) linear/symmetric → cancel, A1 \(\ce{SO2}\) bent (lone pair), A1 dipoles don't cancel.

(b) \(\ce{CCl4}\) is tetrahedral and symmetric → the four C–Cl dipoles cancel → non-polar. \(\ce{CHCl3}\) is not symmetric (one C–H, three C–Cl) → dipoles don't cancel. Therefore \(\ce{CHCl3}\) has the larger dipole moment. A1 answer, A1 symmetry/cancellation reason.

Archetype & trap

Archetype: molecular polarity = vector sum of bond dipoles → decided by shape/symmetry, not by whether bonds are polar.

Trigger: "explain why X is polar/non-polar" or "which has the larger dipole" → draw the shape, mark bond dipoles, check if they cancel.

Trap: answering from electronegativity difference alone ("both have polar bonds so both are polar") — the mark is in the symmetry. This is the same logic that later tells you where an organic molecule is δ+.

Question 4[6]
(a) For propene, \(\ce{CH3CH=CH2}\), state the total number of σ and π bonds, and give the shape and bond angle around each of the three carbon atoms. [3]
(b) Explain, in terms of bonding, why but-2-ene exhibits cis-trans isomerism but but-1-ene does not. [3]
Reveal worked solution

(a) Propene \(\ce{CH3-CH=CH2}\): count every single bond as 1σ; the double bond is 1σ+1π.

σ bonds: 3 (C–H on \(\ce{CH3}\)) + 1 (C–C) + 1 (C–H on middle C) + 2 (C–H on \(\ce{=CH2}\)) + 1 (the σ of \(\ce{C=C}\)) = 8 σ. π bonds: 1 π (in \(\ce{C=C}\)).

Shapes: the \(\ce{CH3}\) carbon has 4 σ (4 domains) → tetrahedral, 109.5°. The two \(\ce{C=C}\) carbons each have 3 σ + part of 1 π (3 domains) → trigonal planar, ≈120°. A1 σ/π counts, A1 sp³ carbon, A1 sp² carbons.

(b) The π bond in \(\ce{C=C}\) prevents rotation about the double bond, so the groups on each doubly-bonded carbon are locked in position. Cis-trans isomerism needs two different groups on each of the two \(\ce{C=C}\) carbons.

In but-2-ene \(\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3}\), each \(\ce{C=C}\) carbon carries an H and a \(\ce{CH3}\) (two different groups) → cis and trans exist. In but-1-ene \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH3}\), the terminal \(\ce{=CH2}\) carbon carries two identical H atoms → no isomerism. A1 π locks rotation, A1 need 2 different groups per C, A1 applied to both molecules.

Archetype & trap

Archetype: σ/π counting + the restricted-rotation origin of geometric isomerism.

Trigger: any molecule with a multiple bond → count σ (all single bonds + one per multiple bond) and π (extras); "does it show cis-trans" → test for a π bond AND two different groups on each end.

Trap: miscounting the σ inside the double bond (a \(\ce{C=C}\) still has 1σ), and asserting cis-trans without checking each carbon has two different groups. This is the exact reasoning alkene questions demand.

Gateway: the π bond you count here is the electron-rich site electrophiles attack in Alkenes.
Question 5[5]
The boiling points of the Group 16 hydrides are: \(\ce{H2O}\) 100 °C, \(\ce{H2S}\) −60 °C, \(\ce{H2Se}\) −41 °C, \(\ce{H2Te}\) −2 °C.
(a) Explain the general increase from \(\ce{H2S}\) to \(\ce{H2Te}\). [2]
(b) Account for the anomalously high boiling point of \(\ce{H2O}\). [3]
Reveal worked solution

(a) From \(\ce{H2S}\)→\(\ce{H2Te}\) the number of electrons increases, so the strength of id–id (dispersion) forces increases. More energy is needed to overcome them → boiling point rises. A1 more electrons/larger \(M_r\), A1 stronger id–id.

(b) \(\ce{H2O}\) can form hydrogen bonds (H bonded to highly electronegative O; O also has lone pairs to accept H-bonds). Each water molecule H-bonds extensively. Hydrogen bonds are much stronger than the id–id / pd–pd forces in the others, so far more energy is needed to separate the molecules → anomalously high bp. A1 H-bonding named with the H–O + lone-pair condition, A1 stronger than id–id/pd–pd, A1 more energy to overcome.

Archetype & trap

Archetype: boiling-point trend = identify the dominant IMF, then explain the change in that IMF.

Trigger: a bp/mp trend down a group or across a series → id–id grows with electrons; a spike at the N/O/F hydride → hydrogen bonding.

Trap: saying "the covalent bonds get weaker/stronger" — boiling breaks intermolecular forces, never covalent bonds. State the H-bond condition properly (H on N/O/F + lone pair acceptor).

Gateway: this H-bonding logic returns for alcohol, carboxylic-acid and amine boiling points/solubility.
Question 6[4]
Ethanoic acid (\(M_r\) 60) boils at 118 °C — higher than propan-1-ol (\(M_r\) 60, 97 °C). Explain this difference, including the structure ethanoic acid adopts in the liquid and vapour phases. [4]
Reveal worked solution

Both molecules hydrogen-bond through their O–H groups, but ethanoic acid H-bonds more effectively. Two ethanoic acid molecules form a cyclic dimer held by two hydrogen bonds (each \(\ce{O-H}\) to the \(\ce{C=O}\) oxygen of the other molecule).

The carbonyl \(\ce{C=O}\) also withdraws electron density, making the O–H more polar (stronger H-bond donor) than the O–H of an alcohol. So the total H-bonding to be overcome on boiling is greater → higher bp. A1 both H-bond, A1 dimer with two H-bonds, A1 \(\ce{C=O}\) makes O–H more polar, A1 more energy → higher bp.

Archetype & trap

Archetype: comparing H-bonding strength/number between two H-bonders of equal \(M_r\).

Trigger: "why does X boil higher than its isomer/near-neighbour" where both H-bond → count/qualify the H-bonds (dimer, extra donor sites, effect of adjacent \(\ce{C=O}\)).

Trap: stopping at "both H-bond" (no discriminating mark). The marks are in the dimer and the carbonyl-enhanced O–H polarity.

Question 7[5]
(a) Using bond polarity, identify and explain which carbon atom in 1-chloropropane, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2Cl}\), is susceptible to attack by a nucleophile such as \(\ce{OH-}\). [3]
(b) Explain why, by contrast, the double bond in propene is attacked by electrophiles. [2]
Reveal worked solution

(a) Cl is more electronegative than C, so the \(\ce{C-Cl}\) bond is polar: the carbon bonded to Cl carries a partial positive charge, \(\ce{C^{δ+}-Cl^{δ-}}\). A nucleophile is an electron-pair donor and is attracted to electron-poor centres, so \(\ce{OH-}\) attacks the \(\delta+\) carbon bearing the Cl (C-1). A1 C–Cl polar, A1 that C is δ+, A1 nucleophile = e⁻-pair donor attracted to δ+.

(b) In propene the \(\ce{C=C}\) has a π bond — a region of high electron density above and below the plane. An electrophile is an electron-pair acceptor, so it is attracted to and attacks this electron-rich π system. A1 π = high e⁻ density, A1 electrophile = e⁻-pair acceptor attracted to it.

Archetype & trap

Archetype: translate bonding (polarity vs π density) directly into the site and type of organic attack.

Trigger: "which atom is attacked / why does it react with a nucleophile/electrophile" → map electronegativity → δ+/δ− → nucleophile hits δ+ C; π/lone-pair density → electrophile hits it.

Trap: mixing up nucleophile (donor, seeks δ+) and electrophile (acceptor, seeks e⁻-rich). Define both by what they donate/accept, not by charge alone.

Gateway: this IS the opening move of every Halogen Derivatives (nucleophilic substitution) and Alkene (electrophilic addition) mechanism.

  1. the carbon in methane, \(\ce{CH4}\);
  2. the carbonyl carbon (\(\ce{C=O}\)) in propanone, \(\ce{(CH3)2CO}\);
  3. each carbon in ethyne, \(\ce{C2H2}\).

Archetype: shape from σ-domain count across sp³/sp²/sp carbons.

Trigger: "shape/angle around this carbon" in an organic molecule → count σ bonds only (2→linear, 3→trigonal planar, 4→tetrahedral).

Trap: counting the π bond of \(\ce{C=O}\) or \(\ce{C#C}\) as a separate domain and getting the wrong shape. π never adds a domain.

Question 9[4]
The \(\ce{C-C}\) bond length is 0.154 nm and \(\ce{C=C}\) is 0.134 nm.
(a) Explain why \(\ce{C=C}\) is shorter than \(\ce{C-C}\). [2]
(b) The \(\ce{C=C}\) bond is stronger overall, yet alkenes are more reactive than alkanes. Explain this apparent contradiction. [2]
Reveal worked solution

(a) A double bond has two shared pairs (1σ + 1π) between the nuclei rather than one. The greater shared electron density pulls the two carbon nuclei closer together, so \(\ce{C=C}\) is shorter. A1 more bonding electrons/pairs, A1 stronger attraction pulls nuclei closer.

(b) Reactivity is not about total bond strength but about accessibility of electrons. The π component is exposed above/below the axis and is the weaker part of the double bond, giving a region of high electron density that is readily attacked by electrophiles. Alkanes have only strong, non-polar σ bonds with no such site. A1 π is exposed/electron-rich, A1 attacked by electrophiles / alkanes have no such site.

Archetype & trap

Archetype: bond order → length/strength, and the split between "total strength" and "site of reactivity."

Trigger: a length/strength comparison, or "why is the alkene more reactive" → separate the σ (strong, buried) from the π (weaker, exposed).

Trap: claiming the stronger bond must be less reactive full-stop. The reactivity mark is specifically the exposed π electron density.

Question 10[7]
Ethanol, \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\).
(a) State the shape and bond angle around the oxygen atom. [2]
(b) Explain, in terms of intermolecular forces, why ethanol is completely miscible with water whereas ethane is not. [3]
(c) Predict, with reasoning, how the boiling point of ethanol compares with that of its structural isomer methoxymethane, \(\ce{CH3OCH3}\). [2]
Reveal worked solution

(a) The O has 2 bonding pairs (to C and to H) + 2 lone pairs = 4 domains → bent/V-shaped, ≈104.5° (two lone pairs squeeze the angle below 109.5°). A1 bent, A1 ≈104.5° with lone-pair reason.

(b) Ethanol's \(\ce{O-H}\) lets it form hydrogen bonds with water (its O–H to water's O, and water's O–H to ethanol's O lone pair). These H-bonds to water release enough energy to make mixing favourable → miscible. Ethane has only \(\ce{C-H}\) bonds (non-polar), can form only weak id–id forces with water and cannot H-bond, so it cannot disrupt water's hydrogen-bonded network → immiscible. A1 ethanol H-bonds to water, A1 mechanism/both directions, A1 ethane only id–id, no H-bond.

(c) Ethanol has an \(\ce{O-H}\) group so its molecules hydrogen-bond to each other; methoxymethane has no O–H (O sits between two carbons), so its molecules cannot H-bond with each other — only weaker pd–pd/id–id. Therefore ethanol boils higher than methoxymethane. A1 ethanol H-bonds / methoxymethane cannot, A1 ethanol higher bp.

Archetype & trap

Archetype: full-molecule synthesis — VSEPR at O + H-bonding to a solvent + isomer bp comparison, all from bonding.

Trigger: a real organic molecule with an O–H/N–H and a "solubility / miscibility / isomer bp" ask → check for H-bond donor and acceptor, and whether an isomer loses the O–H.

Trap: for (c), forgetting that an ether has no O–H so cannot self-H-bond even though it contains oxygen; for (b), not describing H-bonding in both directions with water.

Gateway: parts (b)–(c) are exactly how Hydroxy Compounds questions test alcohol solubility and boiling point.
