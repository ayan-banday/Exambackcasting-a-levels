**1. Ice is less dense than water.** *(memorise — drill prompt 2)*
In ice, each H₂O molecule is hydrogen-bonded to **four** others in a rigid, **open tetrahedral lattice** with large empty spaces. This holds the molecules **further apart** than in liquid water, where they pack more randomly and closely. A given mass of ice therefore occupies a **larger volume → lower density**, so ice floats.

**2. XeF₂ and XeF₄ — and the polarity rule you're missing.**
- **XeF₂:** 5 electron pairs (2 bond, 3 lone). The **3 lone pairs go equatorial**, the 2 bonding pairs go axial → **linear, 180°**. (Square planar is XeF₄, not XeF₂.)
- **XeF₄:** 6 electron pairs (4 bond, 2 lone). The 2 lone pairs sit opposite each other → **square planar, 90°**.
- **Polarity fix:** a lone pair does **not** automatically make a molecule polar. What decides it is whether the **bond dipoles cancel by symmetry**. XeF₂ (linear) and XeF₄ (square planar) are **symmetric → non-polar**, despite having lone pairs. Your rule "any lone pair → polar" works for NH₃/H₂O/SO₂ but **fails for XeF₂ and XeF₄**. Correct test: *do the bond dipoles cancel?* Yes → non-polar.

**3. Metallic vs ionic melting point — don't assume.**
General order: **simple molecular < metallic ≈ ionic < giant covalent.** Metallic vs ionic depends on the actual substances. For **Na vs NaCl → Na (metallic) < NaCl (ionic)**: Na⁺ contributes only one delocalised electron with a +1 charge (relatively weak metallic bonding), whereas NaCl has strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the +1 and −1 ions throughout a giant lattice. Compare the specific bonding strength; don't assume metallic beats ionic.

**4. Lattice energy — the definition you skipped (verbatim).** *(memorise — drill prompt 1)*
Lattice energy is **the energy released when one mole of a solid ionic compound is formed from its constituent gaseous ions.** More exothermic (more negative) = stronger ionic bonding. It is proportional to **(charge of cation × charge of anion) / (sum of the ionic radii).**
- Radii: **Mg²⁺ < Na⁺** (Mg²⁺ has the higher nuclear charge pulling the same electrons in tighter — both are isoelectronic). **O²⁻ < Cl⁻** (O²⁻ has one fewer electron shell). Higher charges **and** smaller radii → MgO lattice energy ≫ NaCl.

**5. Graphite conductivity — assemble it cleanly.**
Each C in graphite is **sp² hybridised**, σ-bonded to 3 other C in layers. The leftover electron in the **unhybridised p-orbital** forms a **delocalised π-electron system** across the layer. These delocalised electrons are **mobile charge carriers**, so graphite conducts **along** the layers. It does **not** conduct perpendicular to them because there are no delocalised electrons **between** the layers (only weak dispersion forces). Diamond: each C is **sp³**, all four electrons in localised σ-bonds → **no mobile charge carriers → does not conduct.**

## GQ1
(a) XeF₂: linear, **180°**, 3 lone pairs equatorial, 2 bond pairs axial. XeF₄: square planar, **90°**, 2 lone pairs opposite each other (axial). [4]
(b) Both **non-polar**: in each the **bond dipoles cancel due to the symmetrical shape** (linear / square planar). Having lone pairs does not make them polar. [3]
(c) XeF₄ has **more electrons** → larger, more polarisable electron cloud → stronger **instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (dispersion)** forces → more energy to overcome → higher bp. [2]

## GQ2
(a) Each H₂O hydrogen-bonds to **4 others in an open tetrahedral lattice** with empty spaces → molecules held further apart than in liquid water → larger volume per mass → lower density. [3]
(b) Only H₂O forms **hydrogen bonds** (H bonded to highly electronegative O + lone pair on O); the others have weaker pd–pd/dispersion. More energy to overcome H-bonds → higher bp. [2]
(c) H₂O forms ~2 H-bonds per molecule (2 δ+H + 2 lone pairs); HF forms only ~1 (1 δ+H). Greater **extent** of H-bonding in water → higher bp. [2]

## GQ3
(a) Energy released when **one mole of a solid ionic compound is formed from its constituent gaseous ions.** [2]
(b) Mg²⁺ has a **greater charge (+2 vs +1)** and contributes **more delocalised electrons (2 vs 1)**, with a smaller cation radius → stronger metallic bonding → higher mp. [2]
(c) Na (metallic, +1, weak) < NaCl (ionic, strong electrostatic attraction between +1/−1 ions) < MgO (ionic, **+2/−2 charges and smaller radii → far larger lattice energy**). More energy needed at each step. [4]
(d) Molten: ions are **mobile → carry charge**. Solid: ions held in fixed positions in the lattice → no mobile charge carriers. [2]

## GQ4
(a) C is **sp² hybridised**, σ-bonded to 3 C; the unhybridised p-electron forms a **delocalised π system** within the layer → **mobile charge carriers** conduct along layers. No delocalised electrons **between** layers (only dispersion) → no conduction perpendicular. [4]
(b) Graphite: layers held by **weak dispersion forces**, slide easily → soft. Diamond: **3-D network of strong covalent bonds** in all directions → hard. [3]
(c) Giant covalent (giant molecular); each Si covalently bonded to **4 O**, each O to 2 Si; many **strong covalent bonds** to break → very high mp. [2]

## GQ5 (indicative)
Agree in part: for **non-polar simple molecular** substances, bp rises with Mr because more electrons → stronger dispersion (e.g. Cl₂ < Br₂ < I₂; noble gases down the group). **But the claim fails** because: (1) **hydrogen bonding** — H₂O (Mr 18) boils far higher than much heavier H₂S/HCl; (2) **shape/surface area** — straight-chain pentane boils higher than its branched isomer of the same Mr (more contact area → stronger dispersion); (3) **polarity** — a polar molecule can boil higher than a non-polar one of similar Mr. Judgement: Mr is a **major factor via dispersion, but not the only one** — H-bonding and shape can override it.
