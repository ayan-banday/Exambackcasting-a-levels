The mole toolkit — memorise cold (Data Booklet values)
\( n = \dfrac{m}{M_r} \)mass ÷ molar mass (g, g mol⁻¹)
\( n = c\,V \)concentration × volume — V in dm³ (÷1000 from cm³)
\( n = \dfrac{V}{V_m} \)gases: \( V_m = \mathbf{22.7}\ \text{dm}^3\,\text{mol}^{-1} \) at s.t.p. (273 K, 10⁵ Pa), \( 24.0 \) at r.t.p.
\( n = \dfrac{N}{L} \)particles ÷ Avogadro constant \( L = 6.02\times10^{23} \)
% yield\( \dfrac{\text{actual}}{\text{theoretical}}\times100 \)  ·  % by mass \( \dfrac{n\times A_r}{M_r}\times100 \)
Two habits that are worth real marks: carry ≥ 5 s.f. through every intermediate value and round only at the end; always write units and the right sig figs — both are discrete marks.

Question 1

Example
(i) Calculate the amount, in mol, of \(\ce{NaOH}\) in a \( 4.00\ \text{g} \) sample. [1]
(ii) State the number of formula units present. [1]
(iii) Calculate the volume, at s.t.p., occupied by \( 4.00\ \text{g} \) of \(\ce{CO2}\) gas. [2]
Solution with mark scheme
Step 1 — (i)
\( M_r(\ce{NaOH}) = 23.0 + 16.0 + 1.0 = 40.0 \). Then \( n = m/M_r \). A1
\( n = \dfrac{4.00}{40.0} = 0.100\ \text{mol} \)
Step 2 — (ii)
\( N = n \times L \). A1
\( N = 0.100 \times 6.02\times10^{23} = 6.02\times10^{22} \)
Step 3 — (iii)
\( M_r(\ce{CO2}) = 44.0 \Rightarrow n = 4.00/44.0 = 0.09091\ \text{mol} \). M1
\( V = n \times V_m = 0.09091 \times 22.7 = 2.06\ \text{dm}^3 \)  A1
Trap: at s.t.p. \( V_m = 22.7 \), not 22.4. The old 22.4 value is pre-2022 and is a marked error now.

Practice
\( 0.240\ \text{g} \) of magnesium reacts completely with excess dilute \(\ce{HCl}\). Calculate the volume of hydrogen produced, measured at r.t.p. [3]
Solution
Step 1
Equation + moles of Mg. M1
\(\ce{Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2}\); \( n(\ce{Mg}) = 0.240/24.3 = 0.009877\ \text{mol} \)
Step 2
Mole ratio \(\ce{Mg}:\ce{H2} = 1:1\). M1
\( n(\ce{H2}) = 0.009877\ \text{mol} \)
Step 3
r.t.p. \(\Rightarrow V_m = 24.0\). A1

Question 3

Example
\( 0.300\ \text{g} \) of a gaseous hydrocarbon is completely burned, giving \( 0.880\ \text{g} \) of \(\ce{CO2}\) and \( 0.540\ \text{g} \) of \(\ce{H2O}\). The relative molecular mass of the hydrocarbon is \( 30.0 \). Determine its molecular formula. [3]
Solution with mark scheme

Note: The school method sets the hydrocarbon as CₓHᵧ from the start, finds moles of compound, then divides element moles by compound moles to get x and y. This is faster than empirical-then-scale.

Working table:

SpeciesMass (g)÷ Ar/MrMoles÷ n(compound)x or y
Hydrocarbon CₓHᵧ0.300÷30.00.01000——
C (from CO₂)—0.880÷44.00.02000÷0.01000x = 2
H (from H₂O)—0.540÷18.0 ×20.06000÷0.01000y = 6
Step 1 — Set up the formula
The compound is CₓHᵧ. Find its molar amount first. M1
\( n(\ce{C}_x\ce{H}_y) = \dfrac{0.300}{30.0} = 0.01000\ \text{mol} \)
Step 2 — Find moles of C and H
All C ends up in CO₂; all H ends up in H₂O. M1
\( n(\ce{C}) = n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.880/44.0 = 0.02000\ \text{mol} \)
\( n(\ce{H}) = 2 \times n(\ce{H2O}) = 2 \times 0.540/18.0 = 0.06000\ \text{mol} \)
Step 3 — Divide by moles of compound to get x and y
This gives the number of each element per molecule. A1
\( x = \dfrac{n(\ce{C})}{n(\ce{C}_x\ce{H}_y)} = \dfrac{0.02000}{0.01000} = 2 \)
\( y = \dfrac{n(\ce{H})}{n(\ce{C}_x\ce{H}_y)} = \dfrac{0.06000}{0.01000} = 6 \)
\( \mathbf{\ce{C2H6}} \)
Your own note: carry 5 s.f. (0.01000, 0.02000, 0.06000) through to the divide step — premature rounding shifts the last figure and loses the A1. Also: this method does NOT compute empirical formula first; it goes straight to molecular formula using Mr. Much faster.

Question 4

Example
\( 8.00\ \text{g} \) of \(\ce{Fe2O3}\) is heated with \( 3.00\ \text{g} \) of carbon: \(\ce{2Fe2O3 + 3C -> 4Fe + 3CO2}\).
(i) Show that \(\ce{Fe2O3}\) is the limiting reagent. [2]
(ii) Calculate the maximum mass of iron obtainable. [1]
(iii) If \( 4.50\ \text{g} \) of iron is actually obtained, calculate the percentage yield. [1]
Solution with mark scheme
Step 1 — (i) Find moles of each reactant
M1
\( n(\ce{Fe2O3}) = 8.00/159.6 = 0.05013\ \text{mol} \)
\( n(\ce{C}) = 3.00/12.0 = 0.2500\ \text{mol} \)
Step 2 — Calculate C needed to react with all the Fe₂O₃
From the equation, \(\ce{Fe2O3}:\ce{C} = 2:3\). A1
\( n(\ce{C})_\text{needed} = 0.05013 \times \tfrac{3}{2} = 0.07520\ \text{mol} \)
We have 0.2500 mol, which is more than 0.07520 mol. C is in excess, so \(\ce{Fe2O3}\) is limiting.
Step 3 — (ii) Maximum Fe from limiting reagent
\(\ce{2Fe2O3 -> 4Fe}\), ratio \(1:2\). A1
\( n(\ce{Fe}) = 0.05013 \times 2 = 0.1003\ \text{mol} \Rightarrow m = 0.1003 \times 55.8 = 5.59\ \text{g} \)
Step 4 — (iii) % yield
Percentage = (actual ÷ theoretical) × 100%. A1
\( \dfrac{4.50}{5.59}\times100 = 80.5\% \)

Practice
\( 5.00\ \text{g} \) of nitrogen reacts with \( 2.00\ \text{g} \) of hydrogen: \(\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}\). Identify the limiting reagent and calculate the maximum mass of ammonia produced. [3]
Solution
Step 1
Moles of each. M1
\( n(\ce{N2}) = 5.00/28.0 = 0.1786\ \text{mol} \); \( n(\ce{H2}) = 2.00/2.0 = 1.000\ \text{mol} \)
Step 2
\(\ce{H2}\) needed for all \(\ce{N2}\) (ratio \(1:3\)) = 0.1786 × 3 = 0.536; we have 1.000. M1
\(\ce{H2}\) in excess → \(\ce{N2}\) limiting
Step 3
\(\ce{N2 -> 2NH3}\). A1
\( n(\ce{NH3}) = 0.1786 \times 2 = 0.3571\ \text{mol} \Rightarrow m = 0.3571 \times 17.0 = 6.07\ \text{g} \)

Archetype & trap
Archetype: "two masses given → find limiting reagent, then product mass / % yield." Trap: you must compare using the equation ratio, not raw moles — the reagent with fewer moles is not always limiting. Always base the product amount on the limiting reagent, and carry 5 s.f. into the % yield to avoid rounding loss.

Question 5

Example
\( 25.0\ \text{cm}^3 \) of a gaseous hydrocarbon is burned in excess oxygen. All volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure. The reaction produces \( 75.0\ \text{cm}^3 \) of \(\ce{CO2}\) and \( 100.0\ \text{cm}^3 \) of water vapour. Deduce the formula of the hydrocarbon. [3]
Solution with mark scheme
Step 1 — Avogadro's law
At the same T and P, volume ratio = mole ratio. Scale to 1 volume of hydrocarbon. M1
\( \ce{C}_x\ce{H}_y : \ce{CO2} : \ce{H2O} = 25.0 : 75.0 : 100.0 = 1 : 3 : 4 \)
Step 2 — Deduce x and y
1 hydrocarbon → 3 \(\ce{CO2}\) means \(x = 3\); → 4 \(\ce{H2O}\) means \(y/2 = 4\). M1
\( x = 3,\quad y = 8 \)
Step 3 — State formula
A1
\( \mathbf{\ce{C3H8}} \) (propane)

\( 10.0\ \text{cm}^3 \) of a gaseous hydrocarbon requires \( 65.0\ \text{cm}^3 \) of oxygen for complete combustion and produces \( 40.0\ \text{cm}^3 \) of carbon dioxide, all volumes at the same T and P. Deduce the molecular formula. [3]
Solution
Step 1 — Scale ratios to 1 volume hydrocarbon
M1
\( \ce{C}_x\ce{H}_y : \ce{O2} : \ce{CO2} = 1 : 6.5 : 4 \Rightarrow x = 4 \)
Step 2 — Use the combustion ratio
For a hydrocarbon, \( \ce{O2} = x + \dfrac{y}{4} \). M1
\( 6.5 = 4 + \dfrac{y}{4} \Rightarrow \dfrac{y}{4} = 2.5 \Rightarrow y = 10 \)
Step 3
A1
\( \mathbf{\ce{C4H10}} \)

Archetype & trap
Archetype: "gaseous combustion, volumes at same T,P → formula (Avogadro's law)." Trap: volume ratio = mole ratio only for gases at the same conditions — water counts only if it is stated as vapour/gas. If liquid water, drop it from the gas ratio. The oxygen balance \(\ce{O2} = x + y/4\) is the usual second mark.

Scale to base, then \( c = n/V \). A1

\( c = 4.480\times10^{-3}/0.02500 = 0.179\ \text{mol dm}^{-3} \)

Acid that reacted with the carbonate; \(\ce{CaCO3 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + H2O + CO2}\). M1

\( n(\ce{CaCO3}) = 0.01520/2 = 7.600\times10^{-3} \)

Mass then % purity, \( M_r(\ce{CaCO3}) = 100.1 \). A1

Question 9

Example
State the oxidation number of the named element in each species:
(i) Mn in \(\ce{MnO4-}\)   (ii) Cr in \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\)   (iii) S in \(\ce{S2O3^2-}\)   (iv) O in \(\ce{H2O2}\). [4]
(v) In \(\ce{Cl2 + 2Br- -> 2Cl- + Br2}\), identify the species oxidised, the species reduced, the oxidising agent and the reducing agent. [2]
Solution with mark scheme
Step 1 — (i)–(iv)
Sum of O.N. = overall charge; O is normally \(-2\) (\(-1\) in peroxides), H is \(+1\). A1 ×4
(i) \(\text{Mn} + 4(-2) = -1 \Rightarrow \mathbf{+7}\)
(ii) \(2\text{Cr} + 7(-2) = -2 \Rightarrow \text{Cr} = \mathbf{+6}\)
(iii) \(2\text{S} + 3(-2) = -2 \Rightarrow \text{S} = \mathbf{+2}\) (average)
(iv) peroxide: \(2(+1) + 2\text{O} = 0 \Rightarrow \text{O} = \mathbf{-1}\)
Step 2 — (v)
Track the O.N. change. A1 A1
Cl: \(0 \to -1\) (reduced) → \(\ce{Cl2}\) is the oxidising agent.
Br: \(-1 \to 0\) (oxidised) → \(\ce{Br-}\) is the reducing agent.
Trap: the oxidising agent is the species reduced (it takes electrons). Name the whole species (\(\ce{Cl2}\), \(\ce{Br-}\)), not just the element.
Solution hidden — click Reveal
Practice
Chlorine reacts with cold aqueous sodium hydroxide: \(\ce{Cl2 + 2NaOH -> NaCl + NaClO + H2O}\). Using oxidation numbers, show that this is a disproportionation reaction. [3]
Solution
Step 1
O.N. of Cl in each species. M1
\(\ce{Cl2}: 0\);   \(\ce{NaCl}: -1\);   \(\ce{NaClO}: \text{Cl} = +1\) (Na \(+1\), O \(-2\))
Step 2
Same element goes both up and down. A1 A1
Cl \(0 \to -1\) (reduced) and \(0 \to +1\) (oxidised) — one element simultaneously oxidised and reduced = disproportionation.

Question 10

Example
Acidified potassium manganate(VII) oxidises iron(II) ions. Construct the two half-equations and combine them into the overall ionic equation. State the colour change. [4]
Solution with mark scheme
Step 1 — reduction
Balance O with \(\ce{H2O}\), H with \(\ce{H+}\), charge with \(\ce{e-}\). M1
\(\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5e- -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O}\)
Step 2 — oxidation
Iron(II) → iron(III). B1
\(\ce{Fe^2+ -> Fe^3+ + e-}\)
Step 3 — combine
Multiply the Fe half-equation by 5 so electrons cancel. M1
\(\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5Fe^2+ -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O + 5Fe^3+}\)
Step 4 — colour
Self-indicating. A1
Purple \(\ce{MnO4-}\) → colourless \(\ce{Mn^2+}\) (end point = first permanent pink).
Solution hidden — click Reveal
Practice
Acidified potassium dichromate(VI) oxidises iodide ions to iodine. Construct both half-equations and the overall ionic equation, and state the colour change. [4]
Solution
Step 1 — reduction
Dichromate → chromium(III); note the \(2\ \ce{Cr}\) and \(6\ \ce{e-}\). M1
\(\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ + 6e- -> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}\)
Step 2 — oxidation
Iodide → iodine. B1
\(\ce{2I- -> I2 + 2e-}\)
Step 3 — combine
×3 the iodide half so \(6\ \ce{e-}\) cancel. M1
\(\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ + 6I- -> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O + 3I2}\)
Step 4 — colour
A1
Orange \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\) → green \(\ce{Cr^3+}\).
Solution hidden — click Reveal
Archetype & trap
Archetype: "construct + combine half-equations in acid." The three you must know cold: \(\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5e-}\), \(\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ + 6e-}\), \(\ce{Fe^3+ + e-}\). Trap: multiply so the electrons cancel exactly before adding — leftover electrons in a final equation is an automatic zero for that mark. Your own note: use the O.N. change to get the electron count, don't re-derive from scratch.

Question 11

Example
Construct the half-equation for the reduction of chlorate(I) ions, \(\ce{ClO-}\), to chloride ions, \(\ce{Cl-}\), in alkaline solution. [2]
