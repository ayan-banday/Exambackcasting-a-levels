### The practical consequence of spanning two Core Ideas

**Paper 1 sweeps the syllabus in learning-outcome order, not in Book order.** On the 9477 Specimen
Paper 1 the Book 4 items landed at **Q3** (viruses and the cell theory, a Core Idea 1 outcome), **Q10**
(prokaryotic against eukaryotic genome, a Core Idea 2 outcome, shared with Genome Organisation),
**Q11** (HIV against influenza reproductive cycles) and **Q12** (bacterial gene transfer). `[OFFICIAL]`,
counted from the specimen.

Two things follow. First, **the virus-structure and cell-theory material sits early on the paper**,
between cell-structure and enzyme questions, because LO 1(e) and 1(f) are Core Idea 1 outcomes. The
bacteria and viral-genetics material sits in the Core Idea 2 block. They will not arrive together.
Second, **Q10 is a Book 4 outcome answered with Book 5 vocabulary**, since it turns on introns and
splicing. A Book 4 revision pass that never touches genome organisation loses it.

### The one exclusion the syllabus states outright

There is exactly one, and §5 works out how far it reaches. `[OFFICIAL]`

**"including the role of F plasmids but not Hfr"** (LO 2g). This is one of only a handful of places in
the whole 9477 syllabus where a named mechanism is cut by name. It is also the single largest time
saving available in this Book, because of what it drags out with it.

**Note what the syllabus does NOT exclude,** because Book 4 is unusual in this respect. There is no
"details of the mechanism are not required" clause anywhere in these eight outcomes. LO 2(e) says
"**outlining** the reproductive cycles", and LO 2(g) says "**outline** the mechanism ... and
**describe** how", which set the expected depth through the command words rather than through an
exclusion. Outline means the ordered steps without elaboration; describe means the ordered steps stated
in full. Do not over-apply the Book 3 reflex of hunting for exclusions here. There is one, and it is
about Hfr.

## 1. Content Node Map (coverage checklist)

Every node the exam can test in Book 4, grouped by the cluster that should teach it. Convergence
requires no UNTOUCHED node. LO letters are `[OFFICIAL]`.

### Cluster 01, viruses as structures and as a problem for the cell theory (LOs 1e, 1f, 2d viral half)

- [ ] **The four structural components (1e)**: genome, capsid, envelope, enzymes, with which are
      universal and which are not. The answer is always built the same way, and the envelope and its
      glycoproteins are the enveloped-virus half of the outcome.
- [ ] **Capsid and capsomeres (1e)**: protein coat built from identical subunits, helical or
      icosahedral. Schemes reject "protein coat" and "capsomere" where **capsid** is wanted, and reject
      "protein" where **glycoprotein** is wanted.
- [ ] **Envelope (1e)**: host-derived phospholipid bilayer taken at budding, with viral glycoproteins
      inserted, and its protective role. Schemes reject "protein envelope", "membrane" and "double
      bilayer" as the identification of an envelope.
- [ ] **Bacteriophage structure (1e)**: head, tail, tail fibres, and the contractile against
      non-contractile distinction between T4 and lambda. The outcome names bacteriophages explicitly.
- [ ] **Interpreting an unfamiliar virus drawing or micrograph (1e)**: the outcome says "and interpret
      drawings and photographs of them" in those words, and 45.7% of in-scope structured parts are
      figure-led.
- [ ] **Viral enzymes (1e, 2e)**: lysozyme, neuraminidase, and the four polymerases with the "dependent"
      naming convention. Which enzyme a virus must carry is dictated by its genome type.
- [ ] **Viral genome axes (2d)**: DNA or RNA but never both, single or double stranded, linear or
      circular, and the size range. The seven-group table, restricted to groups I, V and VI.
- [ ] **Positive against negative sense RNA (2d, 2e)**: positive sense is directly translatable,
      negative sense must first be used as a template for a positive-sense copy. This is the hinge of
      the influenza cycle.
- [ ] **Obligate intracellular parasite (1f)**: what a virus must take from the host, which is
      building blocks, protein-synthesising machinery and ATP.
- [ ] **Virion against virus state (1f)**: metabolically inert outside, active inside.
- [ ] **The three statements of the cell theory, each answered both ways (1f)**: cells are the smallest
      unit of life, all cells come from pre-existing cells, all living organisms are composed of cells.
      This is the only "discuss" outcome in the Book and the corpus files a 14-mark essay on it.
- [ ] **The characteristics of life a virus does and does not show (1f)**: reproduction, metabolism and
      evolution on one side, no growth, no excretion, no nutrition, no ATP synthesis and no response to
      stimuli in the extracellular state on the other.

### Cluster 02, viral reproductive cycles (LO 2e, all three sub-clauses)

- [ ] **The five general steps (2e)**: adsorption, penetration, synthesis and replication, assembly,
      release. Every cycle answer hangs on this frame, and the differences between viruses are
      differences within these five slots.
- [ ] **Host range and specificity (2e)**: complementary fit between a viral surface protein and a
      specific host receptor. Schemes want **complementary** and want the receptor located on the host
      cell.
- [ ] **The lytic cycle in five steps (2e i)**: tail fibres to receptors, sheath contraction and
      lysozyme, host DNA degraded for nucleotides, host polymerases and ribosomes hijacked, assembly,
      lysis with water entering by osmosis. The capsid stays outside the cell wall.
- [ ] **Virulent against temperate phage (2e i)**: which cycles each can run.
- [ ] **The lysogenic cycle (2e i)**: lambda named in the outcome. Circularisation, insertion at the
      prophage insertion site by genetic recombination with no loss of host DNA, replication along with
      the host chromosome at every division, then spontaneous induction on an environmental trigger.
- [ ] **Why lysogeny is a survival strategy (2e i)**: the prophage is propagated without killing the
      host and without needing new infections.
- [ ] **The influenza cycle (2e ii)**: haemagglutinin to sialic acid, receptor-mediated endocytosis,
      endosomal acidification triggering the HA conformational change and fusion, replicase copying
      negative to positive sense, positive sense used both as mRNA and as template for new genomes,
      budding, neuraminidase cleaving sialic acid to release.
- [ ] **Why influenza must carry its own replicase (2e ii)**: animal cells cannot synthesise RNA from
      an RNA template. The school's own Fig. 20 caption says so and it is a stock explain mark.
- [ ] **The HIV cycle (2e iii)**: gp120 to CD4, conformational change, co-receptor, gp41 and fusion,
      two passes of reverse transcription with RNase H between them, integrase and the provirus,
      latency, transcription giving both genome and mRNA, budding, protease-driven maturation.
- [ ] **Why HIV is a retrovirus (2e iii)**: the strictest scheme in the corpus wants single-stranded
      positive-sense RNA, reverse transcribed to double-stranded DNA, integrated as a provirus.
- [ ] **HIV against influenza, point for point (2e ii and iii)**: this is **9477 Specimen P1 Q11**
      exactly, and it is the most efficient single comparison in the Book.
- [ ] **Exit route by virus (2e)**: lysis for phages, budding for HIV and influenza. Schemes police
      budding against exocytosis hard and the rule depends on the virus.

### Cluster 03, variation in viral genomes (LO 2f)

- [ ] **The three stated mechanisms (2f)**: mutation, recombination, reassortment. The school introduces
      recombination and then never uses it, so it needs supplying.
- [ ] **Antigenic shift, defined by reassortment (2f)**: two or more strains co-infecting one host,
      segments reshuffled at packaging, a new combination of haemagglutinin and neuraminidase. **Two or
      more strains is not optional.** See the trap model.
- [ ] **Why influenza is susceptible to shift specifically (2f)**: eight separate segments make
      reassortment mechanically possible. A non-segmented virus cannot do it.
- [ ] **Antigenic drift, defined by mutation accumulation (2f)**: point mutations in the haemagglutinin
      and neuraminidase genes, one strain, gradual.
- [ ] **Why viral polymerases introduce errors (2f)**: the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase and reverse
      transcriptase have no proofreading exonuclease activity of their own. Do not repeat the school's
      version, which blames the host.
- [ ] **The consequence chain (2f)**: altered base sequence, altered amino acid sequence, altered 3D
      conformation of the surface protein, antibodies no longer complementary, no recognition, immune
      escape and vaccine failure.
- [ ] **Shift against drift, six axes (2f)**: number of strains, mechanism, nature of change, rate of
      occurrence, effect of host immunity, cross-species transmission. The school's page 43 table is
      already in mark-scheme shape.
- [ ] **Pandemic against seasonal epidemic (2f)**: shift gives a population with no pre-existing
      immunity, drift leaves some immunity intact.

### Cluster 04, the bacterial cell and the prokaryotic genome (LOs 1a, 1d, 2d prokaryotic half)

- [ ] **The five features LO 1(d) names**: small and unicellular, peptidoglycan cell wall, circular DNA,
      70S ribosomes, lack of membrane-bound organelles. Learn the outcome's own five, then add nucleoid
      and plasmids as the credited extras.
- [ ] **Nucleoid (1d, 2d)**: the DNA-containing region with no surrounding membrane.
- [ ] **Bacterial chromosome (2d)**: single, circular, double-stranded, essential genes.
- [ ] **Packing of DNA in a prokaryote (2d)**: the outcome names "packing of DNA" explicitly, so
      histone-like proteins, looped domains and supercoiling are required even though none of those
      three words appears in the syllabus.
- [ ] **Plasmids (2d, 2g)**: small, circular, double-stranded, extrachromosomal, non-essential, own
      origin of replication so replication is independent, more than one per cell, carrying beneficial
      genes that confer a selective advantage.
- [ ] **Prokaryotic against eukaryotic genome, on the outcome's own axes (2d)**. **This is what the
      specimen actually asked**, at Paper 3 Q1(a)(i) and (ii), for four marks. `[OFFICIAL]`
- [ ] **The cell theory itself (1a)**: the three statements, stated in the outcome's own words, because
      LO 1(f) then asks how viruses challenge them.
- [ ] **Endosymbiont evidence**: circular DNA not associated with histones, 70S ribosomes, division by a
      splitting process, own transcription and translation machinery, inner membrane enzymes resembling
      prokaryotic plasma membranes. **The specimen asked for two of these for two marks** at P3
      Q1(a)(iii), and the word "endosymbiont" appears nowhere in the syllabus. `[OFFICIAL]`

### Cluster 05, transmission and variation in prokaryotic genomes (LO 2g)

- [ ] **Binary fission in outline (2g)**: chromosome attached to the membrane, single origin,
      bidirectional and semi-conservative replication, elongation separating the chromosomes, septal
      ring and septum, membrane invagination, two genetically identical daughter cells.
- [ ] **Why binary fission cannot generate variation (2g)**: it is the sentence that sets up the whole
      of the rest of the outcome.
- [ ] **Vertical against horizontal gene transfer (2g)**: the frame the outcome is built on, and the
      hinge of **9477 Specimen P1 Q12**.
- [ ] **Homologous recombination (2g)**: how transferred DNA becomes heritable, requiring high sequence
      homology, with the replaced recipient segment excised and degraded. Required by transformation
      and both kinds of transduction, and not by conjugation.
- [ ] **Transformation (2g)**: naked DNA from a lysed donor, taken up by a **competent** cell through
      competence factors, then homologous recombination, then a recombinant cell.
- [ ] **Generalised transduction (2g)**: virulent phage, lytic cycle, host chromosome degraded, a
      **random** fragment mispackaged during assembly, defective phage, injection into a second host,
      homologous recombination.
- [ ] **Specialised transduction (2g)**: temperate phage, prophage, induction, **imprecise excision**,
      so only genes **adjacent to the prophage insertion site** travel, then either prophage integration
      or homologous recombination in the recipient.
- [ ] **Generalised against specialised, three axes (2g)**: type of phage, reproductive cycle, which
      donor genes can move. The pairing must be automatic.
- [ ] **Conjugation (2g)**: F factor, F+ and F- cells, sex pilus for contact, **cytoplasmic mating
      bridge** for transfer, endonuclease nick in one strand of the F plasmid, single strand transferred,
      complementary strand synthesised in both cells, both cells end F+.
- [ ] **The rolling circle mechanism and the origin of transfer (2g)**: **not in the school notes at
      all**, and credited by at least eight prelim schemes in the corpus. See §3.
- [ ] **The three mechanisms compared (2g)**: source of DNA, what is transferred, whether cell contact
      is needed, whether a phage vector is needed, whether homologous recombination follows. 8.3% of
      in-scope records touch two or more of the three mechanisms in one question.
- [ ] **Why variation matters (2g)**: new alleles, selective advantage under a selection pressure,
      natural selection, and antibiotic resistance as the worked case. Schemes reject the selective
      advantage claim without the gaining of advantageous genes.

|---|---|---|---|

Four structural facts follow, and each changes how to drill.

1. **Not one of the four is plain recall.** Q3 requires you to test each statement against a criterion
   of life. Q10 requires you to eliminate the statement that does not explain the data. Q11 requires
   both cycles held side by side. Q12 requires the stem's four listed features to be read as
   eliminators.
2. **Two of the four are answered with material from other Books.** Q10 needs introns and splicing from
   genome organisation, and Q3 hands you restriction enzymes, a term that appears **nowhere** in the
   9477 syllabus, as vocabulary you must simply understand.
3. **Q11 is the whole of LO 2(e) compressed into one mark.** It is the single most efficient revision
   target in the Book: build the HIV-against-influenza comparison once and it answers a stem shaped
   like this, a compare question, and half of an essay.
4. **Q12 is a trap about vertical against horizontal transfer**, and it is unresolved. Read the next
   note before drilling it.

**The contested key on Specimen P1 Q12, stated honestly.** `[INFERRED]` The question bank keys this
question **B, conjugation**, and tags that answer itself as inferred with no official key behind it.
I read it as **A,
binary fission**, and the disagreement is worth understanding because it is the exact ambiguity the
school's own chapter 4.2 carries (digest §5.6).

- The case for **A**: the original culture was grown **in ampicillin**, so every cell in it already
  carried the F plasmid. It was then inoculated into **sterile** broth, so no F- partner exists. The
  stated observation is that cell number rose more than 15 million-fold in eight hours, which is
  replication, and that "most, but not all" cells were resistant, which is the classic pattern of
  plasmid loss during growth without selection. Conjugation between two F+ cells does not occur
  efficiently. The school's own Concept Check 1 Q1 on page 27 of chapter 4.2 treats binary fission as
  the process that "always allows both chromosomal and non-chromosomal DNA to be transferred", and a
  prelim scheme in the corpus marks the statement "binary fission always allows chromosomal and
  non-chromosomal DNA to be transferred" as **true**.
- The case for **B**: the phrase "gene transfer from one bacterial cell to another" is the standard
  wording for horizontal transfer, and a prelim scheme elsewhere in the corpus rejects binary fission
  outright on the ground that it "is cell division, not gene transfer between cells".

**Both readings are defensible and no official key exists.** Do not drill this question as if it had a
settled answer. Drill the distinction it is testing instead, which is vertical against horizontal
transfer, and be able to argue either way from the stem.

## 3. Mark Scheme Logic (this topic specifically)

> **Hard cap on this whole section.** SEAB publishes no 9477 mark scheme, and the 9477 specimen papers
> in the corpus arrive without one. Every marking claim below is `[SCHOOL]`, distilled from JC prelim
> schemes reproduced verbatim in the question bank. They are expected to be broadly representative of
> Cambridge house style, not identical to it. Quoted reject and accept lines are verbatim from those
> schemes.

**1. Structure identification is marked at a level of precision most candidates do not reach.** From a
scheme on a coronavirus diagram: **A is the viral envelope**, with *"protein envelope"* and
*"phospholipid bilayer"* both rejected; **C is nucleoproteins / RNA-binding proteins**, with
*"histone"*, *"NS proteins"*, *"replicase"* and *"antigen"* all rejected; **D is glycoprotein**, with
*"HA"*, *"NA"*, *"proteins"* and *"antigen"* all rejected. The examiner comment on the same question is
blunt: *"this question part is poorly done. The number of students who are unable to identify the parts
or are misidentifying them is concerning. Students need to be more specific in their labelling of
structures C and D instead of simply writing them as 'proteins'."* `[SCHOOL]`

**2. Name the glycoproteins in full, and never abbreviate on a state question.** A scheme awards
**Haemagglutinin** and **Neuraminidase** and then prints *"(Reject: HA, NA)"*. `[SCHOOL]` This matters
because the school's own chapter uses HA and NA throughout after introducing them once.

**3. Viral glycoproteins are encoded by the viral genome, and saying they came from the host loses the
mark.** Examiner comment: *"this part of the question was not well attempted; the majority of students
simply wrote that both A and D came from the previous host. Students need to realise that glycoproteins
are necessary for viruses to infect host cells, hence they must be produced from the viral genome."*
The scheme prints *"Reject: both came from the previous host cell."* `[SCHOOL]` The envelope
phospholipid is host-derived; the glycoproteins embedded in it are not.

**4. RNA is not converted into DNA, and schemes reject the verb.** From an HIV enzyme scheme: the idea
of *"formation of (viral) DNA"* is credited, *"(reject the idea that RNA is 'turned into / converted
into' DNA)"*. `[SCHOOL]` A template is read and a new strand is synthesised. The same discipline
applies to the negative-sense to positive-sense step in influenza, where the school's own wording is
loose (digest §5.7).

**5. "HIV has RNA" is not enough for a retrovirus question, and two schools disagree on how strict to
be.** One scheme tells candidates they *"only need to state that HIV's genome is RNA in nature"*; a
second *"rejects simply 'HIV has RNA'"* and demands **single-stranded (+) RNA**, reverse transcribed to
double-stranded DNA, integrated as a provirus. `[SCHOOL]` **Write the strict version.** It scores under
both schemes.

**6. Budding and exocytosis are not interchangeable, and the right word depends on the virus.** One
scheme *"explicitly rejects 'exocytosis' for HIV and insists on budding"*. Four separate SARS-CoV-2
keys *"all require exocytosis and mark budding wrong"*. `[SCHOOL]` The rule that reconciles them is
that budding is for a virus acquiring its envelope from the cell surface membrane on the way out, and
exocytosis is for a virus that acquires its envelope internally and leaves inside a vesicle. **Book 4's
two named enveloped viruses, influenza and HIV, both bud.**

**7. Comparisons must be point-to-point on the same feature, and yes/no comparisons are rejected.** A
scheme *"rejects 'budding vs no budding', 'fusion vs no fusion', 'loss vs no loss of membrane'"* and
adds *"where there is an equivalent process, students should describe the difference in that feature"*.
Another rejects *"phage capsid left outside the cell for lytic phage vs nucleocapsid enters the cell"*
for the same reason. An examiner comment adds: *"A handful of students still did not give point-to-point
differences. Some don't know the meaning of contrast, which means differences."* `[SCHOOL]`

**8. Receptor binding needs the receptor located on the host cell, and needs the word complementary.**
A scheme prints *"Reject if the idea that the receptor is on the host cell is unclear/not present"* and
another prints *"Accept when there is mention of complementary receptors at least once."* `[SCHOOL]`

**9. Attachment is not entry, and a graph of free virus falling is attachment.** From a T4 adsorption
data question: the number of free viruses falls *"Due to attachment of viruses on the E. coli"*, with
*"Reject: viruses enter host cell"* printed beside it. `[SCHOOL]`

**10. The right transduction type must be deduced from the phage, and schemes reject the wrong
pairing in both directions.** *"REJECT specialised transduction, because the phage is T4 and the
genetic material is a 'random piece of bacterial genetic material'"* and *"Reject specialised
transduction, since the figure shows a lytic phage which usually facilitates generalised
transduction"*. Elsewhere, in a generalised-transduction key, *"(R: lytic phage)"* and
*"(R: lysogenic phage)"* appear where **virulent** and **temperate** are the wanted words. `[SCHOOL]`
Virulent and lytic go with generalised; temperate and lysogenic go with specialised.

**11. In conjugation, the cells are F+ and F-, not donor and recipient.** A scheme prints
*"Bacterium A: **F⁺** (REJECT DONOR); Bacterium B: **F⁻** (REJECT RECIPIENT)"*. `[SCHOOL]`

**12. The mating bridge is not the sex pilus, and schemes say so three separate times.** *"X:
**Conjugation bridge** (REJECT SEX PILUS)"*; *"(R: hollow pilus forms a mating bridge)"*; *"transferred
to the recipient / F⁻ cell through the mating bridge via the rolling circle mechanism (**R: sex
pilus**)"*; and one scheme spells it out inside its own answer text, *"a temporary mating / conjugation
bridge can be formed (**sex pilus ≠ conjugation bridge**)"*. `[SCHOOL]` The pilus makes contact and
retracts; the bridge carries the DNA.

**13. What is transferred in conjugation is the F plasmid, named exactly.** A scheme credits **F plasmid
(extra-chromosomal DNA)**, accepts *"F plasmid DNA"*, and prints *"R: F⁺ plasmid / plasmid"*.
`[SCHOOL]` The plasmid is not F+; the cell is.

**14. What is transferred in transformation is fragments of naked DNA, not a chromosome.** The same
scheme credits **fragments of foreign naked DNA** and prints *"R: chromosomal DNA (this is too big)"*.
`[SCHOOL]`

**15. The rolling circle mechanism and the origin of transfer are credited repeatedly, and the school
notes contain neither.** At least eight separate prelim schemes in the corpus name the **rolling circle
mechanism** as a mark point, several name **oriT** or the **origin of transfer**, and two name the
**tra genes**. Chapter 4.2 page 26 says only that one strand is *"nicked by an endonuclease"* and then
replicated *"by semi-conservative replication, catalysed by DNA polymerase"*. `[SCHOOL]` **This is the
largest single gap between the school notes and the schemes anywhere in Book 4.** Supply it.

**16. In binary fission the membrane invaginates, not the wall.** A scheme prints *"the cell membrane
folds inwards (invaginates) to form a double layer across the long axis of the cell. (**Reject: cell
wall invaginates, cell invaginates.**)"* `[SCHOOL]`

**17. Antigenic drift, not genetic drift.** A scheme credits **By antigenic drift** and prints
*"[Reject: genetic drift]"*. `[SCHOOL]` Genetic drift is a population-genetics term from a different
Core Idea and it is a live confusion.

**18. Lack of proofreading is a property of the polymerase, and where it is credited depends on what
was asked.** One scheme credits *"as a result of the lack of proofreading ability of RNA-dependent RNA
polymerase and the fast/high rate of replication of the virus"*. A second, on a question asking for
"specific features of the **influenza genome**", prints *"R! lack of proofreading ability by
RNA-dependent RNA polymerase"* because the polymerase is not a feature of the genome. `[SCHOOL]` The
two are not in conflict. Read what the question makes the subject.

**19. Genetic change alone does not produce an outbreak.** Examiner comment: *"the answers either
lacked depth or were conceptually flawed... An outbreak is defined as the occurrence of viral infection
cases that substantially surpass the usual levels expected in a particular area. Genetic changes alone
(antigenic shift and antigenic drift) do not lead to outbreaks. It is important to link the genetic
changes to contributing factors such as environmental changes (e.g. deforestation), human behaviour and
movement (e.g. travel, urbanisation), economic and trade practices (e.g. illegal wildlife trading), and
public health..."* `[SCHOOL]` Any "suggest how a new viral disease emerges" answer needs shift or drift
**plus** a contributing factor.

**20. The benefit of horizontal transfer must be stated as gaining genes, not as having an advantage.**
A scheme prints *"Reject 'allows bacteria to have selective advantage / advantageous traits' without
reference to gaining of advantageous genes"*, and another rejects *"genetic diversity, variety,
variance"* where **genetic variation** is the wanted term. `[SCHOOL]`

**21. Data questions must quote the data, and both axes.** A scheme adds *"A reminder for students to
quote both x-axis and y-axis when quoting from graphs."* With 7.9% of in-scope structured parts opening
"with reference to" and 45.7% figure-led overall, this is a large amount of mark surface. `[SCHOOL]`

**22. Command-word discipline, as it bites in this Book specifically.** *Describe* wants the ordered
events and nothing about why, and it is 13.4% of parts here, the highest of any Book. *Outline* implies
brevity, and LO 2(e) and LO 2(g) both use it, so the reproductive cycles are expected as compact
sequences rather than as essays unless the mark allocation says otherwise. *List* on Specimen P3
Q1(a)(ii) was worth three marks for three differences, which means three one-line statements. *State*
on a one-mark part means one clause. *Discuss* appears only on the cell-theory node and it is the one
place two-sided argument is wanted. `[OFFICIAL]` glossary meanings, `[SCHOOL]` marking behaviour.

### How far the Hfr exclusion actually reaches

The clause is: *"describe how transformation, transduction and conjugation (**including the role of F
plasmids but not Hfr**) give rise to variation in prokaryotic genomes."* `[OFFICIAL]` LO 2(g).

**What it cuts, with high confidence:**

- **The term Hfr itself**, and any question that names one.
- **The integrated state of the F factor.** Chapter 4.2 page 25 says the F factor "exists as a plasmid
  known as the F plasmid **or as a segment of DNA integrated into the bacterial chromosome**." That
  second clause is the definition of an Hfr cell. Read the F factor as the F plasmid and nothing else.
- **Transfer of chromosomal genes by conjugation.** This is the reach that matters most and it is worth
  stating explicitly: **only an Hfr or F' donor transfers chromosomal genes.** With Hfr excluded,
  conjugation in 9477 transfers the F plasmid, and therefore transfers only whatever genes the plasmid
  itself carries, such as antibiotic resistance. `[INFERRED]` from the exclusion, and corroborated by
  the school's own page 16 note that homologous recombination does not occur in conjugation.
- **F' cells and sexduction**, which are the F factor excising imprecisely and carrying chromosomal
  genes with it. That is Hfr biology by another name.
- **Interrupted-mating gene mapping**, meaning the Wollman and Jacob time-of-entry experiments. This is
  a classic prelim data question and it is dead. Six corpus records name Hfr and this is most of what
  they are.

**What it does NOT cut:**

- **The F plasmid, in full.** Its tra genes, the sex pilus it codes for, the mating bridge, the nick in
  one strand, single-strand transfer, complementary strand synthesis in both cells, and both cells
  ending F+.
- **The rolling circle mechanism and the origin of transfer**, which are properties of plasmid transfer
  and not of Hfr.
- **Conjugation as a route for antibiotic resistance to spread**, which is the applied form the corpus
  most often uses and which needs only the plasmid.
- **The Lederberg and Tatum experiment in its conceptual form**, meaning the demonstration that gene
  transfer between bacteria requires cell contact and is therefore not transformation or transduction.
  The U-tube logic survives; the Hfr mapping built on it does not.

**Net effect.** The exclusion makes **"conjugation transfers the F plasmid, and only the F plasmid"** a
complete and safe answer. That is a real simplification, and it also makes the school's page 16 note
about homologous recombination correct rather than a special case.

### High yield: these get 15 to 20 drill questions, not the standard 10

1. **Interpreting an unfamiliar virus diagram** (LO 1e). The outcome says "and interpret drawings and
   photographs of them" in those words, the corpus is 45.7% figure-led among in-scope structured parts,
   and the single most common opening move in the whole bank is a labelled cross-section of a virus
   Ethan has never seen with four labels to identify. Drill: identify envelope, glycoprotein, capsid,
   genome and nucleoprotein on Ebola, SARS-CoV-2, Hantavirus, Epstein-Barr, rubella and a phage, then
   state two structural differences from a named syllabus virus. The mark schemes reject every near
   synonym, so precision is the whole skill.

2. **HIV against influenza, held side by side** (LOs 2e ii and iii). **9477 Specimen P1 Q11 is exactly
   this** and it is the most efficient revision object in the Book. `[OFFICIAL]` Build one table with
   the five general steps as rows and the two viruses as columns, then add lambda as a third column.
   That single table answers the specimen stem, the 13-mark "compare the reproductive cycles" essay,
   and every "state two differences" part in the bank.

3. **The prokaryote-against-eukaryote genome comparison** (LO 2d). **This is what the specimen actually
   asked**, at P3 Q1(a)(i) and (ii), for four marks, and neither Book 4 chapter teaches it in one
   place. `[OFFICIAL]` Build it yourself on the outcome's own axes: DNA or RNA, single or double
   stranded, number of nucleotides, packing, linearity or circularity, introns. Then add the third
   column for viruses, because the outcome names all three genomes.

4. **The three transfer mechanisms compared** (LO 2g). 8.3% of in-scope records already put two or more
   of them in one question, the corpus files a completion table on exactly this in the school's own
   Concept Check, and the mark schemes carry ready-made comparison rows. The axes that get marked are
   source of DNA, what is transferred, whether cell contact is required, whether a phage vector is
   required, and whether homologous recombination follows.

5. **Antigenic shift against antigenic drift** (LO 2f). This is the highest ratio of outcome weight to
   corpus frequency in the Book: a whole learning outcome sitting at 4.4% of the corpus. The school's
   page 43 table is six rows and already in mark-scheme shape. Drill it to reproducible, and drill the
   consequence chain from altered base sequence through altered 3D conformation to antibodies no longer
   being complementary, which is where the marks actually sit.

6. **The lysogenic cycle and its two transduction consequences** (LOs 2e i and 2g). This is the highest
   value cross-chapter link in the Book and **neither chapter states it in one sentence**. Virulent
   phage, lytic cycle, random gene, generalised transduction; temperate phage, lysogenic cycle, gene
   adjacent to the insertion site, specialised transduction. The schemes reject the wrong pairing in
   both directions, so the link has to be automatic rather than reconstructed.

7. **Applying a known mechanism to an unfamiliar organism** (all LOs). Predict-and-apply is 14.1% of
   the in-scope corpus, against Book 3's 1.7%. Suggest is 9.5% of structured parts. This is a distinct
   drillable skill: given a virus with a stated genome type, deduce which enzyme it must carry, where
   it must replicate, how it must enter and how it must leave. Given a bacterial experiment with pore
   sizes or phage resistance or a sterile medium, deduce which transfer mechanism is possible.

8. **Data-quoting discipline.** 45.7% figure-led among in-scope structured parts, 7.9% opening "with
   reference to", and a scheme that reminds candidates to quote **both** axes. Describe the trend,
   quote two values with units from both axes, then account for it.

### Low yield, and the honest findings of this build

**Finding 1: 44.0% of the Genetics_of_Bacteria bank tests content with no 9477 outcome, and almost all
of it is operons.** Measured:

- **334 of the 759 Genetics_of_Bacteria corpus records (44.0%)** mention an operon, the lac or trp
  genes, a repressor, an inducer, an operator or the catabolite activator protein.
- In the curated main bank the concentration is only slightly better: **190 of 500 (38.0%)**.
- The bank's own concept headings make it visible. The heading covering *"Inducible operons"* and the
  lac operon carries **144 records**, *"Repressible operons and other regulatory switches"* carries 8,
  and *"Coupled transcription and translation in prokaryotes"* carries 3.
- The syllabus returns **zero hits** for "operon", "lac", "trp", "inducible" and "repressible". The
  only regulation outcome, LO 2(i), begins *"explain how differential ... gene expression **in
  eukaryotes** can be regulated"*.
- All four 9477 specimen papers contain **zero** operon questions.

**Conclusion.** This is a syllabus-generation artefact. Prokaryotic gene regulation was examinable
under 9744 and is not under 9477. Filter the Genetics_of_Bacteria bank on the word "operon" before
drilling anything from it, and do not build a cluster around it. **Only 285 of the 759 records in that
file, 37.5%, are Book 4 material.**

**Finding 2: 16.5% of the Genetics_of_Viruses bank is Extension Topic A, not Book 4.**

- **161 of the 977 Genetics_of_Viruses corpus records** touch vaccines, antibodies, immunity,
  lymphocytes, R0, outbreak, epidemic or pandemic.
- These are not out of syllabus. They are **Book 9, Infectious Diseases**, where LO A(g) covers *"how
  viruses, including influenza virus and HIV, cause diseases in humans"*.
- The overlap is genuine and useful, because the same HIV and influenza knowledge serves both. What it
  is not is Book 4 drill time.

**Conclusion.** Tag them and defer them to Book 9. When Book 9 is built, these 161 records are already
mined.

**Finding 3: the corpus and the specimen disagree about Paper 2, and the disagreement is large.** The

**Finding 4: nodes that are low frequency but should not be dropped.**

  one clause with no weighting, and the only specimen question on the outcome was about none of them
  specifically. Treat the corpus ordering as weak evidence and drill all three to the same standard.
- **The reach of the Hfr exclusion into chromosomal gene transfer.** The ruling in §5 that conjugation
  in 9477 transfers only the F plasmid is `[INFERRED]` from the exclusion plus the school's own
  homologous-recombination note. It is almost certainly right, and no specimen question or prelim
  scheme in the corpus states it in those words.

**Recalibration trigger:** if drill performance runs above 70% but a real paper scores below 50%, the
most likely mispredicting dimension is **precision of naming**, not archetype content. This Book's
mark schemes reject near-synonyms harder than any other Book's: envelope against protein envelope,
capsid against protein coat, mating bridge against sex pilus, F+ against donor, antigenic drift against
genetic drift, budding against exocytosis, membrane against wall. Re-measure by scoring a set of
identification and one-word answers against the reject lists in §3 before changing anything else.
