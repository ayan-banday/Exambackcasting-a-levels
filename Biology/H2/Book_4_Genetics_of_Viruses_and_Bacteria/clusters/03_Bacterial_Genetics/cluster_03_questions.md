# Mark Schemes

### Question 1

**(a)** *State two ways in which a plasmid such as the F plasmid differs from the bacterial chromosome.* **[2]**

**1.** Any two of the following contrasts, each stated for both the bacterial chromosome and the plasmid.

**2.** **Relative number of genes and genome size.** The bacterial chromosome has a **bigger genome, that is more genes**, while the plasmid has a **smaller genome, that is fewer genes.**

**3.** **General function of the genes.** The bacterial chromosome's genes are **essential for survival, for example enzymes for cell metabolism, or for the synthesis of amino acids such as tryptophan**, while the plasmid's genes are **not essential for survival but code for advantageous traits, for example antibiotic resistance such as ampicillin resistance.**

**4.** **Number per cell.** There is **only 1** bacterial chromosome, while there may be **none, 1 copy, or many** plasmids.

**5.** A further credited point elsewhere in the papers: the plasmid is **capable of replication independent of the bacterial chromosome since it possesses its own origin of replication.**

**(b)** *Describe how the bacterial DNA is packaged to fit into the nucleoid region of a S. pneumoniae cell.* **[2]**

**1.** **Circular double-stranded DNA molecule associated with a small amount of non-histone proteins.**

**2.** The **associated non-histone proteins cause the DNA to coil tightly to form loop domains,**

**3.** **and subsequently supercoil to form condensed DNA, that is the bacterial chromosome.**

**4.** LO 2(d) names **packing of DNA** by word, so histone-like proteins, looped domains and supercoiling are required even though none of those three words appears anywhere in the syllabus. You will not be handed them in a stem.

**(c)** *Outline the features found within a typical bacterium cell that distinguishes it from a typical plant cell.* **[3]**

**1.** Reference to **lack of membrane-bound organelles in bacteria against presence of such organelles in plant cells.**

**2.** Reference to **bacterial cells containing circular DNA against plant cells containing linear DNA.**

**3.** Reference to **DNA of bacterial cells found in the nucleoid region against DNA in plant cells found in the nucleus.**

**4.** Reference to **bacterial cells containing 70S ribosomes against plant cells containing 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.**

**5.** Reference to **bacterial cells containing plasmids, that is extra-chromosomal DNA, which are absent in plant cells.**

**6.** Any other valid point. **Maximum 3.**

**7.** **Reject: comparisons involving cell wall and flagella. Ignore: comparisons involving chloroplasts, vacuoles, mitochondria and so on.** Read the stem before choosing axes, because this scheme narrows the ground and rejects two of the features LO 1(d) itself names.

The Fig. 1.1 image is the real Fig. 7.1 from the ASRJC 2025 Paper 2 question paper.
**Archetype:** recall, state and describe. All three parts can be asked cold, and all three are answered from the five features LO 1(d) names plus the two credited extras.
**Trap:** giving the plasmid difference one-sidedly, for example "plasmids are small", instead of stating both; treating the packing question as a chromosome question and describing histones and nucleosomes, which are eukaryotic; offering the cell wall or the flagellum in (c), which that scheme rejects by name even though LO 1(d) names the peptidoglycan cell wall; forgetting that the nucleoid is a region and not a compartment, so there is no envelope and no pore.
**LOs:** 1(d) the structure of a typical bacterial cell, in (a) and (c); 2(d) the structure and organisation of prokaryotic genomes, specifically circularity, packing of DNA and extrachromosomal DNA, in (a), (b) and (c). Reach-back to Book 1 for plant cell ultrastructure in (c).

### Question 4

**(a)** *Explain the significance of binary fission in bacteria.* **[2]**

**1.** Reference to **asexual reproduction for a unicellular organism.**

**2.** **Ensuring that offspring are genetically identical to the parent, that is desirable alleles or traits are passed down.**

**3.** **Rapid increase in cell numbers under favourable conditions.**

**4.** **Any 2.**

**(b)** *Explain how binary fission contributes to the extensive genetic variation in bacteria.* **[2]**

**1.** **Mutation** occurs, and

**2.** the **short generation time, that is high rate of bacterial reproduction, allows any mutated DNA sequence to be propagated and accumulated quickly in a population.**

**3.** Hold this apart from the standing statement that **binary fission is unable to give rise to genetic variation in a bacterial population**. Both are true once you separate **transfer** from **variation**: binary fission itself copies a genome faithfully, so it generates nothing new, but it multiplies whatever a mutation has already produced. Your chapter 4.2 states both on consecutive pages, on page 13 and page 14, and Specimen Paper 1 Question 12 turns on exactly this ambiguity.

**(c)** *Which method of gene transfer from one bacterial cell to another is responsible for the enormous increase in the number of DH5α cells that were resistant to ampicillin?* **[1]** [INFERRED: the 9477 specimen papers ship without a mark scheme, and this key is genuinely contested. Do not drill it as though it were settled]

**1.** **The question bank keys this B, conjugation, and tags that answer itself as inferred with no official key behind it. There is a strong case for A, binary fission.** Both readings are defensible. What is not in doubt is the distinction the question is testing, which is **vertical against horizontal gene transfer**, so be able to argue either way from the stem.

**(d)** *Explain your answer, saying for each of the four options what the stem tells you and whether it rules that option in or out.* **[3]** [INFERRED]

**1.** **The case for A, binary fission.** The original culture was grown **in ampicillin**, so **every cell in it already carried the F plasmid**. It was then inoculated into **sterile** broth, so **no F- partner exists** for a mating. The stated observation is that **cell number rose more than 15 million-fold in eight hours**, which is **replication**, and that **"most, but not all" cells were resistant**, which is the classic pattern of **plasmid loss during growth without selection**. Conjugation between two F+ cells does not occur efficiently.

**2.** **The case for B, conjugation.** The phrase **"gene transfer from one bacterial cell to another"** is the standard wording for **horizontal transfer**, and a prelim scheme elsewhere in the papers rejects binary fission outright on the ground that it **"is cell division, not gene transfer between cells"**.

**3.** **C, transduction, is ruled out by feature 4**: the strain is **resistant to infection by T1 phage**, and the broth is sterile, so **no phage vector is present to carry DNA between cells.**

**4.** **D, transformation, is ruled out despite feature 3.** Feature 3 says the cells **take up DNA easily**, and feature 2 says they **lack endonucleases that could degrade plasmids**, which is what makes them useful in a laboratory. But **the broth was sterile and contained no added DNA**, so **there is no naked DNA in the environment to take up.**

**5.** So **the eliminators are in the stem's own numbered list and in the word "sterile"**, and the argument comes down to whether **"gene transfer"** is being used loosely enough to include a cell passing its plasmid to its own daughters.

**Archetype:** applied MCQ with the options pre-eliminated by features listed in the stem, then a full audit. Not one of the four Book 4 items on the specimen Paper 1 is plain recall, and this one requires the stem's four numbered features to be read as eliminators.
**Trap:** answering (a) and (b) with the same paragraph, when (a) is about faithful copying and (b) is about mutation being amplified by a short generation time; assuming any question containing the phrase "gene transfer" must mean a horizontal mechanism; missing that the broth is sterile, which is the single word that removes both transduction and transformation; treating the four listed features as background rather than as the eliminators they are.
**LOs:** 2(g) binary fission, and how transformation, transduction and conjugation give rise to variation in prokaryotic genomes, in all four parts; vertical against horizontal gene transfer as the frame, in (c) and (d).
**Concepts fused:** (1) asexual reproduction of a unicellular organism; (2) genetically identical daughters; (3) rapid population growth; (4) mutation as the source of novelty; (5) short generation time as the amplifier; (6) vertical against horizontal transfer; (7) plasmid loss without selection; (8) the need for an F- partner; (9) a phage vector as the requirement for transduction; (10) naked DNA in the environment as the requirement for transformation; (11) reading a stem's feature list as a set of eliminators.

**(b)** *Describe generalised and specialised transduction.* **[8]**

**Generalised transduction, maximum 4**

**1.** **A virulent phage infects a bacterial cell and hydrolytic enzymes degrade the host bacterial chromosome into fragments.**

**2.** **A small fragment of the host cell's degraded DNA is improperly packaged within a capsid,**

**3.** **rather than the phage genome, due to an error during the viral particle assembly process.**

**4.** **When this phage attaches to or infects another bacterial cell, it will inject this foreign bacterial DNA into its new host,**

**5.** **and it may be integrated into the new host bacterial chromosome under the proper circumstances**

**6.** **via homologous recombination.**

**Specialised transduction, maximum 4**

**7.** **A temperate phage infects a bacterial cell and has its viral DNA genome integrated into the bacterial chromosome.**

**8.** **When the prophage viral DNA is excised from the chromosome,**

**9.** **it sometimes takes with it a small region of adjacent bacterial DNA due to improper excision.**

**10.** **These bacterial DNA are injected, along with the phage's genome, into the next host bacterial cell and**

**11.** **may be integrated into the new host bacterial chromosome under the proper circumstances**

**12.** **via homologous recombination.**

**13.** **Maximum 4 marks for generalised and maximum 4 marks for specialised.**

**14.** The pairing has to be automatic, because schemes reject it in both directions. One prints **"REJECT specialised transduction, because the phage is T4 and the genetic material is a random piece of bacterial genetic material"**, another prints **"Reject specialised transduction, since the figure shows a lytic phage which usually facilitates generalised transduction"**, and a generalised-transduction key prints **(R: lytic phage)** and **(R: lysogenic phage)** where **virulent** and **temperate** are the wanted words. **Virulent and lytic go with generalised; temperate and lysogenic go with specialised**, and the phage type is what you write, not the cycle.

**15.** The single word that distinguishes them: generalised is **random**, because each portion of the bacterial genome has approximately the same probability of being transferred; specialised is **adjacent**, because only genes near the prophage insertion site can travel.

### Question 9

**(a)** *Describe two ways by which vancomycin resistance may spread naturally amongst bacteria.* **[2]**

**1.** **Transformation.** **Release of the plasmid containing the vancomycin resistance gene into the environment, and uptake of the plasmid by bacterial cells.**

**2.** **Conjugation.** **Contact between recipient F- and donor F+ cells, involving formation of a sex pilus, and transfer of the vancomycin resistance gene found on the F plasmid.**

**3.** **Transduction.** **The vancomycin resistance gene from the host or donor bacterium is transferred to the recipient bacterial cell via a bacteriophage, the donor DNA from the host being accidentally packaged within a viral capsid, and progeny phages infecting other bacteria.**

**4.** **Maximum 2.**

**5.** A scheme elsewhere **rejects binary fission** where the stem says "spread naturally amongst bacteria", because that phrase implies horizontal transfer between cells and binary fission is vertical.

**(b)** *Which row correctly describes the transfer of DNA from one bacterium to another?* **[1]**

**1.** The key is **A**.

**(c)** *Explain your answer, saying for each of the three named processes which of the two descriptions offered is correct and why the other is wrong.* **[3]** [INFERRED: the school's teacher copy gives the key only; reasoning cross-checked against the AJC Prelim 2014 P2 Q9(a), IJC Prelim 2018 P2 Q2(c) and DHS Prelim 2017 P1 keys, which set the same three descriptions]

**1.** **Binary fission: "bacterial chromosome and plasmids pass to daughter cells" is correct**, and **"only plasmids pass to daughter cells" is wrong**. In binary fission the **bacterial chromosome is replicated semi-conservatively and one copy goes to each daughter cell**, and **any plasmid replicates independently from its own origin of replication and is also passed on**, so both molecules travel.

**2.** **Transduction: "DNA transferred by bacteriophage from one bacterium to another" is correct**, and **"bacterial cell takes up foreign DNA from culture medium" is wrong**, because that description is **transformation**, which needs no vector at all.

**3.** **Conjugation: "single strand of F plasmid transferred from one bacterium to another" is correct**, and **"double strand of F plasmid transferred" is wrong.** **One strand of the F plasmid is nicked by an endonuclease at oriT and the nicked single strand moves to the F- recipient through the mating bridge**; the **complementary strand is then synthesised in each cell**, so what crosses the bridge is single-stranded and what ends up in each cell is double-stranded.

**4.** So row A is the only row correct on all three, and each wrong row is wrong because it has swapped in the description of a different process or halved what binary fission carries.

**Archetype:** applied recall and an audited table MCQ. The three transfer mechanisms compared in one question account for 8.3% of in-scope records, and this is the shape that comparison usually takes on Paper 1.
**Trap:** naming binary fission as a way resistance spreads between bacteria, which a scheme rejects for exactly that stem wording; confusing transduction with transformation, which is the swap both wrong rows are built on; saying the whole double-stranded plasmid crosses to the recipient; forgetting that the recipient becomes F+ and can then act as a donor itself, which is what makes resistance spread so fast.
