## A. The cell theory, and the bacterial cell the outcome asks for

**A1. The cell theory is three statements, and LO 1(a) writes them out for you.** **Cells are the
smallest unit of life**, **all cells come from pre-existing cells**, and **living organisms are composed
of cells**. Learn them in the outcome's own words, because LO 1(f) in Cluster 1 then asks how viruses
challenge each of them, and the two outcomes are answered from the same three lines.

**A2. LO 1(d) names five features, so those five are the mark scheme.** A typical bacterial cell is
**small and unicellular**, has a **peptidoglycan cell wall**, **circular DNA**, **70S ribosomes**, and
**lacks membrane-bound organelles**. Bacteria themselves are **single-celled microorganisms that lack a
nuclear membrane, are capable of metabolic activities and divide by binary fission**.

**A3. Two credited extras sit just outside the outcome's five.** **The genome is not enclosed by a
membrane and lies in a nucleoid region**, and **plasmids may be present as extra-chromosomal DNA**.
Schemes credit both, but read the stem before choosing axes: one scheme rejected cell wall and flagella
as comparison points in a question that had already narrowed the ground.

**A4. The prokaryote against eukaryote ultrastructure differences that matter.**

| Characteristic | **Prokaryotic** | **Eukaryotic** |
|---|---|---|
| Size of cell | **typically 0.2 to 2.0 µm in diameter** | **typically 10 to 100 µm in diameter** |
| Nucleus | **no nuclear membrane or nucleoli** | **true nucleus, containing nuclear membrane and nucleoli** |
| Membrane-enclosed organelles | **absent** | **present**, such as Golgi apparatus, ER, mitochondria, chloroplasts |
| Cell wall | **usually present, chemically complex, includes peptidoglycan** | when present, **chemically simple**, typically **cellulose** in plants |
| Ribosomes | **70S** | **80S**, with **70S in organelles** |

That last row does double duty, because **mitochondria and chloroplasts carry 70S ribosomes**, which is
one of the endosymbiosis marks in section D.

## B. The prokaryotic genome, on the axes LO 2(d) names

**B1. The nucleoid is a region, not an organelle.** The bacterial genome is **not separated from the
cytoplasm by a nuclear membrane**, but is **aggregated in a region of the cell known as the nucleoid**.
The distinction to hold is region against compartment: no envelope, no pores, and no separation of
transcription from translation.

**B2. The chromosome is single, circular and double-stranded.** **The bacterial chromosome is typically
a single, circular, double-stranded DNA molecule which contains essential genes required for survival.**
Every one of those adjectives is a separate axis LO 2(d) asks about, so state them as a list rather than
as a description.

**B3. Packing is named in the outcome even though none of its vocabulary is.** To fit inside the cell
**the chromosome must be compacted approximately 1000-fold**. That compaction comes in two stages:
**association of the DNA with positively charged histone-like proteins that aid in compacting the DNA
into looped domains**, then **further compaction by supercoiling the bacterial DNA**. LO 2(d) says
**packing of DNA** and stops, so the words histone-like, looped domains and supercoiling all return zero
syllabus hits while the content behind them is fully examinable.

**B4. Prokaryotic genes lack introns, and that has a downstream consequence.** **Prokaryotic genes lack
introns and thus do not require splicing after transcription, unlike eukaryotic gene expression.**
Introns are one of the axes LO 2(d) names by word, and this is the axis Specimen Paper 1 Question 10 was
built on, which means it is answered with genome-organisation vocabulary from a different Book.

**B5. Plasmids carry every property you might be asked for.** Plasmids are **non-essential pieces of
DNA**, existing as **small, circular, double-stranded extrachromosomal DNA molecules**. They are
**capable of replication independent of the bacterial chromosome since they possess their own origin of
replication**, so **cells may contain more than one plasmid**. They **contain beneficial genes which
confer protective traits such as antibiotic resistance, toxin synthesis and enzyme production**, and so
**confer a selective advantage**. Each clause does work in a different question, which is why the list
is worth learning whole.

## C. Prokaryotic against eukaryotic genome, which is what the specimen actually asked

Specimen Paper 3 Question 1(a) asked for **one similarity for one mark** and **three differences for
three marks**, on mitochondrial DNA against the nuclear genome. Neither chapter of Book 4 builds this
table, so build it yourself on the axes the outcome names.

| Axis from LO 2(d) | **Prokaryotic genome** | **Eukaryotic genome** |
|---|---|---|
| Nucleic acid | **DNA, double-stranded** | **DNA, double-stranded** (the similarity) |
| Linearity | **circular** | **linear** |
| Number of molecules | **a single chromosome**, plus plasmids | **many chromosomes** |
| Location | **nucleoid, with no nuclear envelope** | **nucleus, enclosed by a nuclear envelope** |
| Packing | **histone-like proteins**, **looped domains**, **supercoiling** | **histones**, **nucleosomes**, chromatin |
| Introns | **absent, so no splicing** | **present, so splicing is required** |
| Extrachromosomal DNA | **plasmids present** | **plasmids absent**, though mitochondria and chloroplasts carry their own DNA |
| Number of nucleotides | **smaller genome** | **larger genome**, with a higher proportion of non-coding DNA |

**The similarity mark is the one most often thrown away.** Both genomes are **double-stranded DNA**, and
both **contain genes coding for proteins**. Read the command word: a **list** of three differences is
three one-line statements, not three paragraphs.

## D. Endosymbiosis, where the word is absent and the marks are real

**D1. Learn the evidence, not the term.** The word endosymbiosis appears **nowhere in the 9477
syllabus** and **nowhere in the specimen**. Specimen Paper 3 Question 1(a)(iii) instead describes the
hypothesis in plain language, that **mitochondria may have originated as independent free-living
prokaryotic cells that became engulfed by primitive eukaryotic cells in a process similar to
endocytosis**, and then asks for **two features of mitochondria that provide support for it, for two
marks**. So you need the supporting evidence and you never need the label.

**D2. The five lines of evidence, and every one is built from LO 1(d) and LO 2(d).**

1. **Mitochondria and plastids contain circular DNA which is not associated with histones, resembling
   the chromosomes of bacteria.**
2. **Ribosomes of mitochondria and plastids are more similar to prokaryotic ribosomes** in **size, RNA
   sequences and sensitivity to antibiotics** than to the eukaryotic cytoplasmic ribosomes, which is the
   **70S against 80S** row from section A4.
3. **Mitochondria and plastids replicate by a splitting process similar to certain prokaryotes**, which
   is binary fission by another name.
4. **Mitochondria and plastids possess cellular machinery, including ribosomes, needed to transcribe and
   translate their DNA into proteins.**
5. **The inner membranes of both organelles have enzymes and transport systems similar to those found in
   the plasma membranes of living prokaryotes.**

**D3. A double membrane is the sixth answer and the chapter does not list it.** An organelle engulfed by
**endocytosis** ends up wrapped in **an inner membrane of its own plus an outer membrane derived from
the host's cell surface membrane**, which is exactly what a mitochondrion has. On a stem that itself
names endocytosis, the **double membrane** and the **circular DNA** are the two cheapest marks
available.

## E. Binary fission, which LO 2(g) asks you only to outline

**E1. The definition sets both the mechanism and its limitation.** **Binary fission is a form of asexual
reproduction in which two equal-sized genetically identical daughter cells are produced from a single
parent cell**, and **it includes replication of the bacterial chromosome**. Because the daughters are
identical, **binary fission is unable to give rise to genetic variation in a bacterial population**, and
that single sentence is what the whole of the rest of the outcome exists to answer.

**E2. Six steps, with the mark-bearing detail in each.**

1. **The bacterial chromosome is attached to the plasma membrane before DNA replication.**
2. **DNA replication begins at the single origin of replication**, where the **replication bubble forms
   as the two strands separate**, and **each parental strand is used as a template for the synthesis of
   the daughter strand in semi-conservative DNA replication**. The **bubble grows bidirectionally away
   from the origin** until the whole chromosome is replicated.
3. **After replication, cell growth occurs**, and **each circular DNA molecule is attached to the cell
   membrane**, one attachment site each.
4. **The cell elongates, and membrane growth causes the two chromosomes to be moved apart.**
5. **The septal ring, a group of proteins, directs the assembly of the septum**, which **eventually
   separates the two daughter cells**. **The septum extends as the cell membrane invaginates**, with
   **new cell membrane and cell wall material added to it**.
6. **The invaginating cell membrane, together with the newly formed septum, splits the cell into two
   genetically identical daughter cells by cytokinesis.**

**E3. It is the membrane that invaginates.** A scheme prints an explicit reject for saying the cell wall
invaginates or the cell invaginates, and wants **the cell membrane folds inwards to form a double layer
across the long axis of the cell**. The wall material is added afterwards, and saying it the wrong way
round costs the mark.

**E4. Single origin and bidirectional separate this from eukaryotic replication.** A eukaryotic
chromosome has many origins; a bacterial one has **one**, and replication runs **in both directions from
it**. Both words are cheap to include and both get credited.

## F. Homologous recombination is the step that makes transferred DNA heritable

**F1. Without it there is transfer but no inheritance, and therefore no variation.** **For the
transferred DNA to be passed down to its offspring, DNA must be integrated into the chromosome of the
recipient cell**, and this occurs by **homologous recombination**, a process which **occurs naturally
and is needed by cells to accurately repair harmful breaks on both strands of DNA**.

**F2. Homology is a requirement, not a description.** The transferred portion of the donor chromosome is
**exchanged with a portion of the recipient chromosome that is very similar in sequence**, that is
**highly homologous**, so **successful gene transfer usually occurs between bacteria of closely related
species**. **The segment of the recipient chromosome that is exchanged is excised and degraded**, which
is a separate mark from the insertion itself. **Cells that have successfully taken up donor DNA are
known as recombinant cells.**

**F3. Conjugation is the exception, and it is an exception for a reason.** **Homologous recombination
does not occur in conjugation, since the F plasmid is not integrated into the recipient's chromosome.**
That statement is true because Hfr is excluded from the syllabus; with an integrated F factor,
chromosomal genes would move and recombination would follow. Learn the node once here and reuse it in
transformation and in both kinds of transduction.

## G. Transformation is uptake of naked DNA by a competent cell

**G1. The definition names both the material and the source.** **Transformation is the process by which
a recipient cell takes up small fragments of naked DNA from the surrounding environment.** The DNA comes
from **a donor bacterial cell which lyses and releases its DNA into the surrounding environment**. A
scheme credits **fragments of foreign naked DNA** and rejects chromosomal DNA, on the ground that a whole
chromosome is too big.

**G2. Competence is the gatekeeper.** **Only competent bacterial cells are naturally able to undergo
transformation.** Competence depends on **competence factors produced by the bacterial cell**, which are
**cell surface proteins that bind to DNA fragments and aid in their uptake**. Without them the DNA is
present and nothing happens, which is why a question can be built on the uptake step alone.

**G3. Four steps.** **The donor cell lyses and releases naked DNA fragments.** **A competent recipient
cell takes up one or more of the fragments into its cytoplasm via its competence factors.**
**Homologous recombination takes place with a homologous section of the recipient's chromosome.** **The
donor segment is incorporated into the recipient chromosome and the recipient's homologous segment is
excised and degraded**, making it a **recombinant cell**.

**Artificial transformation is out of scope.** Calcium chloride with heat shock and electroporation have
no learning outcome. Read them once so the vocabulary is not strange in a stem, and never drill them.

## H. Transduction is transfer carried by a bacteriophage, and it comes in two forms

**H1. The definition, and where the two forms come from.** In transduction, **bacteriophages carry
bacterial genes from their first host cell, the donor, to their second host cell, the recipient**, and
they do it **due to errors in the phage reproductive cycle**. The two forms are the two errors: a
**packaging accident in the lytic cycle**, and an **excision accident in the lysogenic cycle**. This is
the join between chapter 4.1 and chapter 4.2, and neither states it in one sentence.

**H2. Generalised transduction is a lytic-cycle packaging accident, and random is the whole
distinction.** **During the reproduction of virulent phages, a small fraction of the virions produced
during the lytic cycle contain a random fragment of the bacterial genome instead of phage DNA.** Step by
step: the **virulent phage injects its DNA into the first host and the host chromosome is degraded**;
the phage **uses the host's replication and gene expression machinery**; **occasionally a small piece of
the degraded host DNA is accidentally packaged within a phage capsid in place of the phage genome during
the assembly stage**, giving a **defective phage**; the first host is **lysed** and the defective phage
**infects a second host and injects the donor DNA**; then the **donor DNA is incorporated by homologous
recombination**. It is generalised because **each portion of the bacterial genome has approximately the
same probability of being transferred**.

**H3. Specialised transduction is a lysogenic-cycle excision accident, and adjacent is the whole
distinction.** During the reproduction of temperate phages the **phage genome is integrated into the
first host's chromosome as a prophage during the lysogenic cycle**, and **upon induction it is excised**
and the cell switches to the lytic cycle. **The excision of the prophage is sometimes imprecise**,
giving segments of phage DNA that **lack part of the normal phage genome and contain part of the
bacterial chromosome located adjacent to the prophage insertion site**. In the recipient the donor DNA
is incorporated by either **prophage integration**, if the transferred phage DNA carries the genes
required to enter the lysogenic cycle, or **homologous recombination**, if it does not. It is
specialised because **only genes near the prophage insertion site have a high probability of being
transferred**.

**H4. Generalised against specialised, on the three axes the school's table uses.**

| | **Generalised transduction** | **Specialised transduction** |
|---|---|---|
| Type of phage | **virulent phage** | **temperate phage**, lambda |
| Phage reproductive cycle | **lytic cycle** | **lysogenic cycle, converting to lytic upon induction** |
| Transfer of donor DNA | **any donor gene can be accidentally packaged in a phage capsid** and transferred | **only donor genes near the prophage insertion site**, because of **imprecise excision of the prophage** |

**The pairing has to be automatic, because schemes reject it in both directions.** One prints a reject
for specialised where the phage shown is T4 and the fragment is random; another prints a reject for
specialised where the figure shows a lytic phage. The wanted words are **virulent** and **temperate**,
and lytic phage and lysogenic phage are both rejected as the phage type.

## I. Conjugation transfers the F plasmid, and only the F plasmid

**I1. The syllabus exclusion makes this the simplest of the three mechanisms.** LO 2(g) says
**including the role of F plasmids but not Hfr**, one of very few places in 9477 where a mechanism is
cut by name. It takes with it the **integrated F factor**, **transfer of chromosomal genes by
conjugation**, **F prime cells** and **interrupted-mating gene mapping**. State the net effect plainly,
because it saves real time: **conjugation transfers the F plasmid and only the F plasmid**, and
therefore only the genes the plasmid carries, such as **antibiotic resistance**. That is a complete
answer.

**I2. The definition, and the two structures that are not the same structure.** In conjugation,
**direct contact between the donor and recipient bacteria leads to establishment of a cytoplasmic
bridge**, followed by **transfer of the donor's DNA to the recipient cell**. **Genes on the F factor are
responsible for synthesis of the sex pilus**, facilitating cell-to-cell contact, and for **transfer of
the F factor from a donor cell to a recipient cell**. **The sex pilus makes contact; the cytoplasmic
mating bridge carries the DNA.** Schemes police this three separate times and reject sex pilus where the
bridge is meant.

**I3. Name the cells F+ and F-, not donor and recipient.** **Cells with an extrachromosomal F factor,
that is an F plasmid, are known as F+ cells**, and **cells lacking the F factor are known as F- cells**.
A scheme awards **F+** and **F-** and prints explicit rejects for donor and recipient. Also name the
transferred material as the **F plasmid**, since a scheme rejects both plasmid on its own and F+ plasmid.

**I4. Five steps.** **The F+ donor cell uses a sex pilus to attach to an F- recipient cell.** **A
temporary cytoplasmic mating bridge is formed between the two cells, providing a route for DNA
transfer.** **The sugar-phosphate backbone of one strand of the F plasmid in the F+ donor is nicked by
an endonuclease**, that is **a phosphodiester bond is broken**, and **the nicked single strand separates
from its complementary strand and moves to the F- recipient through the mating bridge**. **Each parental
strand becomes a template for the synthesis of a complementary daughter strand in both cells, by
semi-conservative replication catalysed by DNA polymerase**, and **DNA ligase closes the gap in each F
plasmid**. **The cells move apart, forming two bacterial cells that are both F+.** So **an F+ cell
converts an F- cell to F+**, and the donor does not lose its plasmid, because **the F factor has its own
origin of replication and replicates as it is transferred**.

**I5. Two mark points your notes do not contain at all.** At least eight prelim schemes credit the
**rolling circle mechanism** as the way the F plasmid is replicated and transferred, and several credit
the **origin of transfer, oriT**, with two crediting the **tra genes** that encode the pilus and the
transfer machinery. Chapter 4.2 says only nicked by an endonuclease and then semi-conservative
replication. This is the largest gap between your notes and the schemes anywhere in Book 4, and it is a
missing mark rather than a wrong one, so add the words.

## J. The three mechanisms compared, and why any of it matters

| Axis | **Transformation** | **Transduction** | **Conjugation** |
|---|---|---|---|
| DNA transferred | **naked DNA fragments** from the environment | **fragments of donor bacterial DNA** carried in a phage capsid | **the F plasmid** |
| Source of the DNA | a **dead or lysed donor cell** | a **donor bacterium infected by a phage** | an **F+ donor cell** |
| Recipient | a **competent cell** | a cell **infectable by the same phage** | an **F- cell** |
| Vector needed | **none** | **bacteriophage** | **none**, but **direct cell contact** is required |
| Cell contact needed | **no** | **no** | **yes**, via **sex pilus** then **cytoplasmic mating bridge** |
| Homologous recombination follows | **yes** | **yes** | **no** |

**J1. State the benefit as gaining genes, never as having an advantage.** Horizontal transfer **allows
for genetic recombination and hence the generation of genetic variation in bacterial populations, which
is essential for natural selection**. A scheme rejects the claim that it allows bacteria to have a
selective advantage **without reference to the gaining of advantageous genes**, and another rejects
genetic diversity, variety and variance where **genetic variation** is the wanted term. Run the whole
chain: **new genes gained**, **new variation**, **selection pressure**, **natural selection**,
**antibiotic resistance spreads**.

**J2. Keep vertical and horizontal transfer separate, because one specimen question turns on it.**
**Binary fission passes genes vertically from a parent cell to its offspring**, and **transformation,
transduction and conjugation pass them horizontally to cells of the same generation**. Specimen Paper 1
Question 12 offers binary fission against the three horizontal mechanisms and asks which is responsible
for an increase in the number of resistant cells, so know the distinction cold and argue it from the
stem: **binary fission multiplies cells that already have a gene; the horizontal mechanisms introduce
the gene in the first place**.

## L. Errors and gaps in your notes. Do not encode these.

   factor exists as a plasmid **or as a segment of DNA integrated into the bacterial chromosome**. A cell

   the F plasmid and nothing else.** The chapter never uses the word Hfr and teaches only the F+ by F-
   mating, so it does not take you out of scope, but the sentence reads as though chromosomal transfer

   and both are credited repeatedly in the schemes. Supply them, as in section I5.
4. **Binary fission is called a gene transfer process on page 13 and a source of no variation on page

   exactly this ambiguity.
