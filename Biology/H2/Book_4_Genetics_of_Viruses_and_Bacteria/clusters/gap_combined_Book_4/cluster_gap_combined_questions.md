# Mark Schemes

### Question 1

Any ten. **Adsorption.**

**Multiple tail fibres of the T4 phage attach to specific receptor sites on the bacterial cell surface, the tail fibres being complementary in shape to the receptors.**

**The base plate makes contact with the cell surface, causing a conformational change in the tail sheath so that it contracts.**

**Penetration.** **The tail tube is driven through the cell wall, and phage-encoded lysozyme hydrolyses the peptidoglycan of the bacterial cell wall.**

**Viral DNA is injected into the host cell while the capsid remains outside.**

**Synthesis.** **Host DNA is degraded into nucleotides, which serve as the raw material for the synthesis of phage DNA.**

**Host DNA, RNA and protein synthesis is shut down, and the host's machinery, its DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase, ribosomes, tRNA and ATP, is taken over to replicate phage DNA and synthesise phage proteins.**

**Assembly.** **Phage heads, tail sheaths, base plates and tail fibres are assembled and the DNA is packaged into the head.**

**Release.** **Phage-encoded lysozyme hydrolyses the cell wall from the inside; water enters by osmosis and the cell lyses, releasing the progeny phages to infect neighbouring cells.**

Gap check. Three from Cluster 1 sit inside this one answer.

**B4-7.** *"Host DNA is degraded into nucleotides"* is a marking point and you missed it first time, then caught it yourself: *"if there aren't enough nucleotides, how can I create more phage DNAs?"* Right, and it earns a mark.

**B4-8. Lysozyme, not lysosome.** You said lysosome throughout, twice, once on entry and once on release. **Lysozyme is the phage-encoded enzyme that hydrolyses peptidoglycan. A lysosome is a eukaryotic organelle and a bacterium does not have one.**

**B4-9. Nucleotides, not nucleoproteins.** The raw material is **nucleotides**.

**Source:** SAJC Prelim 2023 P3 Section B, stem verbatim at its real 10 marks, marking points from the SAJC mark scheme.

### Question 2

**(a)** Any three, as difference plus consequence.

**T4 has multiple tail fibres; lambda has a single tail fibre. So T4 anchors at several receptor sites at once and lambda at one.**

**T4 has a contractile tail sheath; lambda has none. So T4 drives its tail tube mechanically through the wall, while lambda's DNA passes through without a contractile mechanism.**

**T4 is virulent and enters the lytic cycle; lambda is temperate and can enter either the lytic or the lysogenic cycle.**

**(b)** Any three.

**The phage DNA, as a prophage, is replicated every time the host cell divides, so the phage genome is copied without the phage having to make new virions.**

**The host cell is not killed, so the phage persists in the population far longer than a virulent phage could.**

**The prophage is silent, so it is not detected or targeted by the host's defences.**

**When conditions become favourable, or on an environmental trigger such as UV, the prophage excises and switches to the lytic cycle, producing many progeny at once.**

Gap check. **B4-11** is part (a). You hedged: *"Do we say multiple tail fibres? I'm pretty sure that's correct, only one tail fibre right there."* The answer is that T4 has many and a contractile sheath, lambda has one and no sheath, and that is exactly why their entry is described differently.

**Source:** (a) is the standard T4-against-lambda contrast, `[INFERRED]` from LO 2(f) and the chapter's own figures; (b) ACJC Prelim 2024 P2, stem and marking points verbatim.

### Question 3

**(a)** Both, one mark each.

**SARS-CoV-2 has a (+) sense single-stranded RNA genome which is used directly as mRNA by host ribosomes, whereas influenza has a (−) sense single-stranded RNA genome which must first be copied into a (+) sense strand by the virus's own RNA-dependent RNA polymerase.**

**SARS-CoV-2 has a single, non-segmented genome and replicates in the cytoplasm, whereas influenza has eight separate RNA segments and replicates in the nucleus.**

**(b)** Any seven.

**Haemagglutinin on the viral envelope binds to sialic-acid-containing receptors on the host cell surface membrane, the two being complementary in shape.**

**The virus enters by receptor-mediated endocytosis, forming an endosome.**

**The endosome acidifies, and the low pH causes a conformational change in the haemagglutinin.**

**The changed haemagglutinin drives fusion of the viral envelope with the endosomal membrane, releasing the eight ribonucleoprotein segments into the cytoplasm.**

**The viral RNA segments are transported into the nucleus.**

**The viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, carried in the virion, catalyses synthesis of (+) sense complementary strands.**

**Some (+) strands leave the nucleus and are translated as mRNA on host ribosomes to give viral proteins; glycoproteins are made on the RER, glycosylated in the Golgi and inserted into the cell surface membrane.**

**Other (+) strands act as templates for new (−) sense genome segments.**

**Assembly packages eight (−) sense segments, each bound to nucleoprotein, together with molecules of RNA-dependent RNA polymerase.**

**The virion buds from the cell surface membrane, acquiring its envelope with the viral glycoproteins already embedded, and neuraminidase cleaves sialic acid residues so the new virions are released rather than sticking to the cell.**

**(c)** All three.

**Antigenic drift is the accumulation of point mutations in the haemagglutinin and neuraminidase genes, because the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase has no proofreading and no exonuclease activity, giving a gradual change in the surface antigens.**

**Antigenic shift is the reassortment of whole genome segments between two different strains, giving a radically new haemagglutinin and neuraminidase combination against which the population has no immunity.**

**Antigenic shift requires co-infection of the same host cell by two different strains; antigenic drift does not.**

Gap check. Six gaps live here and this is the question to sit slowly.

**B4-13.** Marking point 6 of part (b). *"Positive RNA utilises the host cell DNA polymerase"* is the one thing that cannot happen. **No host enzyme copies RNA into RNA**, which is the entire reason the virion packages its own polymerase. Both directions, (−) to (+) and (+) back to (−), use the same viral enzyme.

**B4-14.** Marking point 5. You asked outright whether replication happens on the way to the nucleus or inside it. **Everything goes into the nucleus first**, which is unusual for an RNA virus and is why papers ask.

**B4-15.** Marking points 3 and 4. It is **the low pH of the endosome acting on haemagglutinin** that drives fusion. The lysosome is not part of the answer.

**B4-16.** Haemagglutinin **binds** sialic acid on entry; neuraminidase **cleaves** sialic acid on release. Same molecule, opposite ends of the cycle, which is why without neuraminidase the new virions would stay glued to the cell they came from.

**B4-1.** Marking point 9. The **nucleoprotein** bound along each RNA segment was a total blank first time.

**B4-4.** Part (a). You called SARS-CoV-2 negative-sense. It is **positive**-sense, and that is precisely why it needs no polymerase to start translating.

**B4-20.** Part (c). **Reassortment swaps whole segments between two co-infecting strains and needs a segmented genome. Recombination ends with a single genome carrying both contributions. Point mutation is neither.** Only reassortment gives shift.

**Source:** (a) RI Prelim 2023 P3, stem and both marking points verbatim from the RI answer document. (b) built from LO 2(e) and the chapter's own numbered sequence, `[INFERRED]` where no single paper sets the whole cycle at seven marks. (c) `[INFERRED]` from LO 2(h) with the drift and shift definitions as the chapter words them.

### Question 4

**(a)** Any three.

**gp120 on the viral envelope binds to the CD4 receptor on the helper T cell.**

**This binding triggers a conformational change in gp120 that allows it to bind a co-receptor, CXCR4 on T helper cells or CCR5 on macrophages.**

**This binding triggers an allosteric conformational change in gp41, which penetrates the host cell surface membrane.**

**The envelope membrane of the virus fuses with the host cell surface membrane.**

**The nucleocapsid is released into the cytoplasm, the capsid is degraded and the HIV genome and enzymes are released.**

**(b)** All four.

**The RNA-dependent DNA polymerase activity of reverse transcriptase synthesises a complementary DNA strand using the viral RNA as template, giving an RNA-DNA hybrid.**

**The RNase H activity, at the nuclease active site, hydrolyses the RNA strand of that hybrid.**

**The DNA-dependent DNA polymerase activity of the same enzyme then synthesises the second DNA strand using the first as template.**

**The result is double-stranded viral DNA, which integrase catalyses the integration of into the host chromosome, forming a provirus. No host polymerase is involved at any step.**

**(c)** Any three.

**The primary structure, a unique number and sequence of amino acids linked by peptide bonds.**

**Folding into alpha-helices and beta-pleated sheets, the secondary structure, maintained by hydrogen bonds between the CO and NH groups of the polypeptide backbone.**

**Further folding into the three-dimensional tertiary conformation, maintained by any two of hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions between the R groups, which forms the active sites.**

**Association of the two subunits in the quaternary structure, held by the same kinds of R group interaction, which completes the active sites.**

Gap check. **B4-10 and B4-19 are the same gap and it has now failed twice, in Cluster 1 and Cluster 2.** Part (b) is written so it cannot be dodged: each step names the activity responsible. **One enzyme, three activities.** If you reach for "DNA polymerase" here a third time, stop and write the three activities out longhand before going on.

**B4-17.** Part (a) separates them: **gp120 binds, gp41 fuses.** **B4-18.** **CXCR4 on T helper cells, CCR5 on macrophages.**

Part (c) is a Book 1 protein-structure answer inside a Book 4 stem, which is how the paper actually sets it.

**Source:** (a) and (c) ACJC Prelim 2022 P3 Q1, stems and marking points verbatim including the max 3 cap on (a). (b) `[INFERRED]` from LO 2(e), written to force the three activities apart.

### Question 5

**(a)** Both, one mark each.

**In binary fission the DNA replicates from a single origin of replication, whereas in mitosis each linear chromosome replicates from many origins.**

**In binary fission the chromosomes are separated by elongation of the cell and attachment to the cell surface membrane, with no spindle, no centromeres and no kinetochore microtubules, whereas in mitosis chromosomes condense, align on the metaphase plate and are pulled apart by the spindle.**

**(b)** Both.

**The single circular chromosome is compacted by supercoiling.**

**It is bound and further compacted by histone-LIKE proteins in the nucleoid, which is not membrane-bound.**

Reject: **histones, histone octamer, nucleosome, solenoid, non-histone scaffold, looped domains.** All of those are eukaryotic.

**(c)** Any three.

**Binary fission is asexual reproduction, producing two genetically identical daughter cells, so no genetic variation is generated by the process itself.**

**Desirable alleles are therefore passed on unchanged to every descendant.**

**It causes a rapid increase in cell numbers under favourable conditions.**

**Where a mutation has already arisen in one cell, or a gene has been acquired by transformation, transduction or conjugation, binary fission amplifies that cell into a large clonal population very quickly, which is how a resistant strain sweeps a population.**

Gap check. **B4-21** is part (b), and it is the one you were confident and wrong on. You gave the whole eukaryotic packing answer, lysine-to-phosphate ionic bonds and all. **Supercoiling and histone-like proteins.** Nothing else.

**B4-22** belongs here too: the prokaryotic genome is **smaller**, and say on what axis, **fewer base pairs** or **fewer genes**, since a bare "smaller" is weak.

**B4-24** is part (c). You said binary fission causes a rapid **decrease** in cell numbers, twice. It is an **increase**. And the mutation point is the one you tangled: the process makes no variation, it **amplifies** variation that already exists.

**Source:** (a) RI Prelim 2023 P2, stem and both marking points verbatim. (b) and (c) `[INFERRED]` from LO 2(d) and 2(g), with the reject list taken from the wording the syllabus uses for prokaryotic genome organisation.

### Question 6

**(a)** Any four.

**A donor cell lyses and releases fragments of naked DNA into the surrounding medium.**

**The recipient cell must be competent, that is, able to take up DNA across its cell surface membrane, which requires competence factors, or can be made competent artificially by heat shock or electroporation.**

**The naked DNA fragment is taken up through cell surface proteins of the competent cell.**

**The fragment must be homologous to a region of the recipient's chromosome so that homologous recombination can integrate it.**

**Once integrated, the new allele is replicated and passed to all descendants, so the recipient's genome now differs from its parent's, giving genetic variation.**

**(b)** Both.

**DNase hydrolyses free, naked DNA in the medium.**

**Its presence therefore rules out transformation as the route by which the two strains could exchange genes, so any hybrid colonies that still appear must be explained by something else.**

Gap check. **B4-25** is part (a), and the marking point you had to be shown is the first one: **the DNA fragments must be present in the medium in the first place.** You produced competence and homology unaided.

**B4-23** is part (b), where you guessed DNase blocks synthesis of the sex pilus. It does nothing to the pilus. It **destroys naked DNA**, and the whole point of putting it in the experiment is to eliminate one explanation so the others can be tested.

### Question 7

**(a)** One similarity and one difference, and both are needed for the two marks.

Similarities, any one. **Both involve bacteriophages transferring bacterial DNA from one cell to another, increasing genetic variation. Both result in horizontal gene transfer. Both involve incorporation of the bacterial DNA into the recipient cell by recombination.**

Differences, any one. **In generalised transduction any random fragment of host bacterial DNA can be transferred, while in specialised transduction only specific genes adjacent to the prophage insertion site are transferred. Generalised transduction occurs during the lytic cycle when phages package host DNA by mistake, whereas specialised transduction occurs during the lysogenic cycle when the prophage is excised incorrectly. Generalised transduction produces both normal and transducing phages, while specialised transduction produces only transducing phages.**

The examiner's comment on this question, worth reading twice: *"Many students forget that 'Compare' requires both similarities and differences and are hence capped at only 1 mark. Students are unable to identify that the specific genes excised for specialised transduction should be genes adjacent to prophage insertion site. Many students mention that generalised transduction involves the T4 phage, while specialised transduction involves the Lambda phage. However, this is too specific of a comparison. Generally, generalised transduction involves a virulent phage, while specialised transduction involves a temperate phage."*

So: say **virulent** and **temperate**, not T4 and lambda.

**(b)** Any six.

**A virulent phage infects a donor bacterium and enters the lytic cycle.**

**Host DNA is degraded into fragments.**

**During assembly, a fragment of bacterial DNA is packaged into a phage head by mistake, in place of phage DNA, producing a transducing particle.**

**The cell lyses and the transducing particle is released.**

**The transducing particle attaches to a recipient bacterium and injects the bacterial DNA it is carrying, rather than phage DNA, so the recipient is not killed.**

**The transferred fragment is incorporated into the recipient's chromosome by homologous recombination.**

**The recipient now carries a gene it did not have before and passes it to all its descendants by binary fission, giving genetic variation.**

Gap check. This is one of the two processes you built well from scratch, so this question is a decay test rather than a repair. What it adds is the discipline the examiner asks for: **compare means both sides, and name virulent and temperate rather than T4 and lambda.**

**Source:** (a) YIJC Prelim 2025 P2, stem, all six marking points, the "any 1 from S + any 1 from D" rule and the published examiner comment verbatim. (b) `[INFERRED]` from LO 2(g), following the same scheme's sequence.

### Question 8

**(a)** One similarity and one difference.

Similarities. **Both involve introduction of new genes from a donor to a recipient. Neither requires a viral vector such as a phage.**

Differences, from the paper's own table. **Conjugation transfers the F plasmid, extra-chromosomal DNA, while transformation transfers fragments of foreign naked DNA. In conjugation the source is an F⁺ donor cell, in transformation it is lysed bacterial cells. Conjugation transfers more genes, transformation fewer. Conjugation is via a cytoplasmic mating bridge, transformation via cell surface proteins of competent cells. Homologous recombination does not occur in conjugation but does occur in transformation. Conjugation needs contact between the F⁺ donor and F⁻ recipient; transformation needs no contact.**

Rejects the paper prints: for transformation, **chromosomal DNA**, because that is too big; for conjugation, describing the transfer as being **between F⁺ and F⁺ cells**.

**(b)** Both.

**One strand is nicked and transferred while the other is retained, and each cell then synthesises the complementary strand of the strand it holds.**

**Both cells therefore end up with a complete double-stranded F plasmid and both become F⁺. If the whole double-stranded plasmid crossed, the donor would be left with none and would become F⁻, so the number of donors in the population would not increase.**

**(c)** Any two.

**Genes for antibiotic resistance can be transferred directly to other bacteria, so resistance spreads through the population rapidly.**

**Transfer occurs at a high rate because every recipient that becomes F⁺ can itself act as a donor, so the number of donor cells grows.**

**It generates genetic variation in an otherwise clonal population, which is the raw material for natural selection.**

Gap check. Part (a) marking point on **homologous recombination not occurring in conjugation** is the sharp distinction you already made unaided and should keep: the **F plasmid stays separate**, so nothing has to recombine, whereas transformed and transduced DNA **must** integrate to be inherited.

Part (b) is your own reasoning from the transcript, written up: *"if you shift double-strand, one of them becomes an F-minus. That's counterproductive."* Correct, and worth two marks when stated properly.

**Source:** (a) RI Prelim 2022 P2 Q4(a)(i), stem, the full comparison table and both reject lines verbatim. (b) and (c) `[INFERRED]` from LO 2(g).

### Question 9

**(a)** Both.

**The error: a virus has no plasma membrane.**

**The correction: a naked virus has only a capsid of capsomeres surrounding its genome. An enveloped virus has, outside the capsid, a lipid envelope derived from a host membrane, which is not a plasma membrane of its own because the virus neither makes it nor maintains it.**

**(b)** Both.

**The glycoproteins are of viral origin, because they are encoded by viral genes; the host's rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi are only the machinery used to build and modify them, and the machinery being the host's does not make the product the host's.**

**The lipid bilayer of the envelope is of host origin, because it is taken directly from the host cell membrane as the virion buds out.**

**(c)** One.

**The envelope is a phospholipid bilayer taken from the host membrane, and a phospholipid bilayer is fluid, so it has no fixed geometry and enveloped viruses are pleomorphic. A naked virus is bounded by a capsid built from capsomeres, which has a fixed geometry.**

**(d)** Both.

**Host range is determined by complementarity between the virus's attachment protein and a receptor on the host cell surface. A bacteriophage's tail fibres are complementary to receptors on a bacterial cell surface.**

**Human immune cells do not carry those receptors, so the phage cannot attach, and nothing downstream can happen.**

Gap check. Four gaps in seven marks, all from Cluster 1 or 3.

**B4-26** is part (a). **B4-3** is part (b), and it is the one that genuinely surprised you: *"is the glycoprotein not from the host cell?"* No. **B4-12** is part (c), which you reached only after seeing it. **B4-5** is part (d), where you proposed connecting a phage to an immune cell and then said *"oh f***, another one."*

**B4-2** belongs here too: on an enveloped virion the outermost layer is the **envelope**, and the capsid sits inside it.

**B4-6** is the other half of part (d), and it is a distinction you collapsed. Both of these break complementarity, but they are different events in different organisms. **Antigenic drift is accumulated point mutation in the VIRUS's own surface antigens, haemagglutinin and neuraminidase, so existing antibodies no longer fit it.** **A phage that can no longer infect its host has met a mutation in the BACTERIUM's surface receptor, so the phage's tail fibres no longer fit the cell.** You explained a phage failing to bind by saying antigenic drift changed the receptor. Antigenic drift changes the virus, never the host.
