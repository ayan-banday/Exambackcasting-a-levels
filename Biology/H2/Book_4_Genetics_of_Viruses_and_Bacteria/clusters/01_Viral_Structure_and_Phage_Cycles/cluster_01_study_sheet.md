## A. A virus is an obligate intracellular parasite

**A1. The three words in the phrase are the definition.** **Obligate** means by necessity,
**intracellular** means within the cell, and **parasite** means an organism living and feeding on a
host. Put together, the parasite **cannot survive without its living host cell**. A virus **lacks
enzymes for most metabolic processes** and lacks **the machinery for protein synthesis**, so it depends
on its host for exactly three things: **building blocks such as amino acids and nucleotides**,
**protein-synthesising machinery such as ribosomes**, and **energy in the form of ATP**. Those three
answer any question asking what a virus must take from a host.

**A2. Virion is the extracellular state, virus is the intracellular state.** In the **extracellular
state** the particle is a **virion**, which is **metabolically inert and does not carry out respiratory
or biosynthetic functions**. Its job is to **carry the viral genome from the cell in which it was
produced to another cell**. In the **intracellular state** viral replication occurs: **the genome and
the proteins that make up the virus coat are synthesised and assembled to form new viruses**. A cell a
virus can infect and reproduce in is a **host cell**. Which state you are describing decides whether
every statement about metabolism in section D comes out true or false.

**A3. Host range is set by a complementary fit, and both halves of that get marked.** The suitable host
cells for a particular virus are collectively its **host range**. Viruses identify their hosts by a
**complementary fit between proteins on the outside of the virus and specific receptor molecules on the
surface of the host cell**. Schemes want the word **complementary**, and they want the receptor
**located on the host cell**; leave either out and the mark goes. By host range, viruses sort into
**bacteriophages**, **animal viruses** and **plant viruses**.

## B. Four structural components, and only two of them are universal

**B1. Genome and capsid are present in all viruses; envelope and enzymes are not.** The four basic
structures are the **genome comprising DNA or RNA**, the **capsid, a protein coat comprising protein
subunits called capsomeres**, the **envelope comprising phospholipids from the host cell**, and
**enzymes**. Build every structure answer on that split, because LO 1(e) asks for the components
**including enveloped viruses and bacteriophages**, which is the outcome asking you to say which
component belongs to which.

**B2. The capsid is a protein coat assembled from identical subunits.** All viruses have a protein
coat, the **capsid**, which **either encloses or surrounds the viral genome**, and each capsid is
**constructed from identical protein subunits called capsomeres**. Depending on how the capsomeres are
shaped and arranged, the assembly gives a **helical** or an **icosahedral** capsid. On a labelling
question the wanted word is **capsid**, and protein coat and capsomere are both rejected as the label
even though the definition uses them.

**B3. The envelope is host membrane, but the glycoproteins in it are not.** The envelope is **derived
from host cells**: when viruses are **released from the host cell by budding, they take with them the
host's cell surface membrane, that is the phospholipid bilayer**, and **proteins of viral origin are
inserted into the membrane**. Among those are **viral glycoproteins, exposed on the outside of the
envelope**, which are **essential for the attachment of viruses to the next host cell**. The envelope
**protects the virion's nucleic acid from the effects of various enzymes and chemicals**. Viruses not
surrounded by the lipid membrane envelope are **naked or non-enveloped viruses**.

The split of origin is a standing mark point. **The bilayer is host-derived; the glycoproteins are
encoded by the viral genome.** An examiner comment on this exact part records that most candidates
wrote that both came from the previous host, and the scheme prints a reject for it. Identification is
policed just as hard: the wanted answer is **viral envelope**, with protein envelope, phospholipid
bilayer and double bilayer all rejected.

**B4. Which enzymes a virus carries is dictated by what its genome cannot do.** **Lysozyme makes a
small hole in the bacterial cell wall that allows the viral nucleic acid to enter**, and the same
enzyme, produced in large amounts later in infection, **causes lysis of the host cell and release of
the virus**. **Neuraminidase breaks down glycosidic bonds of glycoproteins and glycolipids of the
connective tissue of animal cells, aiding in the liberation of the virus**, and it is **associated with
the viral envelope instead of being located within the capsid**.

Then the four polymerases: **DNA-dependent RNA polymerase**, **RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, also
called viral replicase**, **DNA-dependent DNA polymerase**, and **RNA-dependent DNA polymerase, also
called reverse transcriptase**. One convention decodes all four: **the dependent part of the name
indicates the type of nucleic acid being copied, and the polymerase part indicates the type of nucleic
acid being synthesised**.

**B5. Interpreting an unfamiliar virus drawing is part of the outcome.** LO 1(e) ends with **interpret
drawings and photographs of them**, and the commonest opening move in this Book is a labelled
cross-section of a virus you have never seen. The skill is precision rather than recognition:
**envelope**, **glycoprotein**, **capsid**, **nucleoprotein** and **genome** each have one accepted word
and several rejected ones, and those five labels transfer to any virus in the stem.

## C. The viral genome, described on the axes the outcome names

**C1. DNA or RNA, but never both.** All eukaryotic cells contain double-stranded DNA genomes, while
**virus genomes are highly diverse and contain either DNA or RNA, but not both**. They subdivide by
whether they are **single-stranded or double-stranded** and **linear or circular**; some viral
chromosomes are circular, **but most are linear**. Size runs from the **1759 bases of the
single-stranded DNA viruses to over one million base pairs**, which is larger than some bacterial
genomes. Those are the axes LO 2(d) lists, so run them in order and the description writes itself.

**C2. The sense of an RNA genome decides which enzyme the virion has to carry.** **Positive-sense RNA
is identical to viral mRNA and can therefore be immediately translated.** **Negative-sense RNA is
complementary to viral mRNA and must be used as a template for the synthesis of positive-sense RNA by
RNA polymerase before translation.** Say it as templating and synthesis, never as conversion: schemes
reject the idea that one nucleic acid is turned into or converted into another, because the original
strand survives and a new complementary strand is made.

**C3. Three of the seven nucleic-acid groups carry the syllabus examples.** **Group I, double-stranded
DNA viruses, example lambda phage.** **Group V, negative-sense single-stranded RNA viruses, example
influenza**, whose genome must be used to make positive-sense RNA by an **RNA-dependent RNA
polymerase** before translation. **Group VI, single-stranded RNA reverse transcriptase viruses, example
HIV**, which uses **reverse transcriptase, an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase, to produce DNA from the
viral RNA genome**. Groups II, III, IV and VII are marked FYI by the school; read them once for the
pattern and drop them.

**C4. Every viral genome, whatever its type, encodes two classes of protein.** **Regulatory proteins**
are **necessary for regulating the action of the host genes**, so that **host cell machinery is used by
the virus to replicate the viral genome** and synthesise the components needed to form the virus.
**Structural proteins, such as the viral capsid protein, are essential for assembly of the complete
virus.** Anything beyond those two, such as viral replicase in influenza or reverse transcriptase in
HIV, is present only because that virus's cycle needs it.

## D. Viruses challenge the cell theory because they satisfy parts of it and fail others

**D1. Start from the definition of life the chapter tests them against.** The seven listed
characteristics are **growth**, **reproduction**, **nutrition**, **metabolism including cellular
respiration and biosynthesis**, **movement**, **irritability or the ability to respond to stimuli**, and
**excretion**. Viruses **are able to reproduce and respond to stimuli while in the intracellular
state**, but in the extracellular state they are **unable to carry out metabolic processes such as
nucleic acid and protein synthesis**, **do not require nutrition**, are **unable to synthesise their
own ATP as an energy source**, are **unable to respond to stimuli**, and **neither grow nor excrete**.

**D2. Answer the three statements of the cell theory one at a time, each from both sides.** LO 1(f)
says **discuss**, the only two-sided command word in this Book, so a list of things viruses do is not
the answer shape. The shape is three statements, each conceded and then qualified.

| Statement of the cell theory | Where a virus fits it | Where a virus fails it |
|---|---|---|
| **Cells are the smallest unit of life** | viruses **contain the genetic material necessary to form the next generation** and are **able to evolve in response to the environment** | they **lack the necessary molecular machinery to conduct many of the biochemical reactions a normal cell would need** |
| **All cells come from pre-existing cells** | viruses **are capable of replicating**, and their genes are replicated on infection | they **rely on host cells to provide the energy and materials** and **cannot replicate unless they have entered a suitable host cell**. **A new virion is never formed directly from a pre-existing virion**; replication is **synthesis of a large number of viral components followed by their assembly into virions** |
| **All living organisms are composed of cells** | viruses **direct metabolic processes** such as **protein synthesis and genome replication** in the intracellular state | viruses **are acellular and do not have protoplasm or organelles**, and as a virion the particle is **metabolically inert** |

**D3. Viral genomes evolve, which is the strongest argument on the living side.** **No single gene is
shared by all viruses or viral lineages**, and viral genomes can be **single-stranded or double-stranded
DNA or RNA**, which indicates that **viruses descended from more than one ancestral line and do not
share a common ancestor**. They also **evolve with their host and acquire their metabolic and
translational genes from the host cells**, and **genetic recombination can result in changing viral
genomes**.

**D4. The chapter's own conclusion is the safest closing line.** Because they are difficult to classify
against the cell theory, **it is best to describe viruses as infectious particles which are active as
the intracellular virus state, or inactive as the extracellular virion state, as opposed to living or
non-living**. That resolves the discussion rather than leaving it open, which is what the command word
wants.

## E. Every reproductive cycle runs the same five slots

**Adsorption**, **penetration**, **synthesis and replication**, **assembly**, **release**. What changes
between one virus and the next is what fills each slot, not the frame, so learn the frame once and hang
three cycles on it. The general enveloped-virus version reads: attachment by **specific binding of its
glycoproteins to host cell receptors**; **the virus is engulfed into a vesicle and its envelope is
uncoated, freeing the viral nucleic acid into the cytoplasm**; **under the control of viral genes the
cell synthesises nucleic acid, capsomeres and glycoproteins**; the **nucleocapsid is formed** and
glycoproteins are **inserted into the host cell membrane**; then **enveloped viruses bud off the
membrane, carrying away an envelope with the glycoproteins**.

## F. The lytic cycle kills the host, and T4 is the virulent case

**F1. The definitions come first, because phage type and cycle are marked as a pair.** A phage
reproductive cycle that **finally results in death of the host cell is known as a lytic cycle**, and
**lytic refers to the last stage of infection, during which the bacterium lyses and releases the new
phages** produced within the cell. A phage that **reproduces only by a lytic cycle, such as T4, is a
virulent phage**. Bacteriophages themselves are **DNA viruses that infect bacteria**.

**F2. Adsorption is attachment and nothing more.** The **multiple tail fibres of the T4 phage attach to
specific receptor sites on the surface of a bacterial host cell** such as E. coli, and the **base plate
settles down on the host cell surface**. On a data question showing free virus numbers falling, the
cause is **attachment of viruses to the bacteria**, and a scheme prints a reject for saying the viruses
have entered the host cell.

**F3. Penetration is mechanical and enzymatic together, and the capsid stays outside.**
**Conformational changes occur in the tail sheath causing it to contract**, and its **core pierces
through the bacterial cell wall and cell membrane**. **T4 uses lysozyme to hydrolyse peptidoglycan,
degrading a portion of the bacterial cell wall for insertion of the tail core.** **DNA is extruded from
the head, through the tail tube, into the host cell.** **The capsid is left on the outside of the
bacterial cell wall.** That last line recurs as a mark point, and it is also the one most often
misdeployed: in a comparison against an enveloped virus the difference has to be stated on the same
feature for both viruses, not as one having something the other lacks.

**F4. Synthesis is a hijack, and the host genome becomes raw material.** **Soon after the phage DNA is
injected, the synthesis of host DNA, RNA and proteins is halted** and the **host cell machinery is
taken over**. **Phage DNA is replicated by host DNA polymerase**, and **the host DNA is degraded into
nucleotides, providing raw materials for phage DNA replication**. **Phage mRNAs are synthesised by the
host RNA polymerase**, then **translated by host cell ribosomes, tRNAs and translation factors** into
viral proteins and enzymes, including **inhibitory factors that stop host cell RNA and protein
synthesis**.

**F5. Assembly and release.** **Viral proteins are assembled to form phage heads, tails and tail
fibres**, and the components are **assembled into the complete bacteriophage**. Then **the phages lyse
the host cell by the action of the enzyme lysozyme, which digests the bacterial cell wall**, and
**water enters the cell by osmosis causing the cell to swell and burst**. The osmotic step earns a
separate mark from the enzyme.

## G. The lysogenic cycle hides the genome instead of spending it, and lambda is the temperate case

**G1. Temperate is defined by having both options.** **Phages capable of using both the lytic and
lysogenic modes of reproduction within a bacterium are called temperate phages**, the example being
**lambda phage**. In contrast to the lytic cycle, which kills the host, the **lysogenic cycle involves
replication of the phage genome without destroying the host in the initial steps**.

**G2. Lambda's structure differs from T4's in two ways that matter.** Its genome is **linear
double-stranded DNA**, and **the 5' terminus of each DNA strand is a single-stranded tail of 12
nucleotides, which is important in prophage formation**. It has **a single non-contractile tail
fibre**, which **allows the phage to adsorb onto the surface of the bacterial cell by binding to the
specific receptor site**. Because the tail is not contractile, it **serves to deliver the viral DNA to
the cell membrane** rather than driving a tube through the wall, and **DNA is extruded from the head
through the tail tube and injected into the host cell**. As in T4, **the capsid is left on the outside
of the bacterial cell wall**.

**G3. Prophage formation is the step the cycle is named for.** **The lambda phage genome circularises
and inserts itself into a specific site on the bacterial chromosome, known as the prophage insertion
site, by genetic recombination.** **This does not cause any loss of the host DNA.** **The integrated
lambda phage is known as a prophage.** Two details carry the marks, **circularisation** and the **named
insertion site**, because together they make the insertion specific rather than random, and that
specificity is what produces specialised transduction in Cluster 3.

**G4. In the integrated state the phage is propagated for free.** **The viral DNA is replicated along
with the chromosome each time the host cell divides, and is passed on to generations of host daughter
cells**, so **a single infected cell can soon give rise to a large population of bacteria carrying the
viral DNA in prophage form**. That is the survival argument for lysogeny: the genome multiplies without
killing a host and without needing a fresh infection each round.

**G5. Spontaneous induction switches it back.** **When there is an environmental trigger, such as UV
radiation or the presence of certain chemicals, the virus switches from the lysogenic cycle to the
lytic cycle.** **Lysis genes which were repressed during lysogeny are activated, allowing the lambda
phage genome to be excised from the bacterial chromosome to give rise to new active phages.** From
there, **steps 3 to 5 of the lytic cycle resume**, so synthesis, assembly and release are never learned
twice.

## H. Lytic against lysogenic, on the axes that get compared

| | **Lytic cycle** | **Lysogenic cycle** |
|---|---|---|
| Phage type | **virulent phage**, T4 | **temperate phage**, lambda |
| Fate of the host | **death of the host cell** by **lysis** | **host is not destroyed in the initial steps** |
| State of the phage genome | free in the host cell, replicated by host machinery | **integrated into the bacterial chromosome as a prophage** |
| How copies are made | many phage genomes replicated per infection, then assembled | **one copy replicated along with the host chromosome at every cell division** |
| Exit | **lysozyme digests the cell wall**, **water enters by osmosis**, cell bursts | no exit until **spontaneous induction** on an environmental trigger |
| Time course | one round, then the host is spent | **latent**, potentially for many host generations |

**The two cycles are two options open to the same kind of virus, which is why the pairing has to be
automatic.** A **virulent** phage running a **lytic** cycle mispackages a **random** fragment of the
host genome, which is **generalised transduction**. A **temperate** phage excising imprecisely from its
insertion site takes the genes **adjacent** to that site, which is **specialised transduction**. Neither
chapter writes that sentence down, and Cluster 3 spends a section on it.
