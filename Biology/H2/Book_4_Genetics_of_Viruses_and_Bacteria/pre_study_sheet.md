## PART A: GENETICS OF VIRUSES

### 1. Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites that challenge the definition of life

- **1.1** They lack metabolic enzymes, ribosomes and ATP production, so they can only reproduce inside a living host cell.
- **1.2** Two states: extracellular **virion** (inert) vs intracellular virus (actively replicating).
- **1.3** Arguments FOR living: they reproduce, direct metabolic processes (inside hosts), and evolve.
- **1.4** Arguments AGAINST: not cells, no organelles, metabolically inert outside hosts, do not grow, excrete or respond alone.
- **1.5** They challenge all 3 tenets of **cell theory** (smallest unit of life; cells from pre-existing cells; organisms made of cells), so they are best described as infectious particles that are "active" or "inactive", not living or non-living.

### 2. Viruses share a basic structure, with variations

- **2.1** All viruses: a **genome** (DNA or RNA, never both) plus a **capsid** (protein coat of **capsomeres**).
- **2.2** Genomes are diverse: single or double stranded, linear or circular, and RNA can be **(+) sense** (acts directly as mRNA) or **(−) sense** (must be copied to (+) first).
- **2.3** Some viruses add an **envelope** (phospholipid bilayer stolen from host membrane, studded with viral **glycoproteins** for attachment).
- **2.4** Some carry their own enzymes: entry and exit enzymes (**lysozyme**, **neuraminidase**) and polymerases (**RNA-dependent RNA polymerase**, **reverse transcriptase**) for jobs the host cannot do.

### 3. All viral reproduction follows the same 5-step logic

- **3.1** **Adsorption** (specific virus-protein-to-host-receptor binding, which explains narrow host range) → **Penetration** → **Synthesis and replication** (hijack host machinery) → **Assembly** → **Release**.
- **3.2** How each step happens differs by virus type (phage vs enveloped vs retrovirus), which is Section 4.

### 4. Three model viruses show three different reproductive strategies

- **4.1** **Bacteriophages** (DNA viruses infecting bacteria):
    - **T4** (virulent) → **LYTIC** cycle: inject DNA, capsid stays outside; host DNA degraded; host machinery makes phage parts; cell lyses (lysozyme + osmosis) releasing phages. Host dies.
    - **Lambda** (temperate) → **LYSOGENIC** cycle: phage DNA circularises and integrates into the bacterial chromosome as a **prophage**; silently copied with every host division; an environmental trigger (e.g. UV) induces excision, which switches it to the lytic cycle.
- **4.2** **Influenza** (enveloped animal virus): genome = eight segments of (−) sense ssRNA; enters by receptor-mediated endocytosis (**HA** binds sialic acid); carries its own replicase to make (+) RNA (for translation) and new (−) RNA (genomes); exits by **budding**; **NA** cleaves receptors to free new virions.
- **4.3** **HIV** (retrovirus): genome = two identical (+) ssRNA; **gp120** binds **CD4** on T-helper cells; **reverse transcriptase** makes dsDNA from RNA; **integrase** inserts it into the host genome as a **provirus** (can lie latent for years); when activated, host machinery makes viral RNA and proteins; buds off; **protease** cleaves the polyprotein into the mature virus.

### 5. Viral genomes vary constantly, which lets viruses evade immunity

- **5.1** Three molecular mechanisms of variation:
    - **Mutation**: no proofreading of RNA plus error-prone viral polymerases and RT, giving a high mutation rate.
    - **Recombination**: genomes of two strains exchange genetic information.
    - **Reassortment**: two strains co-infect one cell and their genome segments get shuffled into progeny.
- **5.2** **Antigenic DRIFT** = gradual accumulation of small mutations in surface antigen genes (HA/NA), giving minor antigen changes and seasonal epidemics (some pre-existing immunity remains).
- **5.3** **Antigenic SHIFT** = sudden **reassortment** of segmented genomes from different strains (often via an intermediate host like a pig), giving a radically new HA/NA combination, no population immunity, and pandemic potential.
- **5.4** Both work by changing antigen 3D shape so existing antibodies no longer bind. Shift is rare but drastic, drift is constant but minor.

## PART B: GENETICS OF BACTERIA

### 1. Bacteria are structurally simple cells, fundamentally different from eukaryotes

- **1.1** Bacteria come in 3 basic shapes: spherical (**coccus**), rod (**bacillus**), spiral.
- **1.2** They are **prokaryotic**: no true nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles, smaller (**70S**) ribosomes.
- **1.3** Surface structures matter: **peptidoglycan** cell wall (not cellulose), cell membrane, capsule, flagella, pili.
- **1.4** **Endosymbiont theory**: mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living prokaryotes engulfed by an ancestral cell, which links prokaryotes and eukaryotes evolutionarily (evidence: circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, splitting replication, similar membranes).

### 2. The prokaryotic genome is organised very differently from the eukaryotic genome

- **2.1** Main genome = ONE circular, double-stranded DNA chromosome carrying essential genes, packed in the **nucleoid** (no membrane), compacted by **histone-like proteins** and **supercoiling**.
- **2.2** Bacterial genes have NO **introns**, so the coding sequence runs uninterrupted.
- **2.3** **Plasmids** = extra, small, circular dsDNA carrying non-essential but beneficial genes (e.g. antibiotic resistance); replicate independently (own origin of replication).

### 3. Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission, which creates NO variation

- **3.1** One parent cell → two genetically identical daughter cells.
- **3.2** Process logic: chromosome attaches to membrane → DNA replicates bidirectionally from a single origin (semi-conservative) → cell elongates, chromosomes separate → septum forms → cytokinesis.
- **3.3** Because daughters are identical, variation must come from elsewhere (mutation + gene transfer → Section 4).

### 4. Genetic variation in bacteria comes from horizontal gene transfer (3 mechanisms)

- **4.0** Unifying idea: a donor cell gives DNA to a recipient cell; for the DNA to be kept and inherited, it must usually be integrated into the recipient's chromosome by **homologous recombination** (exception: conjugation, where the F plasmid stays separate).
- **4.1** **Transformation** = recipient takes up naked DNA from the environment (from lysed cells); requires the cell to be **competent**; can be induced artificially (heat shock / electroporation), which is the basis of recombinant DNA technology.
- **4.2** **Transduction** = a bacteriophage accidentally carries bacterial DNA from one host to another. Two versions:
    - **Generalised**: virulent phage, lytic cycle; a random fragment of host DNA gets packaged into a capsid by mistake, so ANY gene can be transferred.
    - **Specialised**: temperate phage, lysogenic cycle; imprecise excision of the prophage drags along ONLY genes adjacent to the insertion site.
- **4.3** **Conjugation** = direct cell-to-cell transfer via a **sex pilus** and cytoplasmic mating bridge; donor (F⁺) has an **F plasmid**, one strand is nicked and transferred, both cells replicate the strand, so both end up F⁺.

## One-line mental map

**Viruses:** not-quite-alive particles → genome + capsid (± envelope, enzymes) → 5-step hijack cycle (lytic / lysogenic / budding / retro variants) → mutation, recombination, reassortment → drift and shift.

**Bacteria:** simple cell → circular genome + plasmids → binary fission copies it → 3 transfer mechanisms create variation.
