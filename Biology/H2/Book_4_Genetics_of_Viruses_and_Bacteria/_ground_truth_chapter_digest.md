### 2. Content node map

- **What is a virus** (p.5 to 7)
  - Obligate intracellular parasite, unpacked word by word, and the three things a virus must take from a host cell
  - Virion as the extracellular state, metabolically inert, versus the intracellular virus state
  - Reasons for studying viruses, boxed: Hershey and Chase, sources of enzymes, antibacterial agents, anticancer agents, gene vectors for protein production, gene vectors for gene therapy
  - The seven-item definition of life the chapter tests viruses against
  - Arguments for viruses being living, organised as three "cell theory" headings: they reproduce, they direct metabolic processes, their genomes evolve
  - Arguments for viruses being non-living: they are not cells, and in the extracellular state they lack five listed characteristics
  - How viruses challenge the cell theory, taken clause by clause against the three statements of the theory
  - The chapter's conclusion, that viruses are better described as infectious particles that are active or inactive than as living or non-living
- **General structure of viruses** (p.8 to 15)
  - Size range 20 to 300 nm and the consequent need for electron microscopy
  - The four basic structures: genome, capsid, envelope, enzymes, with which are universal and which are not
  - Structure and organisation of the viral genome
    - DNA or RNA but never both, single or double stranded, linear or circular
    - The seven Baltimore-style groups, with groups II, III, IV and VII marked (FYI) and groups I, V and VI carrying the syllabus examples
    - Genome size range, from 1759 bases to over one million base pairs
    - What every viral genome must encode: regulatory proteins and structural proteins
  - Capsid: universal, built from capsomeres, helical or icosahedral
  - Envelope: enveloped viruses only, derived from the host cell surface membrane by budding, studded with viral glycoproteins, protective, with naked viruses as the contrast
  - Enzymes: lysozyme and neuraminidase for entry and release, and the four polymerases, with the "dependent" naming convention explained
- **General reproductive cycle of viruses** (p.16)
  - Host range, and identification of the host by complementary fit between viral surface protein and host receptor
  - The three host-range groups: bacteriophages, animal viruses, plant viruses
  - The five general steps: adsorption, penetration, synthesis and replication, assembly, release
- **Bacteriophages** (p.17 to 24)
  - Bacteriophages as DNA viruses of bacteria, capable of both cycles, and their role in transduction
  - The generalised DNA-virus replication model: host transcribes phage genes, host translates the mRNA, host machinery replicates the phage genome
  - T4 phage, marked (FYI): head, tail sheath, tail fibres, base plate, and what each does
  - The lytic cycle in five steps, with T4 as the virulent phage
  - Lambda phage: linear double-stranded DNA, the 12-nucleotide single-stranded 5' tails, a single non-contractile tail fibre
  - The lysogenic cycle: adsorption, penetration, prophage formation by circularisation and site-specific recombination, then spontaneous induction back into the lytic cycle
  - Concept Check: label an electron micrograph, then describe what happened before it
- **Enveloped animal viruses** (p.25 to 35)
  - Influenza virus
    - Eight segments of negative-sense single-stranded RNA, and why they must be copied to positive sense
    - Types A, B and C, marked (FYI)
    - Structure: nucleoprotein capsid, envelope, haemagglutinin and neuraminidase, the M1 and M2 matrix layer, and the PB1, PB2, PA and NS1 proteins
    - The five-step reproductive cycle, with the replicase doing double duty for transcription and replication
    - Neuraminidase at release, and neuraminidase inhibitors as the drug class
  - Human immunodeficiency virus
    - Retrovirus defined as an enveloped RNA virus replicating through a DNA intermediate
    - Structure: two identical single-stranded RNAs, capsid, envelope, gp120 and gp41, matrix, and the three enzymes reverse transcriptase, integrase and protease
    - The five-step reproductive cycle, with reverse transcription and integration expanded into six numbered sub-steps
    - Provirus and latency, and maturation by protease after budding
- **Mechanisms for variation in viral genomes** (p.36 to 43)
  - The three mechanisms: mutation, recombination, reassortment
  - Antigen, antigenicity and antibody defined, flagged forward to Infectious Diseases
  - Antigenic shift: definition, reassortment in a co-infected host, the pig as mixing vessel, the three modes of inter-species transmission
  - Antigenic drift: definition, accumulation of point mutations in the haemagglutinin and neuraminidase genes, and the stated reasons the mutation rate is high
  - Effects of both: escape from antibodies, vaccine failure, pandemic versus seasonal epidemic
  - The six-row comparison table of shift against drift

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 "Antigenic shift" is used for two incompatible things, and one of them is flatly wrong.** This is the most serious error in the chapter and it sits on the exact distinction LO 2(f) exists to test.

- Page 38, the definition, correct: "Antigenic shift is a sudden change in the antigenicity of a virus owing to **reassortment** of the segmented virus genome with another genome of a different antigenic type."
- Page 43, the comparison table, correct and consistent: under Antigenic Shift, "Number of viruses: **Two or more viral strains are involved**"; "Mechanism for change: **Reshuffling of genome between different strains**".
- Page 40, contradicting both: "**Antigenic Shift 2** ... **Without undergoing genetic change**, a bird strain of influenza A can jump directly from a duck or other aquatic bird to humans." And "**Antigenic Shift 3** ... **Without undergoing genetic change**, a bird strain of influenza A can jump directly from a duck or other aquatic bird to an intermediate animal host and then to humans."

A cross-species jump with no genetic change is not antigenic shift under the chapter's own definition, under the chapter's own table, or under any standard usage. It is direct zoonotic transmission of an unchanged strain. Only the chapter's "Antigenic Shift 1", where avian and human strains co-infect an intermediate host and reassort, is antigenic shift. `[SCHOOL]` on both conflicting statements, `[INFERRED]` on the correction. **Learn shift as reassortment, always requiring two or more strains in one host, and treat page 40's modes 2 and 3 as routes of cross-species transmission rather than as shift.**

**5.2 Two figure-reference errors and one stale figure label, all in the lambda phage section.**

- Page 21: "the lysogenic cycle (**Fig. 18**) involves replication of the phage genome without destroying the host". Fig. 18 is the electron micrograph of an influenza virus on page 25. The intended reference is **Fig. 17 on page 23**, which is the combined lytic and lysogenic cycle diagram. The parallel sentence on page 19 gets it right: "known as a lytic cycle (Fig. 17)".
- Page 21: the caption block for the lambda micrograph prints **twice**, once as "**Fig. 19** / Electron micrograph of a lambda λ phage." and immediately below it as "Fig. 15: Electron photomicrograph of a lambda phage". The Fig. 19 label is a leftover from an earlier numbering, and Fig. 19 in this chapter is actually the influenza diagram on page 25.
- Page 22, the closing line of the lysogenic section: "Upon exiting the lysogenic cycle, steps 3 – 5 of the lytic cycle resumes (Pg: 19 & 20)." That cross-reference is correct.

**5.3 Lambda is given a base plate it does not have, in the chapter's own account.** Page 21, Specific lambda Bacteriophage Structural Features, lists exactly two things: "(1) Head contains the DNA of the virus" and "(2) A single tail fibre (non-contractile)". No base plate. Then eight lines later, page 21, Step 1: Adsorption: "The single tail fibres of lambda (λ) phages attach to specific receptor sites on the surface of a bacterial host cell. **The base plate settles down on the host's cell surface.**" That sentence is copied verbatim from the T4 lytic-cycle step on page 19, where T4 does have a base plate because page 18 gives it one. `[SCHOOL]` The safe answer for lambda adsorption names the **tail fibre** and the specific receptor site and stops there.

**5.4 The influenza penetration step names the wrong organelle, and contradicts the chapter's own M2 entry.**

- Page 26, the structure list, essentially correct: "**M2 – acts as an ion channel to lower or maintain the pH of the endosome** in the host cell".
- Page 27, the penetration step: "**Fusion of this vesicle with an acidic lysosome lowers the pH of the vesicle.** This triggers conformational changes in the HA protein, which causes the viral envelope and endosome membranes to fuse".

Endosomal acidification is done by the host cell's proton pumps in the endosome membrane, assisted for the virion interior by the viral M2 channel that page 26 has already named. Fusion with a lysosome would deliver the virion to hydrolases and destroy it, which is what happens when influenza fails to escape in time. `[INFERRED]` on the correction. The two pages give two different accounts of the same pH drop, and the page 26 account is the one to keep. Write **the endosome becomes acidified**, not **the vesicle fuses with a lysosome**.

**5.5 Influenza is repeatedly said to have capsomeres, contradicting the chapter's own capsid entry for it.**

- Page 26: "(2) Capsid • **nucleoprotein (NP)** associate with the viral nucleic acid to form **nucleocapsid**".
- Page 28: "Three sets of proteins are synthesised, namely enzymes, matrix and **capsomeres** proteins as well as glycoproteins" and "Free ribosomes are used to synthesise enzymes, matrix and **capsomeres**".
- Page 34, the same sentence recycled for HIV: "Viral proteins synthesised include enzymes, matrix and **capsomeres** proteins as well as glycoproteins."

Influenza's genome is wrapped in a helical ribonucleoprotein by NP, not in an icosahedral shell of capsomeres, and the chapter says so itself on page 26. Pages 28 and 34 are boilerplate reused from the general five-step template on page 16. `[SCHOOL]` For influenza, say **nucleoprotein** and **nucleocapsid**.

**5.6 The stated reason RNA viruses mutate fast is wrong on page 36 and only half right on page 41.**

- Page 36: "As **RNA is typically only a messenger molecule in eukaryotic cells, there are no proofreading mechanisms in the host cell.** As a result, RNA viruses will experience much higher rates of mutations since errors are not corrected."
- Page 41: "As the genome of influenza consists of 8 single-strand RNA strands which **lack a complementary strand**, polymerases cannot perform proofreading during replication."
- Page 41, the correct half, immediately below: "**Viral polymerases are also prone to errors** and will introduce mutations during the course of DNA replications."

The real reason is that viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerases and reverse transcriptases have **no 3' to 5' exonuclease proofreading activity of their own**. It is a property of the viral enzyme, not an absence in the host, and not a consequence of single-strandedness, since a complementary strand does exist during replication. `[INFERRED]` The corpus supports this: several prelim schemes credit "lack of proofreading ability of RNA-dependent RNA polymerase" or "of the reverse transcriptase" as the mark point. Note the third error hiding in the correct half: influenza replicates **RNA**, not "DNA replications".

**5.7 The negative-sense RNA is said to be "converted to" positive sense, and the two chapters do not agree.** Chapter 4.1 page 1 prints: negative-sense RNA "must be **converted to** positive-sense RNA by RNA polymerase before translation". Chapter 4.2 page 1 prints what is otherwise the same paragraph as: negative-sense RNA "must be **used as a template for the synthesis of** positive-sense RNA by RNA polymerase before translation". The 4.2 wording is correct and 4.1's is loose. The same looseness runs through Fig. 20's caption on page 27, which says the genome "is first **converted to** positive (+) sense single-stranded RNA ... before being **converted back to** a negative (-) sense single-stranded RNA". Nothing is converted; a complementary strand is synthesised and the original strand survives. `[SCHOOL]` for both wordings. This matters because the corpus records a prelim scheme rejecting the equivalent phrasing on the HIV side, "reject the idea that RNA is 'turned into / converted into' DNA".

**5.8 "Hemagglutinin" and "haemagglutinin" are both used, and the mark schemes want one of them.** The chapter spells it **haemagglutinin** twice, on pages 26 and 27, and **hemagglutinin** nine times, on pages 37, 38, 41 and 43. The corpus records a scheme awarding the mark for "**Haemagglutinin**" with "(Reject: HA, NA)" beside it. `[SCHOOL]` Write the British spelling in full, and never abbreviate to HA or NA on a question that asks you to state or name.

**5.9 Section 5's own three-mechanism framing does not survive into section 5.4.** Page 36 sets out **three** mechanisms of viral genome variation: mutation, recombination and reassortment, and Fig. 29's caption confirms "Three molecular mechanisms". Recombination is then never mentioned again in the chapter. Section 5.1 onwards deals only with shift and drift, and the page 43 summary table has no recombination row. The chapter has introduced a mechanism it does not use. This is not a factual error, but it leaves recombination undefined in operational terms, and the corpus does ask for it. `[SCHOOL]`

**5.10 Two definitional wordings on page 38 are garbled as printed.**

- "In the influenza virus, antigenic shift results in **new hemagglutinin and/or new hemagglutinin and neuraminidase proteins** in the viruses that infect humans." As printed, the two halves of the "and/or" overlap, so the sentence says nothing. The intended sense is a new haemagglutinin, or a new haemagglutinin **and** a new neuraminidase.
- "Antigenic shift results in a new influenza A subtype **or a virus that has emerged from an animal population** that is so different from the same subtype in humans that most people do not have immunity to the new virus." This second clause is the sentence that page 40's modes 2 and 3 appear to have been built from, and it is the root of error 5.1.

**Load-bearing, and this is where every mark in the chapter sits:**

The corpus is 44.0% figure-led for this chapter, and the single most common opening move is a labelled cross-section of an unfamiliar virus with four labels to identify.

The structure the chapter gives, taking each of the three statements of the cell theory and saying how a virus both fits and fails it, is the answer shape.
- **The lytic cycle in five steps, pages 19 and 20.** Adsorption by tail fibres to specific receptors, penetration with sheath contraction and lysozyme, synthesis using host polymerases and ribosomes with host DNA degraded for nucleotides, assembly, then lysis by lysozyme with water entering by osmosis. The detail that the **capsid is left outside the cell wall** is a recurring mark point.
- **The lysogenic cycle, pages 21 and 22, including prophage formation and spontaneous induction.** LO 2(e)i names lambda phage explicitly. The mark-bearing details are circularisation, insertion at the prophage insertion site by genetic recombination, replication along with the host chromosome at every division, and the environmental trigger that excises it.
- **The influenza cycle, pages 27 to 29**, with the replicase doing two jobs. Haemagglutinin to sialic acid, receptor-mediated endocytosis into an endosome, acidification triggering the HA conformational change and membrane fusion, replicase copying negative to positive sense, positive sense serving both as mRNA and as template for new genomes, budding, then neuraminidase cleaving sialic acid to release. The reason the replicase must be carried **inside the virion** is that animal cells cannot make RNA from an RNA template, and Fig. 20's caption says so.
- **The HIV cycle, pages 33 to 35.** gp120 to CD4, conformational change, co-receptor CXCR4 or CCR5, gp41 and fusion, reverse transcription in two passes with RNase H degrading the RNA strand between them, integrase forming the provirus, latency, transcription giving both genomes and mRNA, budding, then protease-driven maturation. The two-pass structure of reverse transcription is worth its own drill.
- **Antigenic shift and antigenic drift, pages 38 to 43**, with the page 43 comparison table as the answer skeleton. Six axes, both sides stated positively. This table is the most directly reproducible block in the chapter.
- **Why the changes matter, page 43.** New surface protein means a different 3D conformation, means existing antibodies are no longer complementary, means no recognition and no binding, means immune escape and vaccine failure. That chain is worth four marks on its own and it links straight into Infectious Diseases.

**Read once and move on:**

- **The Reasons for Studying Viruses box on page 5.** Six bullet points on phage therapy, viral enzymes, oncolytic viruses and gene vectors. No outcome. Useful only as stem context.
- **T4's specific structural features on page 18**, which the school marks (FYI) and which chapter 4.2 calls "not in syllabus". Keep the lytic cycle; drop the base plate pin, the central tube and the sheath anatomy as recall.
- **Influenza types A, B and C on page 26**, marked (FYI) by the school.
- **The M1 and M2 detail, the PB1, PB2, PA and NS1 inventory on page 26.** Know that the virion carries its own replicase and why. The subunit names are past what any outcome asks for.
- **Neuraminidase inhibitors, Fig. 23B on page 30.** A drug mechanism with no outcome in Core Idea 2. It is excellent stem material and the corpus uses it that way, so understand it, but do not memorise it as recall.
- **Antigen, antigenicity and antibody on page 37.** The chapter itself says "These terms will be further discussed in the topic of ETA: Infectious Diseases." Learn them there.
- **Fig. 33 on page 39, the 20th-century pandemic history.** Named strains and years. Context, not content.

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 The chapter defines the F factor in a way that includes the state the syllabus excludes.** Page 25: "The donor bacterial cell possesses an F factor ('F' for fertility), which exists as a plasmid known as the F plasmid **or as a segment of DNA integrated into the bacterial chromosome**." A cell whose F factor is integrated into the chromosome is by definition an Hfr cell, and LO 2(g) says "(including the role of F plasmids **but not Hfr**)". `[OFFICIAL]` The chapter never uses the word Hfr, never draws the integrated state, and teaches only the F+ by F- mating, so it does not actually take Ethan out of scope. But the clause is the one place in Book 4 where the notes step across the line, and the sentence is worded so that a reader could take chromosomal gene transfer to be examinable. Read the F factor as the F plasmid and nothing else.

**5.2 The specialised transduction figure and its own text name two different genes.** Page 23 opens: "(For simplicity, we will use the example in Fig. 13 of the λ phage infecting E. coli, and the **bio** gene being transferred.)" Every step of the text then tracks the **bio** gene. The label inside step 2 of the accompanying diagram reads: "Excised phage DNA with the **gal** gene from donor bacterium's chromosome." The rest of the diagram's labels say bio. `[SCHOOL]` Both genes are real neighbours of the lambda attachment site in E. coli, so the underlying biology is fine, but the figure disagrees with its own caption on the same page.

**5.3 Page 23's cross-reference points at the wrong figure.** "we will use the example in **Fig. 13**" refers to the specialised transduction walkthrough. Fig. 13 in this chapter is "Bacterial cell division" on page 14. The walkthrough it means has no figure number at all, which is why the reference could not be written correctly.

**5.4 The chapter promises two sections that do not exist in this file.** Page 11: "Genes in one operon also come under the control of the same regulatory elements (**KIV: Sect 3.4**)" and "Prokaryotic genes also lack introns and thus do not require splicing after transcription, unlike eukaryotic gene expression (**KIV: Sect 3.3**)". Page 13: "The genetic material of bacterial cells is utilized in replication (Section 3.1), gene transfer (Section 3.2) and **gene expression (Section 3.3)**... **These processes will be dealt with in the remaining sections of the lecture notes.**" The lecture outline on page 2 stops at 3.2.3 followed by Concept Check 1 on page 27, and the PDF ends there. Sections 3.3 and 3.4 are absent. `[SCHOOL]` This turns out to be exactly right on scope, because 3.3 and 3.4 would be prokaryotic gene expression and operon regulation, and 9477 has no outcome for either. But the file promises content it does not deliver, and a reader will go looking for it.

**5.5 The cell size range in the body text does not match the table three pages later.** Page 4: "The sizes of bacterial cells vary from less than 1 µm to approximately **10 µm** in size." Page 9, Fig. 8 gives the prokaryotic size of cell as "Typically **0.2 – 2.0 µm** in diameter". Both are defensible statements about different things, since one is a length and the other a diameter, but the chapter does not say so, and a candidate quoting a range in an answer needs to know which one to use. `[SCHOOL]` LO 1(d) asks only that a bacterial cell is "small and unicellular", so no number is required.

**5.6 Binary fission is described both as something that transfers genetic material and as something that cannot generate variation, and the chapter never reconciles them.**

- Page 13, Fig. 12 caption: "**Gene transfer** from a parent cell to its offspring (daughter cells) occurs by binary fission while horizontal transfer to other cells of similar generation occurs via processes such as transformation, transduction and conjugation."
- Page 14: "Note that binary fission is unable to give rise to genetic variation in a bacterial population."
- Page 16, opening section 3.2: "**Transfer of genetic material can also occur between bacterial cells that do not share a parent-offspring relationship.**"
- Page 27, Concept Check 1 Q1: "What process in bacteria **always allows both chromosomal and non-chromosomal DNA to be transferred?** A. Binary fission B. Conjugation C. Transduction D. Transformation."

Both statements are true once you separate transfer from variation, but the Concept Check treats binary fission as a transfer process while section 3.2 defines transfer as something that happens between cells with no parent-offspring relationship. `[SCHOOL]` This is not just a wording wobble: **9477 Specimen Paper 1 Question 12 turns on exactly this ambiguity**, offering binary fission as option A against the three horizontal mechanisms, and asking which is "responsible for the enormous increase in the number of cells that were resistant". The examiner guide records both readings of that question and why they cannot be settled without a mark scheme.

**5.7 The chapter never names two mechanisms that the prelim mark schemes require.** The conjugation account on page 26 says only that "one strand of the F plasmid in the F+ donor cell is **nicked by an endonuclease**" and then that "Each parental strand becomes a template for the DNA synthesis of a complementary daughter strand ... by **semi-conservative replication**, catalysed by DNA polymerase". The corpus records at least eight prelim schemes that award marks for the **rolling circle mechanism** and several that award marks for the **origin of transfer (oriT)** and for the **tra genes**, none of which appears anywhere in this chapter. `[SCHOOL]` This is a gap in the notes rather than an error in them, but it is a mark-losing gap and it is recorded in the examiner guide's mark scheme section.

**5.8 The chapter says homologous recombination does not occur in conjugation, without saying that this holds only for the F+ by F- case.** Page 16: "Note that homologous recombination does not occur in conjugation since the F plasmid is not integrated into the recipient's chromosome (Sect 3.2.3)." That is correct for the F+ by F- mating the chapter teaches, and it is correct for everything LO 2(g) requires. It would not be correct if chromosomal genes were being transferred, which is the excluded Hfr case. `[INFERRED]` No action needed, but note that the statement is true because of the exclusion, not independently of it.

**5.9 Generalised transduction is taught entirely through a phage the chapter calls out of syllabus.** Page 20 opens: "During the reproduction of virulent phages (**e.g. T4 phage – not in syllabus**), a small fraction of the virions produced during the lytic cycle contain a random fragment of the bacterial genome instead of phage DNA." The comparison table on page 24 then prints "Virulent phage (e.g. T4 phage)" as the defining feature of generalised transduction. `[SCHOOL]` LO 2(g) requires transduction without qualification, so generalised transduction is examinable and it needs a virulent phage in its lytic cycle to explain it. The "not in syllabus" note is about T4's structure, not about the lytic cycle or about generalised transduction.

**5.10 Small typographical and grammatical errors.** Page 12: "Plasmids exist as small, circular, double-stranded extrachromosomal DNA **molecule** which may be passed on to cells of the same generation **(e.g. conjugation, transformation and transduction; or to its offspring (binary fission)**" with an unclosed parenthesis. Pages 20 to 24 mix British and American spellings of the same two words, printing "generalised" and "specialised" in the prose but "Generalized Transduction" and "Specialized Transduction" in the section headings and the outline.

- **The prokaryote-against-eukaryote genome comparison, pages 11 and 12**, on the axes LO 2(d) names. Circular against linear, single molecule against many, no introns against introns, histone-like proteins and supercoiling against histones and nucleosomes, plasmids present against absent, no nuclear envelope against nuclear envelope. **This was tested on the 9477 specimen at Paper 3 Question 1(a)(i) and (ii) for four marks**, one for a similarity and three for differences. `[OFFICIAL]`
- **Plasmids, page 12, all four properties.** Small, circular, double-stranded, extrachromosomal, non-essential, own origin of replication so replication is independent, and carrying beneficial genes that confer a selective advantage. Every one of those clauses does work in an answer.
- **Binary fission in six steps, page 15.** LO 2(g) says "outline the mechanism", which fixes the expected length. The mark-bearing details are attachment of the chromosome to the membrane, a **single** origin of replication, **bidirectional** replication, semi-conservative replication, elongation moving the chromosomes apart, the septum forming as the **membrane** invaginates, and two genetically identical daughter cells. The corpus records an explicit reject of "cell wall invaginates, cell invaginates" where the membrane is meant.
- **Homologous recombination, page 16**, as the step that makes transferred DNA heritable, plus the fact that the replaced recipient segment is **excised and degraded**, plus the requirement of close sequence homology. This node is shared by transformation and both kinds of transduction, so it is worth learning once and reusing three times.
- **Transformation, pages 17 and 18.** Naked DNA from a lysed donor, uptake by a **competent** cell through **competence factors**, then homologous recombination, then a recombinant cell. The corpus rejects "chromosomal DNA (this is too big)" as the material transferred, so say fragments of naked DNA.
- **Generalised transduction, pages 20 and 21.** Virulent phage, lytic cycle, host chromosome degraded, a random fragment mispackaged into a capsid during assembly, defective phage, injection into a second host, homologous recombination. The word **random** is the whole distinction.
- **Specialised transduction, pages 22 and 23.** Temperate phage, prophage, induction, **imprecise excision**, so only genes **adjacent to the prophage insertion site** travel, and then two possible endings in the recipient, prophage integration or homologous recombination. The words **imprecise** and **adjacent** carry the marks.
- **The generalised-against-specialised comparison table on page 24.** Three axes, both sides stated positively. The corpus records schemes rejecting the wrong pairing in both directions, so the pairing of virulent with generalised and temperate with specialised must be automatic.
- **Conjugation, pages 25 and 26.** F factor, F+ and F- cells, sex pilus for contact, **cytoplasmic mating bridge** for transfer, endonuclease nick in one strand, transfer of the single strand, complementary strand synthesised in **both** cells, both cells end F+. Two precision points from the corpus: the mating bridge is **not** the sex pilus and one scheme rejects sex pilus where the bridge is meant, and the cells are to be named **F+** and **F-**, with one scheme rejecting "donor" and "recipient" as the answer.
- **Why any of this matters, page 16.** Horizontal transfer generates genetic variation, which is the raw material for natural selection, which is how antibiotic resistance spreads. The corpus rejects "allows bacteria to have selective advantage" **without reference to gaining of advantageous genes**, so the chain must be stated in full.
- **The endosymbiont evidence on page 10.** No syllabus outcome names endosymbiosis, and yet **9477 Specimen Paper 3 Question 1(a)(iii) asked for two features of mitochondria supporting it, for two marks.** `[OFFICIAL]` The answer is built entirely from LO 1(d) and LO 2(d) content: circular DNA not associated with histones, 70S ribosomes, division by a splitting process, and their own transcription and translation machinery. Learn the five bullet points on page 10 as an application of the bacterial-cell outcome, not as a topic of its own.

**Read once and move on:**

- **The whole of the Gram section, pages 5 to 8.** Gram-positive against Gram-negative walls, teichoic acid, lipopolysaccharide, O-polysaccharide, lipid A, endotoxin against exotoxin, the periplasmic space, and the four-step Gram staining procedure with crystal violet, iodine, alcohol and safranin. **Four pages and four figures with no learning outcome behind any of it.** LO 1(d) asks only for a peptidoglycan cell wall. The word "Gram" returns zero real hits in the 9477 syllabus. Read it once because a stem could name a Gram-negative organism, and never drill it.
- **The NAG and NAM chemistry on page 5**, including the β(1,4) bond, the peptide cross-links and the D-amino acids. LO 1(d) wants the word peptidoglycan. The corpus records a scheme rejecting "NAM & NAG vs β-glucose" as a comparison point.
- **Morphology on page 3.** Cocci, bacilli, spirilla, diplococci, streptococci, staphylococci. No outcome. Vocabulary only.
- **The capsule on page 5**, which the school marks (FYI) itself.
- **Serial endosymbiosis as a narrative, page 10.** Keep the five lines of evidence, which are examinable through LO 1(d) and 2(d) as the specimen showed. The engulfment story and the mitochondria-before-plastids ordering are context.
- **Operons, page 11.** One sentence in this chapter, and it is the only mention. The word "operon" returns **zero** hits in the 9477 syllabus and the only gene-regulation outcome, LO 2(i), is restricted to "differential gene expression **in eukaryotes**". `[OFFICIAL]` This single sentence is the tip of a very large iceberg in the question bank, and the examiner guide measures it.
- **Artificial transformation on page 18**, meaning calcium chloride with heat shock and electroporation. That is molecular technique, and LO 2(j) names only PCR, gel electrophoresis and Southern blotting. Useful as stem context in a genetic engineering question, not as recall here.
- **The R plasmid, page 2.** Named once in the Learning Experiences block and never again. Know that plasmids can carry resistance genes, which page 12 says anyway.

## 7. Cross-chapter findings

**7.1 The two chapters print the same opening abstract and disagree on one clause of it.** Both chapters open with a near-identical paragraph on genome diversity. Chapter 4.1 page 1 ends it: negative-sense RNA "must be **converted to** positive-sense RNA by RNA polymerase before translation". Chapter 4.2 page 1 ends the same sentence: negative-sense RNA "must be **used as a template for the synthesis of** positive-sense RNA by RNA polymerase before translation". The 4.2 version is correct and the 4.1 version is the one Ethan will read first, because 4.1 is the first chapter. Chapter 4.2's paragraph is also longer, adding the endosymbiotic theory and the prokaryote-against-eukaryote framing that 4.1 omits. `[SCHOOL]`

**7.2 LO 2(d) is printed in both chapters and taught in neither of them completely.** Chapter 4.1 covers the viral half on pages 10 to 12. Chapter 4.2 covers the prokaryotic half on pages 11 and 12. **The eukaryotic half is in neither chapter.** LO 2(d) is a three-way comparison and Book 4 supplies two of the three sides. The eukaryotic side lives in Books 2 and 5. This matters directly, because the one place LO 2(d) surfaced on the specimen, Paper 3 Question 1(a)(i) and (ii), asked for a **prokaryote against eukaryote** comparison, which is precisely the pairing Book 4 does not teach in one place. `[OFFICIAL]` Build that comparison table yourself, or lose four marks that were sitting in the open.

**7.3 The two chapters describe the phage lifecycle from opposite ends, and the join is where transduction is understood.** Chapter 4.1 teaches the lytic and lysogenic cycles as the virus's reproductive strategy, and never mentions transduction except in one forward reference on page 17. Chapter 4.2 teaches transduction as the bacterium's route to variation, and depends entirely on 4.1's two cycles to explain it: generalised transduction is a lytic-cycle packaging accident, specialised transduction is a lysogenic-cycle excision accident. **Neither chapter states this pairing as a single sentence.** It is the highest-value cross-chapter link in the Book and it is worth writing out explicitly: virulent phage, lytic cycle, random gene, generalised; temperate phage, lysogenic cycle, adjacent gene, specialised.

**7.4 The two chapters give different accounts of what the phage capsid does at penetration, and the corpus polices it.** Chapter 4.1 says twice, on pages 19 and 22, that "The capsid is left on the outside of the bacterial cell wall" for both T4 and lambda. The corpus records a scheme rejecting the phrasing "phage capsid left outside the cell for lytic phage vs nucleocapsid enters the cell" as a comparison against an enveloped virus, on the ground that the difference must be stated as a difference in the same feature, not as a presence against an absence. `[SCHOOL]` The fact is right and the way it is deployed in a comparison is what gets marked.

**7.5 Neither chapter ever states the exit route in the vocabulary the schemes enforce.** Chapter 4.1 uses **budding** for influenza and HIV, correctly, on pages 29 and 35, and **lysis** for the lytic phage on page 20, correctly. The corpus records a genuine cross-school conflict on a third virus: one scheme "explicitly rejects 'exocytosis' for HIV and insists on budding", while the SARS-CoV-2 keys in four separate papers "all require exocytosis and mark budding wrong". `[SCHOOL]` The rule that reconciles them is that budding is for enveloped viruses that take the **cell surface membrane** on the way out, and exocytosis is for viruses that acquire their envelope internally and leave in a vesicle. Book 4's two named enveloped viruses both bud.

**7.6 Both chapters flag material forward to Infectious Diseases, and both stop at exactly the right place.** Chapter 4.1 page 37 defines antigen, antigenicity and antibody and then says "These terms will be further discussed in the topic of ETA: Infectious Diseases", and page 39 and page 43 both flag "(KIV: ETA- Infectious Diseases)". Chapter 4.2 page 16 flags "(KIV: JC2 Diversity & Evolution)". Those are correct boundaries. The scale of what sits on the far side of them is measured in the examiner guide: 161 of the 977 records in the virus question bank touch vaccines, antibodies, immunity or epidemiology, which is Book 9 material reached through a Book 4 stem.

**7.7 Neither chapter contains a single bioethics passage, and neither needs one.** Recorded for completeness, since Book 3 carried a substantial ethics section that had to be cut.

**7.8 Error count and where it lands.** Chapter 4.1 carries **12 findings**, of which four are genuine factual or internal-contradiction errors that would cost marks: the antigenic shift definition breaking down on page 40, lambda being given a base plate, the lysosome fusion in influenza penetration, and the wrong stated reason for the high RNA-virus mutation rate. Chapter 4.2 carries **10 findings**, of which one is a genuine factual conflict, the bio and gal genes in the specialised transduction figure, and one is a scope hazard rather than an error, the F factor definition including the integrated state. **Chapter 4.2 is the cleaner of the two by a clear margin,** which is the reverse of the usual pattern where the longer chapter is the more reliable one. The single most dangerous finding in the Book is 4.1's §5.1, because it teaches a definition of antigenic shift that the same chapter contradicts twice and that LO 2(f) exists to test.
