## A. The chloroplast is two compartments, each running a different half of the process

**A1. The envelope is a double membrane with two different permeabilities.** The chloroplast is
**lens-shaped** and bounded by a **double membrane**. The outer membrane is **selectively permeable to
some solutes**; the inner one is **highly permeable**, with substances passing **with the aid of
transporters**.

**A2. The stroma holds the Calvin cycle.** It is a **gel-like matrix enclosed by the chloroplast
envelope**, containing **circular DNA**, **70S ribosomes**, **starch granules**, oil droplets and
**enzymes involved in the Calvin cycle**. When a stem asks **precisely** where something happens, the
answer is **stroma** and never chloroplast. The 2026 specimen printed that word into the stem.

**A3. The thylakoid membrane holds the machinery and the thylakoid space holds the gradient.**
Thylakoids are a **third membrane system within the stroma consisting of flattened sacs**, with
**pigments and electron carriers embedded within the membrane**, and the enclosed space is the
**thylakoid lumen or thylakoid space**. The mark-bearing consequence is the chapter's own: this
**compartmentalisation allows chemiosmosis to take place and ATP to be produced by
photophosphorylation**. A gradient needs a sealed compartment on one side of a membrane, and this is
the only structure that supplies one.

**A4. Stacking is a surface-area argument, and the lamellae make the compartment continuous.** A
**granum** is a **stack of thylakoids**, and stacking **increases the surface area and the amount of
pigments available for the light-dependent reaction**. Connecting the grana are **intergranal
lamellae**, flattened tubular thylakoids that **connect the thylakoid compartments into a single,
continuous compartment**. One continuous compartment means one gradient serves the whole organelle.

**A5. Number is marked on these names.** A scheme rejects **grana** where **granum** was asked for, and
rejects the plural lamellae where the singular **lamella** was asked for. If the stem says name the
structure, answer in the singular. The school's key accepts **chloroplast membrane** for the envelope.

## B. Pigments, and the one comparison with an entire outcome to itself

**B1. A pigment is defined by the wavelengths it refuses.** Pigments **reflect only certain wavelengths
of visible light**, and more importantly they **absorb only certain wavelengths**, acting as
**energy-receiving antennas**. Because each pigment absorbs a **narrow range**, plants use several
kinds **to increase the range of wavelengths from which they can obtain energy**. Both classes,
**chlorophylls** and **carotenoids**, sit in the **thylakoid membrane**.

**B2. Only chlorophyll a acts, and everything else feeds it.** Chlorophyll a is the **major pigment in
photoautotrophs**, it **absorbs blue and red light**, and **only chlorophyll a can participate directly
in the light-dependent reaction**. Any pigment that **absorbs light energy and transfers it to
chlorophyll a of the reaction centre is an accessory pigment**, of which **chlorophyll b is the most
common**. Chlorophyll **reflects green light**, which is why leaves look green, and its **porphyrin
ring** carries a **magnesium** atom, so **magnesium deficiency reduces chlorophyll production and
causes chlorosis**.

**B3. Carotenoids do two separate jobs, and the second is the forgotten one.** They **absorb strongly
in the blue-violet range**, roughly **460 to 550 nm**. First, they **broaden the spectrum of light for
photosynthesis** by **absorbing the intermediate wavelengths which chlorophyll cannot**, although they
**transfer only about 10% of their absorbed energy**. Second, **photoprotection**: they **absorb
excessive light and prevent auto-oxidation of chlorophyll**, hence **preventing photobleaching**.

**B4. The two spectra are different measurements, and the marks come from setting them side by side.**
An absorption spectrum plots the **amount of light absorbed at different wavelengths by a pigment**. An
action spectrum plots the **effectiveness of different wavelengths in driving photosynthesis**. The
inference the outcome wants runs: the **wavelengths absorbed by chlorophyll, namely red and blue light,
are very similar to the wavelengths that drive photosynthesis**, hence **chlorophyll is mainly
responsible for the absorption of light in photosynthesis**. Where the action spectrum sits above the
chlorophyll curve, in the middle of the visible range, the difference is the **accessory pigments**
feeding energy in.

## C. The light-dependent stage converts light energy into ATP and reduced NADP

**C1. Three stages, and each has an address.** Light harvesting and the light-dependent reaction both
**require light and occur in the thylakoid membranes**; the light-independent reaction needs **no light
and takes place in the stroma**. Every long answer hangs on that skeleton,
so state the location as you go.

**C2. An excited chlorophyll has three routes back down, and photosynthesis uses two.** Absorbing light
moves the molecule **from its ground state to an excited state**, which is **unstable**. It can return
by **transferring the energy, but not the electron, to a neighbouring chlorophyll molecule by resonance
energy transfer**, which happens **during light harvesting**. It can return by **boosting an electron
to a higher energy level and transferring it to an electron acceptor**, which happens **in the
light-dependent reaction**, with the deficit refilled from an **electron donor**. Or the energy is
**lost as heat, or as heat and light of a longer wavelength, fluorescence**.

**C3. A photosystem is a light-harvesting system plus a reaction centre.** The **light-harvesting
complexes** carry **200 to 300 pigment molecules** and **transfer the light energy to the reaction
centre**. The reaction centre **contains a pair of special chlorophyll a molecules which act as an
irreversible trap for energy**, passing an excited electron **immediately to an adjacent chain of
electron acceptors**. The **primary electron acceptor** sits inside that reaction centre. The two
photosystems are **PSII**, whose special chlorophyll a is **P680** because it **absorbs maximally at
680 nm**, and **PSI**, whose special chlorophyll a is **P700** at **700 nm**.

**C4. The chain releases the energy in steps rather than all at once.** Carriers sit **in series in the
thylakoid membrane between the photosystems**, and electrons pass down them **by a series of redox
reactions**, each carrier **reduced as it receives an electron and oxidised as it donates one**. They
are **arranged in increasing electron affinity so that transport down the chain is unidirectional**. An
electron **progressively loses energy as it is transferred from one carrier to another**, and **some of
the energy released is used to make ATP**.

**C5. Non-cyclic photophosphorylation, run forwards as one chain.** It **involves both Photosystems II
and I in the Z-scheme of electron flow** and produces **ATP and NADPH**.

Light strikes a pigment in a light-harvesting complex → energy is relayed by **resonance energy
transfer** to **P680** → an electron in P680 is **excited to a higher energy state** → the
**photoexcited electron is captured by the primary electron acceptor**, leaving P680 short of an
electron → **photolysis of water replenishes that deficit** → the electron passes down the **first
electron transport chain** toward PSI, **losing free energy at each step** → that free energy **pumps
protons from the stroma into the thylakoid space** → light meanwhile excites an electron in **P700**,
which is **captured by PSI's primary electron acceptor** and **replaced by the electron arriving from
PSII** → the electron passes down a **second chain through ferredoxin** → **NADP reductase transfers
electrons to NADP**, two being required to **reduce it to NADPH**.

Two naming points sit on that chain. ATP synthesis here is **called photophosphorylation because it
uses light energy to phosphorylate ADP**. And because schemes disagree over photoactivation against
photoexcitation, name both correctly: the **chlorophyll a is photoactivated** and the **electron is
photoexcited**.

**C6. Photolysis has three roles and candidates give one.** An **enzyme splits a water molecule into
two electrons, two hydrogen ions and an oxygen atom**, a process that **involves light**, hence
**photolysis of water**, written **H2O → 2e- + 2H+ + ½O2**. The electrons **replenish the deficit of
electrons from the reaction centre of PSII**; the oxygen atom **combines with another and is released
as a by-product**; and the hydrogen ions **add to the proton concentration in the thylakoid space**.
The third is the one that gets left out. Two rejects live here. **Photolysis is never called
hydrolysis**, because water is split by **light** and not by water. And **oxygen is a by-product of
photolysis in photosynthesis, never the final electron acceptor**, which is an imported respiration
answer and one of the two commonest confusions an examiner has named in this topic.

**C7. Cyclic photophosphorylation is defined by what it lacks.** It **involves Photosystem I only** and
**no NADPH is produced**. Light is passed to **P700** → P700 **emits an energised electron to the
primary electron acceptor** → the electron **reaches ferredoxin, is cycled back to the cytochrome
complex on the first chain, and returns to PSI** → **enough energy is released along that chain to
synthesise ATP**. The
comparison below is the school's own answer key, in its accepted wording.

| Feature | Non-cyclic | Cyclic |
|---|---|---|
| Products | **NADPH, ATP**, and **oxygen as a by-product** | **ATP only** |
| Pathway of electrons | **non-cyclic / linear flow** | **cyclic flow** |
| Photosystems involved | **PSII and PSI** | **PSI only** |
| First electron donor | **water** | **PSI (P700)** |
| Last electron acceptor | **NADP+** | **PSI (P700)** |
| Source of high [H+] in the thylakoid space | **photolysis of water** and **active transport of H+ ions into thylakoid space from stroma** | **active transport of H+ ions into thylakoid space from stroma** |

The last row is the only genuine difference in how the gradient is built, so it is what a comparison
question is really testing. Note the accepted phrase for the pumping step, **active transport of H+
ions into thylakoid space from stroma**, which is neither diffusion nor pumping on its own.

**C8. Chemiosmosis in the chloroplast, in four moves.** Chemiosmosis is the process in which **energy
stored in the form of a hydrogen ion gradient across a membrane is used to drive cellular work such as
the synthesis of ATP**. First, the **thylakoid membrane is impermeable to H+**, so protons accumulate
in the thylakoid space. Second, as electrons **flow down energy levels from PSII to PSI they release
free energy**, and using it the **cytochrome complex pumps hydrogen ions against the concentration
gradient from the stroma into the thylakoid space**. Third, **photolysis also contributes H+**, so the
gradient has two sources, and the result is an **electrochemical and concentration gradient known as a
proton gradient**, with **more hydrogen ions inside the thylakoid space than in the stroma**. Fourth, hydrogen ions
**diffuse down this gradient back into the stroma through the ATP synthase complex**, and this **drives
the formation of ATP**. Never attach a proton-to-ATP ratio to that fourth move.

## D. The Calvin cycle builds sugar from carbon dioxide using the light stage's products

**D1. Location, purpose, and the outcome's own three phases.** It **occurs in the stroma**, **none of
its steps requires light directly**, and its purpose is to **reduce carbon dioxide using ATP as the
energy source and NADPH as reducing power**. The phases, in the order the outcome names them, are
**carbon dioxide fixation**, **reduction of PGA**, and **regeneration of RuBP**.

**D2. Fixation.** Carbon dioxide is fixed when it **combines with a five-carbon carbon dioxide
acceptor, ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP), to form an unstable six-carbon intermediate**, catalysed by
**ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase oxygenase, rubisco**. That intermediate **breaks down spontaneously
into two molecules of the three-carbon compound phosphoglyceric acid (PGA)**, also written
**glycerate-3-phosphate (GP)**.

**D3. Reduction.** Each PGA is **phosphorylated by ATP** and then **reduced by a pair of electrons
donated from NADPH to form triose phosphate (TP)**, also called glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. Split the
two inputs cleanly, because they are separately creditable: the **hydrogen for the reduction comes from
the NADPH while the energy comes from ATP**. The phosphorylated intermediate between the two steps is
excluded by the outcome, so run PGA straight to triose phosphate, as the school's own key does.

**D4. Regeneration, and the arithmetic that survives the exclusion.** For **every three molecules of
carbon dioxide entering the cycle, three RuBP are carboxylated and six TP are formed**. Only **one TP
counts as a net gain of carbohydrate**; the **other five regenerate the three RuBP**, and **three more
ATP are invested** to do it. Then **two TP make one hexose**, so **one hexose requires six turns**. The
school's key marks **5 out of 6, or 83%**, as a mark point, so this ratio is examined directly. Where
the atoms end up is also asked: the **carbon and oxygen atoms of hexose come from carbon dioxide while
the hydrogen atoms come from NADPH**.

**D5. The three roles the outcome names, kept separate.** **Rubisco catalyses the carboxylation of
RuBP.** **ATP supplies the energy** for phosphorylation and for regeneration. **Reduced NADP supplies
the hydrogen** that reduces PGA. A question can ask for any one alone, so hold them as three sentences
rather than one blur.

**D6. Rubisco accepts oxygen too, and the competition is an enzyme argument.** **Oxygen competes with
carbon dioxide for the active site in rubisco**, so **less carbon dioxide is fixed**, **previously
fixed carbon in RuBP is lost as carbon dioxide**, **less PGA is synthesised** and **photosynthetic
output is decreased**. The word photorespiration has no outcome and C4 and CAM plants go with it, but competition
for one active site is competitive inhibition from Core Idea 1, and that is examinable.

## E. Limiting factors, and the two graph shapes questions are built on

**E1. The principle, in the chapter's own words.** The **rate of a biochemical process, which consists
of a series of reactions, is limited by the slowest reaction in the series**, and when several factors
act, the rate is **limited by that factor which is nearest its minimum value**. **That limiting factor
directly affects the process if its quantity is changed**, which is the practical test: change it, and
if the rate moves it was limiting.

**E2. Reading the standard light-intensity curve.** In the rising region **light intensity is the
limiting factor**, and **any increase in carbon dioxide concentration or temperature will not change
the rate**. On the plateau, **light intensity is no longer limiting and some other factor is**. The
**light saturation point** is the **point beyond which an increase in light intensity causes no further
increase in the rate**. **A plateau means the plotted variable has stopped limiting**, which is the most examined inference
here, and the trap is naming the axis variable because it is the one on the axis.

**E3. Light intensity itself.** **Light saturation is reached at about 10 000 lux** while full sun is about **100 000 lux**, so
**except in shaded plants light is not normally a major limiting factor**. At the other extreme, **very
high light intensities damage the chlorophyll**, **photobleaching**, and lower the rate.

**E4. Carbon dioxide is the usual real limiter.** **Under normal conditions carbon dioxide is the major
limiting factor**, at **0.03% to 0.04% of the atmosphere**, and raising it raises the rate until it
saturates. On a multi-curve figure each curve is one carbon dioxide concentration: below the saturation point of a
curve **light intensity is limiting**, and beyond it **carbon dioxide concentration is**, which is why
the curves differ mainly in plateau height.

**E5. Temperature behaves differently, and that difference is the question.** The **light-independent
reactions are enzyme-controlled and thus temperature-sensitive**. Rising towards the optimum, the
**rate doubles for every 10 °C rise up to about 35 °C** because the **molecules move more rapidly and
have a greater chance of colliding**, with an **optimum of about 25 °C for temperate plants**. Past it the
**rate decreases as enzymes start to denature**. So the temperature curve **rises to a peak and falls
again** while the light and carbon dioxide curves **rise and plateau**. Light and carbon dioxide are
substrates and a substrate has no harmful excess; temperature acts on the enzyme itself.

**E6. The switch-off question, the highest-frequency shape in the Book.** Reason from what is being
consumed and what is being made, never from an assumption that everything moves together.

- **Remove carbon dioxide:** fixation stops, so **RuBP rises because it is no longer being consumed**
  and **PGA falls because it is no longer being made**.
- **Remove light:** ATP and reduced NADP stop arriving, so **PGA rises because it is still made but no
  longer reduced**, and **RuBP falls because regeneration needs ATP**.
- **Restore either:** the movements reverse.

One reject to hold: **RuBP not available** and **RuBP is saturated** are rejected as limiting factors.
The accepted answers are **temperature**, **light intensity**, **RuBP regeneration**, or **saturation
of rubisco active sites**.

## G. Errors in your notes, and what this chapter teaches that is not examined

**Five printed errors. Learn the corrected version.**

1. **The colours of chlorophyll a and b are transposed in Table 2, page 13.** **Chlorophyll a is
   blue-green and chlorophyll b is yellow-green**, not the other way round.
2. **Oxidative phosphorylation is defined backwards on page 6**, as releasing energy trapped in ATP.
   That is ATP hydrolysis. Oxidative phosphorylation **traps released energy in ATP**.
3. **Page 21 says FAD takes a pair of electrons and a proton.** True of NAD and NADP. **FAD takes two
   electrons and two protons**, which is what the 2 in FADH2 means.
4. **Page 26 gives one ATP synthesised for every two H+.** Chapter 5.2 implies 3.33 and 3.0 for the
   same enzyme. **Three values, one Book, and all of them excluded.** Never quote a ratio.
5. **A photosystem is listed as three components and the third sits inside the second.** Learn it as
   **light-harvesting complexes plus a reaction centre, with the primary electron acceptor inside the
   reaction centre**.

**One imprecision worth fixing.** Page 20 calls **P680 and P700 the two photosystems**; they are the
**reaction-centre chlorophyll a molecules inside PSII and PSI**, which the same page states correctly
two bullets earlier.

**Cut, and the syllabus says so in writing.** The **named electron carriers**, the **detailed mechanism
of ATP synthase**, the **structures of the Calvin cycle intermediates**, and **every enzyme except
rubisco**. On zero hits, also cut **leaf anatomy and plant tissue types**, the **structure of ATP**
while keeping its roles, the **electromagnetic spectrum**, **Calvin's lollipop experiment**, and
**photorespiration, C4 and CAM as named topics**.

**Absent as words, examined anyway. Do not cut these.** **Photolysis**, **photosystem**, **PSI**,
**PSII**, **P680**, **P700**, **reaction centre**, **photophosphorylation**, **triose phosphate**,
**proton gradient** and **compensation point** return zero syllabus hits and every one is carried by a
broader clause in an outcome. Triose phosphate is the clearest case: the outcome demands the PGA
reduction phase, and a phase has to have a product. The **carbon counts and ratios** survive too,
because a carbon count is arithmetic and the exclusion names structure.
