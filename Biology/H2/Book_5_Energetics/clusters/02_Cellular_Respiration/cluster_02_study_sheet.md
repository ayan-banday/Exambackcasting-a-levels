## A. The mitochondrion is two membranes making two compartments

**A1. The outer membrane is the permeable one.** It is **freely permeable to ATP, ADP and similar
molecules**, so nothing in respiration depends on it holding anything back.

**A2. The inner membrane does three jobs at once.** It is **selectively permeable** and **highly folded
to form cristae which increase surface area for embedding electron transport chain and ATP synthase
complexes**. It is **not permeable to NADH**, it **contains proteins for transporting H+, ATP and
ADP**, and it is the **site of oxidative phosphorylation**. The property that makes chemiosmosis
possible is stated later in the chapter rather than in the table: the **inner mitochondrial membrane is
impermeable to H+**, so **protons can only re-enter the matrix via the ATP synthase complex**. Without
that impermeability there is no gradient and no ATP.

**A3. The matrix is where the two decarboxylating stages run.** It is the **compartment enclosed by the
inner membrane**, the **site of the link reaction and Krebs cycle**, and it **contains the enzymes
required for those reactions**. It is also where **oxygen is reduced to water** at the end of the
chain.

**A4. The intermembrane space is a reservoir, not a workshop.** No reaction happens there. It is
simply the compartment protons are **pumped into**, and its **high proton concentration** is the store
of energy that ATP synthase spends.

**A5. Locations are asked directly and they are cheap marks.** One specimen Paper 1 item was nothing
but where the link reaction and oxidative phosphorylation happen, and a six-mark Paper 3 part asked for
two respiratory processes that depend on proteins in the matrix, which is the **link reaction and the
Krebs cycle**.

## B. The vocabulary the outcomes force you to write in

**B1. Redox, three ways.** **Oxidation is loss of electrons, loss of hydrogen, or gain of oxygen**, and
**reduction is the reverse of each**. Because **an electron transfer requires both a donor and an
acceptor, oxidation and reduction always go hand in hand**. Every stage of respiration is a redox
event, so say which species is oxidised and which is reduced.

**B2. Three named reaction types, and LO 3(g) hands you two of them.** **Decarboxylation is removal of
carbon atoms from a compound to form carbon dioxide.** **Dehydrogenation is the removal of electrons
as well as hydrogen ions from organic molecules**, catalysed by **dehydrogenases**. **Oxidative
decarboxylation is an oxidation reaction whereby a carboxylate group is removed, forming carbon
dioxide**, and it is the pair of the two happening together.

**B3. Coenzymes are transient carriers.** **Coenzymes are loosely associated with the enzyme during the
reaction and act as transient carriers of electrons, hydrogen or specific functional groups.** The two
here are **NAD**, reduced to **NADH or reduced NAD**, and **FAD**, reduced to **FADH2 or reduced FAD**.
Their job is to **serve as reservoirs of electrons and protons** that later feed oxidative
phosphorylation. A specimen structured question asked for the role of NAD alone, for three marks, so
be able to say it without the surrounding pathway: it is a **coenzyme and a hydrogen and electron
carrier**; it is **reduced during glycolysis, the link reaction and the Krebs cycle**; and it is
**reoxidised at the inner membrane** by delivering electrons to the chain.

**B4. Three rejected phrasings, all verified.** A scheme rejects a substrate being **broken down** or
**converted** where it should be **decarboxylated** or **oxidised**. Schemes reject **produce energy**
and **synthesise energy** outright: **ATP is synthesised; energy is released, transferred or trapped**.
And a scheme rejects any ATP accounting given **without consideration of stoichiometry**, so every
number carries its denominator, **per glucose** or **per acetyl CoA**.

## C. Glycolysis, at exactly the depth the outcome asks for

**C1. The outcome names three things and only three.** **Location, raw materials used, products
formed.** The **ten named intermediates, the enzymes and the isomerisation are excluded in writing**,
which deletes four pages of your chapter.

**C2. Location.** It **occurs in the cytosol or cytoplasm in all cells**, plants, animals and
prokaryotes, and it needs **no oxygen and no organelle**. That is why it is the one stage every route
shares.

**C3. Raw materials and products, per glucose.** In go **glucose, ATP, NAD, ADP and inorganic
phosphate**. Out come **two pyruvate**, a **net gain of 2 ATP** and **2 reduced NAD**. The net is worth
stating as a net: **4 ATP are made by substrate-level phosphorylation while 2 are invested in the
energy-investment phase**, so the profit is two.

**C4. Two mechanisms carry all of it.** **Substrate-level phosphorylation** makes the ATP, and it is
**the enzymatic endergonic phosphorylation of ADP coupled to exergonic dephosphorylation of an organic
substrate**. **Dehydrogenation** makes the reduced NAD, by **oxidising the substrate and transferring
hydrogen to NAD**. Name both mechanisms rather than describing the steps and you have answered the
outcome.

**C5. Why glycolysis matters beyond its yield.** It **can proceed anaerobically**, so it keeps a cell
alive with no oxygen, and it **feeds pyruvate to the mitochondrion** when oxygen is present.

## D. The link reaction, in the two words the outcome gives you

**D1. Pyruvate crosses by active transport.** **Pyruvate is a charged molecule, so it must enter the
mitochondrion via active transport, with the help of a transport protein.** Your notes contradict
themselves here and the table is the wrong one, so see section J.

**D2. One multienzyme complex catalyses three reactions by oxidative decarboxylation.** First, the
**carboxyl group is removed and liberated as CO2**, a **decarboxylation**. Second, the **remaining
two-carbon fragment is oxidised, forming acetate**, with **two electrons and a proton transferred to
NAD, reducing it**, a **dehydrogenation**. Third, **coenzyme A is attached to acetate, forming acetyl
CoA**.

**D3. Location, stoichiometry and equation.** It **occurs in the mitochondrial matrix** and **twice for
each glucose molecule**, giving **2 pyruvate + 2 NAD + 2 CoA → 2 acetyl CoA + 2 NADH + 2 CO2**. Carbon
balances: **six carbons enter as two pyruvate, four leave as two acetyl groups and two leave as carbon
dioxide**.

**D4. Acetyl CoA is not a two-carbon molecule.** The chapter calls it one throughout. The **acetyl
group** is two carbons; coenzyme A is much larger. Harmless in prose, wrong the moment a question makes
you count carbons through this reaction, which is exactly what LO 3(g) invites.

## E. The Krebs cycle, at outcome depth and no deeper

**E1. What enters, what it joins, and where.** In the **mitochondrial matrix**, the **two-carbon acetyl
group of acetyl CoA combines with the four-carbon oxaloacetate to form the six-carbon citrate**. Per
turn, **two carbon atoms enter in organic form as acetate and two leave in inorganic form as CO2**.

**E2. Three crucial reaction types, which is how the outcome wants it organised.** **Oxidative
decarboxylation**, **substrate-level phosphorylation**, and **production of reduced coenzymes by
dehydrogenation**. Organise a Krebs answer by these three rather than by walking the eight steps, and
you have written the outcome instead of the diagram.

**E3. The count per turn, then doubled.** Per turn: **two decarboxylations releasing 2 CO2**, **three
NAD reduced**, **one FAD reduced**, **one ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation**. Per glucose, double
every one of those, because **the cycle must occur twice to metabolise both acetyl CoA molecules
derived from a single glucose**.

**E4. Why it is called a cycle.** **The molecule that is added to incoming acetyl CoA, oxaloacetate, is
regenerated at the end of one turn to receive more acetyl groups.** Oxaloacetate is the one Krebs
intermediate worth keeping, and only for this sentence. **Citrate, isocitrate, succinate and the rest
are excluded**, since the outcome asks for raw materials and products rather than intermediates.

**E5. Other respiratory substrates feed in.** **Breakdown products of lipids, amino acids and
carbohydrates enter the cycle at various steps**, and **fatty acids enter as acetyl CoA**. Keep that
fact, because Paper 4 respiratory quotient questions are built on lipid and protein substrates.

**E6. The one numerical table in this chapter that is fully correct and fully in scope.** It is
products formed, not ATP yield, so nothing excludes it.

| Stage | Location | Raw materials | Products, per glucose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Glycolysis** | **cytoplasm** | **glucose, ATP, NAD, ADP, Pi** | **2 pyruvate**, **net 2 ATP**, **2 reduced NAD** |
| **Link reaction** | **matrix** | **pyruvate, coenzyme A, NAD** | **2 acetyl CoA**, **2 CO2**, **2 reduced NAD** |
| **Krebs cycle** | **matrix** | **acetyl CoA, NAD, FAD, ADP, Pi** | **4 CO2**, **2 ATP**, **6 reduced NAD**, **2 reduced FAD** |
| **Oxidative phosphorylation** | **inner membrane** | **reduced NAD, reduced FAD, O2, ADP, Pi** | **ATP**, **H2O**, **regenerated NAD and FAD** |
| **Total to the end of Krebs** | | | **6 CO2**, **4 ATP**, **10 reduced NAD**, **2 reduced FAD** |

**Six carbon dioxide, not four.** Two from the link reaction plus four from the Krebs cycle. Your
chapter's Overview page prints four and it is wrong.

## F. Oxidative phosphorylation is the chain plus chemiosmosis

**F1. The chain, at outcome depth.** The **ETC is a sequence of electron carriers** which **undergo
temporary reduction and oxidation as electrons from reduced NAD and reduced FAD are passed down to the
final electron acceptor, molecular oxygen**. **Each subsequent member has a higher affinity for
electrons than its predecessor**, which **ensures one-way transport down the chain**. **No ATP is
directly generated** by the chain. Instead the **energy released is used to pump protons across the
inner mitochondrial membrane into the intermembrane space**.

**F2. The role of oxygen, and the consequence of removing it.** Oxygen is the **final electron acceptor
and is reduced in the matrix to water**. Take it away and the chain backs up: electrons have nowhere to
go, so the **carriers stay reduced** → **reduced NAD and reduced FAD cannot be reoxidised** → the
**link reaction and Krebs cycle stop** for want of oxidised coenzyme → **no proton gradient forms** →
**no ATP is made by oxidative phosphorylation**. That chain was worth three marks on the specimen and it is the
hinge between this section and anaerobic respiration.

**F3. Chemiosmosis in the mitochondrion, the same four moves as the chloroplast.** Chemiosmosis is
**energy stored in the form of a hydrogen ion gradient across a membrane used to drive the synthesis of
ATP**. First, **as electrons are transferred along the ETC, energy is released**. Second, that energy
**drives proton pumps to actively pump H+ unidirectionally from the matrix to the intermembrane
space**. Third, the gradient has **two components**: a **concentration gradient of H+**, the chemical or pH
gradient, and an **electrical gradient**, a voltage across the membrane from the higher concentration
of positively charged protons on one side. The **tendency of H+ to diffuse back into the matrix is the
proton motive force**. Fourth, because the **membrane is impermeable to H+, protons re-enter only
through the ATP synthase complex**, which **couples the exergonic passage of H+ to the endergonic
phosphorylation of ADP to form ATP**. Stop there. The rotor mechanism is excluded twice.

**F4. Two ways to make ATP, and the marks are in the contrast.**

| | **Substrate-level phosphorylation** | **Oxidative phosphorylation** |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | **phosphorylation of ADP coupled to exergonic dephosphorylation of an organic substrate** | **phosphorylation of ADP by ATP synthase, coupled to exergonic electron transport to the final electron acceptor, oxygen** |
| Driven by | the **substrate itself** | the **exergonic passage of protons along a proton gradient** |
| Where | **glycolysis in the cytoplasm** and the **Krebs cycle in the matrix** | the **inner mitochondrial membrane** |
| Share of respiratory ATP | **only a small amount** | **almost 90%** |

**Almost 90% is the safe way to say it.** It is the chapter's own phrase, it makes the point, and it
avoids quoting a total that the syllabus excludes.

**F5. Three classes of respiratory poison, three different failures.** Learn the three consequences and
forget the compound names. **Block electron flow and no protons are pumped, so no gradient forms and
ATP production stops.** **Inhibit ATP synthase and the gradient grows larger than normal, but its
potential energy cannot be tapped to make ATP.** **Uncouple the membrane**, meaning make it leaky to protons,
**and the gradient dissipates**, so **no ATP is made** and the **energy from electron transport is
released as heat**.

## G. Anaerobic respiration exists to regenerate NAD

**G1. Why the yield collapses.** Without oxygen, **no further oxidation of pyruvate occurs, no acetyl
CoA is formed and no additional ATP can be generated**, so the cell is left with the **modest yield of
2 ATP per glucose from glycolysis**, all by **substrate-level phosphorylation**. So **cells must consume
glucose more rapidly to maintain steady-state ATP levels**.

**G2. The definition carries the argument.** **Fermentation is an oxygen-independent process, occurring
in the cytosol, that uses organic molecules as terminal electron acceptors for the regeneration of
NAD.** Read that sentence twice: the electron acceptor is now an organic molecule instead of oxygen,
and the purpose named is regeneration of the coenzyme, not the making of a product.

**G3. The chain LO 3(j) is asking for.** **Glycolysis requires oxidised NAD** → without oxygen the
**electron transport chain cannot reoxidise reduced NAD** → **pyruvate is used as the electron acceptor
instead** → **reduced NAD is oxidised back to NAD** → **glycolysis can continue** → a **small yield of
2 ATP per glucose** is maintained. The ethanol and the lactate are waste; the regenerated NAD is the
point.

**G4. Two fermentations, and only one is reversible.**

| | **Lactic acid fermentation** | **Alcoholic fermentation** |
|---|---|---|
| Where | **mammalian muscle**, and certain fungi and bacteria | **yeast** |
| Steps | **pyruvate is reduced directly by NADH to form lactate**, with **no release of CO2** | **CO2 is released from pyruvate, which is converted to acetaldehyde**, then **acetaldehyde is reduced by NADH to ethanol** |
| Products | **lactate** and **NAD** | **ethanol**, **CO2** and **NAD** |
| Afterwards | **lactate can be reconverted to pyruvate** when oxygen returns | **ethanol cannot be reconverted to pyruvate, because a carbon has been lost as CO2** |

**Write lactate, not lactic acid.** **Lactate is the ionised form of lactic acid** and it is the word
the syllabus uses. Write the recovery contrast as a **carbon-loss argument**, since that is what earns
it.

**G5. Comparing the yields without quoting a total.** Aerobic respiration captures **far more** ATP per
glucose, because **complete oxidation to CO2 and water** feeds reduced coenzymes into oxidative
phosphorylation while **incomplete oxidation** does not. Your notes give a nineteen-fold figure
calculated from an excluded total, so give the reason rather than the ratio.
