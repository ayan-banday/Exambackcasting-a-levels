## A. The two shared outcomes, which is why they are taught here rather than twice

**A1. LO 3(a) is one outcome across two organelles, and it is an identification skill.** The verb is
**identify** and the media are **drawings, photomicrographs and electronmicrographs**, so the skill is
naming a structure in an image, in the right number. Hold the two organelles as one paired table.

| | **Chloroplast** | **Mitochondrion** |
|---|---|---|
| Boundary | **double membrane envelope**, outer **selectively permeable**, inner **highly permeable** | **outer membrane freely permeable**, **inner membrane selectively permeable and impermeable to H+** |
| Surface-area device | **grana**, **stacks of thylakoids** | **cristae**, **folds of the inner membrane** |
| What that surface carries | **pigments and electron carriers**, plus **ATP synthase** | **electron transport chain and ATP synthase complexes** |
| Enclosed inner compartment | **thylakoid lumen or thylakoid space** | **intermembrane space** |
| Bulk internal matrix | **stroma**, holding **Calvin cycle enzymes**, **circular DNA**, **70S ribosomes**, **starch granules** | **matrix**, holding **link reaction and Krebs cycle enzymes** |
| Where protons build up | **thylakoid space** | **intermembrane space** |
| Where protons are pumped from | **stroma** | **matrix** |

**A2. LO 3(l) is one process in two places, so learn one answer and change the nouns.** The four moves
are identical: a **membrane impermeable to protons**, **electron flow releasing free energy that pumps
protons across it**, an **electrochemical gradient** forming, and **protons returning through ATP
synthase to drive ATP synthesis**. Everything else is vocabulary.

| | **Chloroplast** | **Mitochondrion** |
|---|---|---|
| Membrane the gradient sits across | **thylakoid membrane** | **inner mitochondrial membrane** |
| Energy source for pumping | **electron flow from photoexcitation** | **electron flow from reduced NAD and reduced FAD** |
| Second proton source | **photolysis of water** | **none** |
| Protons return into | **stroma** | **matrix** |
| Final electron acceptor | **NADP**, or back to **PSI** in cyclic flow | **oxygen**, reduced to **water** |
| Name for the ATP synthesis | **photophosphorylation** | **oxidative phosphorylation** |

**A3. Two asymmetries are the whole comparison.** **Only the chloroplast has a second proton source**,
because **photolysis releases H+ directly into the thylakoid space**. And **only the mitochondrion has
oxygen as the final electron acceptor**; in photosynthesis **oxygen is a by-product of photolysis**.

## B. Photosynthesis against respiration, as one comparison

| | **Photosynthesis** | **Aerobic respiration** |
|---|---|---|
| Energy conversion | **light energy to chemical energy** | **chemical energy in glucose to chemical energy in ATP** |
| Raw materials | **carbon dioxide and water** | **glucose and oxygen** |
| Products | **glucose (and oxygen as a by-product)** | **carbon dioxide and water** |
| Overall reaction type | **reduction of carbon dioxide**, anabolic | **complete oxidation of glucose**, catabolic |
| Organelle | **chloroplast** | **mitochondrion** |
| When it runs | **only in the light** | **continuously, light or dark** |
| Which organisms | **photoautotrophs only** | **effectively all cells** |
| Role of the coenzyme | **NADP reduced in the light stage, oxidised in the Calvin cycle** | **NAD and FAD reduced in the matrix, oxidised at the inner membrane** |

**B1. The two run at once in a plant, and every gas-exchange question turns on it.** **Plants respire
while they photosynthesise**, so any measurement of gas exchange in green tissue reports the **net** of
the two. **In darkness only respiration runs**, giving a **net release of carbon
dioxide and net uptake of oxygen**. As light intensity rises, photosynthesis climbs to meet
respiration, and past that point there is a **net uptake of carbon dioxide and net release of oxygen**.
The crossing point is the compensation point in section E.

## C. Measuring the rate of photosynthesis

**C1. Rate means an amount per unit time, and you choose which amount.** The three routes are **oxygen
produced**, **carbon dioxide taken up**, and **an artificial electron acceptor being reduced**. Oxygen
is measured as **gas volume per unit time**, by **displacement along a capillary tube** or with a
**dissolved oxygen probe**; carbon dioxide by **gas sensor** or by **hydrogencarbonate indicator
colour**.

**C2. Counting bubbles is the weak version and the exam knows it.** **Bubbles vary in size**, so a
count is not proportional to the volume of gas released, while the **distance a meniscus moves along a
capillary of known bore per unit time gives a volume**. That is why the specimen used a syringe and
capillary. Prefer the measurement that yields a **quantity with a unit** over one that yields a tally.

**C3. Sodium hydrogencarbonate is there to remove a confounding limiting factor.** It is a **source of
dissolved carbon dioxide**, so **carbon dioxide is kept in excess and cannot become the limiting
factor**. That means **any change in rate can be attributed to the independent variable** rather than
to carbon dioxide running out, which **increases the validity of the conclusion**. The specimen asked
for exactly that reasoning, for two marks.

**C4. The controlled variables all come from the limiting-factor argument.** **Temperature** must be
held constant, because it acts on Calvin cycle enzymes and a lamp heats the water, so use a **heat
shield or water bath between lamp and plant**. **Carbon dioxide** is held constant by the
hydrogencarbonate solution. The **same piece of pondweed**, of the **same length and species**, runs
throughout, because tissue varies. A short **equilibration period** after each change lets the rate
settle before a reading.

**C5. Redox dyes measure the light-dependent reaction on its own.** **DCPIP and methylene blue act as
artificial electron acceptors**, taking the electrons **NADP would normally receive**, and **as these
dyes are reduced they change colour**, so the **rate of colour change measures the rate of the
light-dependent reaction**. That is Hill's experiment: **isolated chloroplasts illuminated with no
carbon dioxide evolved oxygen only when an electron acceptor was added**.

**C6. The DCPIP endpoint is green, not colourless**, and three quarters of a cohort got this wrong.
The **dye alone goes from blue to colourless when reduced**, but the **suspension contains
chloroplasts, which are green**, so the mixture goes from **blue-green to green**. Read what is in the
tube, not what is in the definition.

## D. Light intensity, and the inverse square relationship

**D1. There are two ways to vary light intensity, and they are not equally clean.** **Neutral-density
filters** change intensity by a **stated percentage** while **leaving lamp distance and therefore
heating unchanged**, which is why the specimen supplied five of them. **Moving the lamp** changes
intensity too, but it changes the **heat reaching the plant** at the same time, so temperature is no
longer controlled.

**D2. Intensity falls with the square of the distance.** Light spreads over a sphere, so **light
intensity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the source**: **doubling the
distance quarters the intensity**, trebling it leaves a ninth. So **distance is not a linear measure of
intensity**, and plotting rate against distance produces a curve that means nothing on its own. If
distance was your variable, **plot rate against 1 over distance squared**.

**D3. The predicted graph, and its explanation, is a four-mark answer on its own.** The specimen opened
by asking for a **sketched, fully labelled graph of rate against light intensity, with the shape
explained**. The shape is Cluster 1's limiting-factor curve: the **rate rises steeply and roughly
linearly at low light intensity**, because **light intensity is the limiting factor**; then it **levels
off at a plateau**, because **light intensity is no longer limiting** and **another factor, carbon
dioxide concentration or temperature, has become limiting**. Label both axes and mark the **light
saturation point**.

**D4. Reading the results back against the prediction.** At the **darkest filter the limiting factor is
light intensity**, because the rate is still rising there. Where the points **plateau**, the plotted
variable has stopped limiting. If they never plateau across the range tested, the honest conclusion is
that **light was limiting throughout**, not that the prediction failed.

**D5. Improving confidence, a stock two-mark ask.** **Repeat and take a mean**, to reduce random
error. **Test more intermediate light intensities**, to define the curve's shape. **Control the
confounder you have not controlled yet**, usually temperature. **Allow the same equilibration time at
each intensity**, so every reading comes from a settled rate.

## E. Hydrogencarbonate indicator and the compensation point

**E1. Compensation point, defined the way the chapter defines it.** It is the **point at which the rate
of photosynthesis is equal to the rate of respiration**, and therefore the point at which there is **no
NET gaseous exchange between the plant and its environment**. On a graph of carbon dioxide exchange
against light intensity, it is **where the curve cuts the x axis**. It is **reached at quite low light
intensities, usually around sunrise and sunset**.

**E2. What sits either side of it.** **Below the compensation point, the rate of photosynthesis is less
than the rate of respiration**, giving a **net release of carbon dioxide and absorption of oxygen**,
and a **net loss of sugars**. **Above it, photosynthesis exceeds respiration**, giving a **net
absorption of carbon dioxide and release of oxygen**, and a **net gain of sugars**. At the point
itself, **all the carbon dioxide produced in respiration is used in photosynthesis** and **all the
oxygen produced in photosynthesis is used in respiration**.

**E3. The indicator turns a gas measurement into a colour.** It is **harmless to living organisms** and
**changes colour over a range of carbon dioxide concentrations, because of the change in pH**. The
scale the specimen printed: **red at 0.04%, normal atmospheric**; **purple below 0.04%**; **yellow
above 0.04%**. Underneath, **dissolved carbon dioxide forms an acid, lowering the pH**, so **more
carbon dioxide means yellow and less means purple**.

**E4. How that finds a compensation point.** Seal leaf material in tubes of indicator at a series of
light intensities. A tube turning **yellow** is **below** its compensation point, because the tissue
releases more carbon dioxide than it takes in; a tube turning **purple** is **above** it. The tube
whose indicator **stays red, showing no net change in carbon dioxide concentration**, marks the **light
intensity at which photosynthesis and respiration are equal**. The measured quantity is therefore a
**light intensity**, not a colour.

**E5. Variables, for the planning mark.** The **independent variable is light intensity**, varied by
filters or by distance with the inverse square correction. The **dependent variable is the carbon
dioxide concentration around the tissue, shown by indicator colour**, and the result reported is the
**light intensity at which no colour change occurs**. Controlled: **temperature**, **the area or mass
of leaf tissue**, **the volume of indicator**, **the time each tube is left**, **the starting carbon
dioxide concentration**, and **leaf age and species**.

**E6. Two controls, and only one is obvious.** A **tube of indicator with no leaf tissue** shows any
colour change came from the tissue rather than from handling or temperature. A **tube wrapped in foil**
gives the dark case, in which **only respiration runs**, so its indicator should turn yellow, which
confirms the tissue is respiring throughout.

## F. Sun and shade leaves

**F1. Shade leaves are built cheaper, and that is the whole argument.** **Shade leaves are thin with
fewer palisade mesophyll layers** and therefore **have fewer cells, so they require less energy for
maintenance**. Fewer cells maintained means a **lower rate of respiration**, and a lower respiration
rate means **less photosynthesis is needed to match it**, so **shade plants reach compensation point at
a lower light intensity and much sooner than sun plants**.

**F2. Sun leaves are built for capacity.** **Sun leaves are thick with more palisade mesophyll
layers**, so they **trap more light energy** and **absorb higher light intensities**. They carry a
**higher rate of respiration** and a **much higher maximum rate of photosynthesis**, which puts both
their **light saturation point and compensation point higher**.

**F3. Four rows of that twelve-row table do the work.** **Respiration rate**, **leaf thickness and
palisade layers**, **light saturation point**, and **compensation point**. The photosystem ratio,
chlorophyll ratio and thylakoid arrangement rows carry no learning outcome.

**F4. Reading the two-curve graph.** Plot carbon dioxide exchange against light intensity for both:
the **shade curve crosses the x axis at a lower light intensity** and **plateaus at a lower maximum
rate**. The **x intercepts are the two compensation points**, the **plateau heights the two maximum
rates**.

**F5. Why the specimen chose ivy.** **A single ivy plant produces sun leaves at the top and shade
leaves lower down**, so the comparison is made **within one plant**, controlling species, genotype, age
and growing conditions at a stroke.

## G. Measuring the rate of respiration

**G1. Respiration rate is oxygen uptake per unit time, and a respirometer measures it indirectly.**
**The manometer detects change in pressure or volume of a gas** in a sealed chamber holding small
respiring organisms such as **germinating seeds or maggots**.

**G2. The absorbent is what makes the reading mean oxygen.** **Carbon dioxide produced in respiration
is absorbed by soda lime or concentrated potassium or sodium hydroxide**, so **any decrease in the
volume of air results from oxygen consumption**. It is read as **movement of manometer fluid against a
scale**, or as **the volume of air injected from a syringe to level the fluid again**.

**G3. The compensation tube and the water bath control what would otherwise ruin the reading.**
**Changes in temperature and pressure alter the volume of air in the apparatus**, so **the temperature
must be kept constant with a thermostatically controlled water bath**, and **a control tube containing
an equal volume of inert material compensates for changes in atmospheric pressure**. Without it you
cannot tell a respiring organism from a passing weather front.

**G4. The three factors LO 3(k) names, and what each does.** **Substrate concentration**: more
substrate raises the rate until something else limits, and **plants respire more rapidly after
conditions favourable for photosynthesis**, when soluble sugars have accumulated. **Oxygen
concentration**: with little or none, **cells shift to anaerobic respiration and generate far less
ATP**, with **lactate accumulating in animals and ethanol and carbon dioxide in yeast**.
**Temperature**: the rate rises as with any enzyme-catalysed process, **Q10 about 2 between 8 and 18
°C**, then falls once enzymes denature.

**G5. The yeast dye method is the respiration twin of the DCPIP method.** **DCPIP or methylene blue is
added to a yeast suspension**, the dyes **do not damage the cells**, and **when reduced these blue dyes
become colourless**, so **the rate of change from blue to colourless measures the rate of
respiration**. One apparatus tests all three factors LO 3(k) names.

## H. Two things Paper 4 requires that your notes teach nowhere

Both of these are real holes, not oversights on your part. Do not invent content for either. Fill them
in the October practical block, from a Paper 4 mark scheme rather than from a textbook.

**H1. Respiratory quotient values by substrate.** The chapter defines RQ, derives both calculation
cases correctly, then stops without giving the values. What it does give: **RQ is carbon dioxide
produced divided by oxygen consumed**. Run the respirometer **with the absorbent** and the fall in
volume gives **oxygen consumed, x**; run it again **without the absorbent**, same organism, same
temperature. **A rise of y means more carbon dioxide was produced than oxygen absorbed, so RQ =
(x + y) / x.** **A fall of z means less, so RQ = (x − z) / x.** **No change means the volumes are equal
and RQ = 1.** What is missing is **which substrate gives which value**, which the question bank asks
for in twelve separate records including a full planning question. **Gap flagged. Take the values from
a mark scheme in October.**

**H2. Pigment separation by chromatography, and Rf calculation.** Book 5 mentions chromatography once,
inside the history box on Calvin's experiment. The **pigment separation practical is absent from the
notes entirely**, while the question bank carries it with full Paper 4 schemes covering table layout,
the calculation and the standard limitations. Your school teaches it in the laboratory instead:
**spinach pigment extraction is listed in the chapter's own Learning Experiences block**. What connects
it to Cluster 1 is that **different pigments absorb different wavelengths and appear as different
colours**, so separating an extract shows **which pigments a leaf actually contains**, which is LO 3(b)
evidence. **Rf is the distance moved by the pigment divided by the distance moved by the solvent
front.** **Gap flagged. Do not memorise Rf values until you have a mark scheme in front of you.**

## I. Data handling and planning, which are marked on every practical question

**I1. Quoting data has its own rejects.** Three separate examiner comments name the same four
failures: **selecting the wrong data series**, **omitting units**, **not reading to half the smallest
division**, and **not noticing the graph does not start at the origin**. The habit that beats all four:
**describe the trend, quote two values with units from the correct series, then account for it**.

**I2. A planning question is marked against a printed list, so answer the list.** The specimen's own
bullets ask for **a clear structure anyone could repeat**, **how results will be collected so they are
accurate and repeatable**, **relevant diagrams if needed**, the **dependent and independent variables
identified**, the **controlled variables identified**, and **correct technical and scientific terms**.
Ten marks, most of them for structure rather than cleverness.

**I3. Results tables are designed before the experiment starts.** Every column gets a **heading with
its unit**, there are **rows for repeats and a mean**, and the **independent variable comes first**.
The specimen gave five marks for a table built from scratch before any reading was taken.

## L. Errors in your notes, do not encode these

**Three errors in the practical sections.**

1. **The cyanide explanation on page 37 of chapter 5.1 is wrong for the pathway it names.** It says
   cyanide disrupts photosynthesis by disrupting bond interactions between R groups of proteins in the
   electron transport chain, which is a generic description of denaturation. **Cyanide's known action
   is to block electron transfer to oxygen in respiration**, which is why it appears again in chapter
   5.2 as an electron-flow blocker. The DCMU example above it is sound in substance, and is better
   written as **blocking electron transfer out of photosystem II**.
2. **Sclerenchyma is said to be impermeable to water and therefore able to transport it**, on page 8.
   The reasoning does not follow and sclerenchyma does not transport water. No outcome covers that
   section, so the cost is zero, but do not learn the sentence.
3. **The temperature section calls the same organism white potato and Irish potato in consecutive
   sentences.** Trivial, but it sits in the one section LO 3(k) points at.
