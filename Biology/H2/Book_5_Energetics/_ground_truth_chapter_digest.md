## Chapter 5.1: Energetics I, Photosynthesis (39 pages)

### 1. Stated learning outcomes (page 2, verbatim)

> **Learning Outcomes**
>
> Candidates should be able to:
> 3(a) identify components of chloroplasts and mitochondria in drawings, photomicrographs and electronmicrographs
> 3(b) explain the absorption and action spectra of photosynthetic pigments
> 3(c) with reference to the chloroplast structure, describe and explain how light energy is harnessed and converted into chemical energy during the light-dependent reactions of photosynthesis (names of complexes in the ETC and detailed mechanism of action of ATP synthase are not required)
> 3(d) outline the three phases of the Calvin cycle in the light-independent reactions of photosynthesis in C3 plants: (i) CO2 fixation, (ii) PGA reduction and (iii) ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP) regeneration, indicating the roles of rubisco, ATP and reduced NADP in these processes that ultimately allow synthesis of sugars (knowledge of details of the structure of intermediate compounds and the names of other enzymes is not required)
> 3(e) discuss limiting factors in photosynthesis and carry out investigations on the effect of limiting factors such as temperature, light intensity and carbon dioxide concentration on the rate of photosynthesis

> Use the knowledge gained in this section in new situations or to solve related problems.

### 2. Content node map

- **Introduction** (p.5 to 7)
  - Energy flow in the biosphere: solar energy captured, converted, used for cell work, dissipated as heat
  - ATP as the energy currency: adenine, ribose, three phosphates, phosphoanhydride bonds, 30.6 kJ per mole
  - The three routes to phosphorylating ADP: photophosphorylation, substrate-level phosphorylation, oxidative phosphorylation
  - Nutrition, autotrophs, photoautotrophs, the word equation for photosynthesis, why photosynthesis matters
- **The leaf as a photosynthetic organ** (p.8 to 10)
  - The three plant tissue types: dermal, vascular, ground
  - The three ground-tissue cell types: parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma
  - Leaf anatomy in transverse section: cuticle, upper epidermis, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll, intercellular spaces, lower epidermis, guard cells, vascular bundle
- **Chloroplasts** (p.11 to 15)
  - Structural features in five rows: size and shape, envelope, stroma, thylakoids, granum and intergranal lamellae
  - Light and pigments: the electromagnetic spectrum, reflection, transmission, absorption
  - Chlorophyll: porphyrin ring with magnesium, hydrocarbon tail, chlorophyll a versus accessory pigments, chlorosis
  - Carotenoids: carotenes and xanthophylls, the two functions, broadening the spectrum and photoprotection
  - Absorption spectrum against action spectrum, and the inference drawn from their similarity
- **Photosynthesis as a three-stage process** (p.17 to 27)
  - The three stages and where each happens
  - Excitation of chlorophyll: ground state, excited state, and the three routes back down (resonance energy transfer, electron transfer, heat and fluorescence)
  - Photosystems: light-harvesting complexes, reaction centre, primary electron acceptor, PSII with P680 and PSI with P700
  - The electron transport chain: carriers in series, stepwise energy release, the coenzymes NAD, FAD and NADP
  - Light harvesting stage
  - Non-cyclic photophosphorylation, the Z scheme, in eight numbered steps, including photolysis of water
  - Cyclic photophosphorylation, PSI only, in four numbered steps
  - Chemiosmosis in the chloroplast: impermeable thylakoid membrane, proton pumping by the cytochrome complex, photolysis as a second proton source, ATP synthase
  - Hill's experiment as the scientific-inquiry box, with DCPIP and methylene blue as lab substitutes
- **The Calvin cycle** (p.28 to 29)
  - Purpose, location, and the three phases named in the outcome's own order
  - Carbon dioxide fixation: RuBP, the unstable six-carbon intermediate, rubisco, two PGA
  - Reduction of PGA: phosphorylation by ATP, reduction by NADPH, triose phosphate
  - Regeneration of RuBP: five of six triose phosphate returned, three further ATP invested
  - Product synthesis: two triose phosphate per hexose, six turns per hexose, where the carbon, oxygen and hydrogen atoms come from
  - Calvin's lollipop experiment, 14C, two-dimensional paper chromatography, autoradiography
- **Fate of photosynthetic products** (p.30 to 31)
  - Carbohydrates, lipids and proteins from PGA and triose phosphate
- **Factors affecting photosynthesis** (p.33 to 38)
  - The principle of limiting factors, read off a light-intensity curve with regions A, B, C and points D and E
  - Light intensity, light saturation point, photobleaching
  - Compensation point, and sun plants against shade plants in a twelve-row table
  - Light quality, carbon dioxide concentration, temperature, chlorophyll concentration
  - Specific inhibitors: DCMU and cyanide
  - Water, oxygen and photorespiration, pollution

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 The colours of chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b are transposed in Table 2, page 13.** The table prints:

> "(1) Chlorophylls | chlorophyll a | **yellow-green** | All photosynthetic organisms except some photosynthetic bacteria"
> "chlorophyll b | **blue-green** | Higher plants and green algae"

Chlorophyll a is blue-green and chlorophyll b is yellow-green. `[INFERRED]` on the correction, from Campbell and from Taylor, Green and Stout, which is reference 4 in the chapter's own list and which the rest of the table matches row for row (chlorophyll c green, chlorophyll d green, bacteriochlorophyll pale blue, carotenes orange, xanthophylls all yellow, fucoxanthin brown). The transposition looks like a transcription slip from that source. It is a small error and it has no learning outcome behind it, but it sits inside LO 3(b) territory and a candidate who has memorised the table will describe the wrong pigment.

**5.2 Oxidative phosphorylation is defined backwards on page 6.** The chapter lists the three ways ADP is phosphorylated and writes:

> "(3) Oxidative phosphorylation: the final stage in aerobic respiration, which occurs in the presence of oxygen and **releases energy trapped in ATP** (mitochondria)."

Oxidative phosphorylation traps released energy **in** ATP. It does not release energy trapped in ATP, which is what ATP hydrolysis does. The chapter's own chapter 5.2 gets this right throughout. `[SCHOOL]` error, `[INFERRED]` correction.

**5.3 NAD, NADP and FAD are said to be reduced the same way, and they are not.** Page 21:

> "These coenzymes can pick up **a pair of electrons and a proton** thereby becoming reduced to **FADH2**, NADH and NADPH respectively."

That is right for NAD and NADP, which take two electrons and one proton. It is wrong for FAD, which takes two electrons and two protons, as the subscript 2 in FADH2 says out loud. Chapter 5.2's own Fig. 5 caption on page 8 states the NAD case correctly and separately. `[SCHOOL]` error.

**5.4 The proton-to-ATP ratio given on page 26 is not the modern figure and it does not agree with chapter 5.2.** Page 26:

> "This drives the formation of ATP catalysed by the enzyme ATP synthase, where **one ATP is synthesised for every two H+** that return to the stroma though the ATP synthase complex."

Two protons per ATP is Mitchell's original 1961 estimate. The chloroplast enzyme has a fourteen-subunit c ring and makes three ATP per full rotation, which is about 4.7 protons per ATP. Chapter 5.2 page 29 implies 3.33 protons per ATP for NADH and 3.0 for FADH2. **So the Book states or implies three different values for the same physical constant.** `[INFERRED]` on the modern figure. This is a real inconsistency but it is also entirely outside the syllabus, since LO 3(c), 3(h) and 3(l) each exclude the detailed mechanism of ATP synthase. Read it, do not memorise it, and never quote a proton-to-ATP ratio in an answer.

**5.5 A photosystem is said to have three components, one of which sits inside another.** Page 19 lists "(1) Light-harvesting complexes (LHCs) (2) Reaction centre (3) A primary electron acceptor", and then immediately says of the primary electron acceptor that "It is found in the reaction centre". A list of three parallel components in which the third is a part of the second is not a coherent breakdown. The standard structure is two components, the light-harvesting complexes and the reaction-centre complex, with the primary electron acceptor inside the latter. `[SCHOOL]` internal inconsistency.

**5.6 Photosystems are named by their reaction-centre chlorophylls.** Page 20: "**The two photosystems, P680 and P700**: are identical in their special chlorophyll a molecule." The two photosystems are PSII and PSI. P680 and P700 are the reaction-centre chlorophyll a molecules inside them, which the same page has just said correctly two bullets earlier. Imprecision rather than error, but it is exactly the imprecision a scheme penalises when it asks candidates to name a photosystem.

**5.7 Sclerenchyma is said to be impermeable to water and therefore able to transport it.** Page 8:

> "Cell walls contain lignin, a polymer which is **impermeable to water, hence, this also allows** specialised sclerenchyma cells to transport water"

The reasoning does not follow as written, and sclerenchyma fibres and sclereids do not transport water; lignified xylem elements do. This is inside the leaf-anatomy section, which has no learning outcome at all, so the cost is zero. Do not learn it.

**5.8 The carotenoid peak claim on page 14 is ambiguous as written.** "This can be seen from Fig. 11a - carotenoids peaks at a slightly higher wavelength than chlorophylls a and b." Carotenoids peak at roughly 450 to 500 nm, which is a higher wavelength than the **blue** peaks of chlorophyll a and b but a much lower one than their **red** peaks at 640 to 680 nm. The sentence is true only of the blue region and the chapter does not say so. `[INFERRED]` Read it as "in the blue part of the spectrum".

**5.9 The cyanide explanation on page 37 is vague and probably wrong for the pathway named.** "Another example is cyanide which disrupts photosynthesis by **disrupting the bond interactions between R-groups of proteins in the electron transport chain**." That is a generic description of denaturation. Cyanide's known action is to bind the iron of cytochrome oxidase, which is a respiratory complex, not a photosynthetic one. `[INFERRED]` The DCMU example immediately above it is sound in substance, although "short-circuits non-cyclic electron flow" would be better written as "blocks electron transfer out of photosystem II".

**5.10 The caption on page 39 is copied from page 26 and does not describe the figure.** Page 39 is a full-page OVERVIEW graphic showing the entire Z scheme across a labelled thylakoid membrane, from photolysis through both photosystems and the cytochrome complex to NADP reductase and ATP synthase. Its caption reads "**Fig. 33: Proton gradient driving the synthesis of ATP**", which is the caption of Fig. 24 on page 26, a small ATP synthase inset. The figure is correct and useful; the caption is a copy-paste. `[SCHOOL]`

**5.11 The answer key omits the answer to Concept Check 1 Question 2.** Chapter 5.1a reprints the question and its five-row option table and then gives nothing. Every other part of every Concept Check is answered. See the 5.1a section below.

**5.12 Minor typography and dated figures.** Page 15 and page 15's Fig. 11 caption use "absorption **spectrums**" where the chapter elsewhere uses "spectra". Page 36 gives atmospheric carbon dioxide as "between 0.03% and 0.04%", which is the traditional teaching figure and is what the 9477 specimen itself prints, so keep it. Page 30 refers to Calvin's discovery as "the dark reaction" while the whole chapter otherwise calls it the light-independent reaction; "dark reaction" is a term worth recognising and not worth writing.

**Everything else in the chapter checks out.** The non-cyclic and cyclic step sequences, the photolysis equation, the carrier order plastoquinone to cytochrome b-f to plastocyanin, the chemiosmosis argument, the Calvin cycle carbon accounting, the limiting-factor curve and the compensation-point treatment are all internally consistent and correct. **The Calvin cycle arithmetic in particular is exactly right**, which is worth saying plainly because it is the one place in the Book where a numerical error would cost real marks. ### 6. Load-bearing versus skippable

**Load-bearing, and this is where every mark in the chapter sits:**

- **Table 1 on page 12, the chloroplast structure-to-function table.** LO 3(a) asks for identification of chloroplast components and LO 3(c) says "with reference to the chloroplast structure", so this table is the answer to two outcomes. The two rows that do the most work are thylakoids (compartmentalisation makes chemiosmosis possible) and granum (stacking increases surface area for pigments).
- **Absorption spectrum against action spectrum, pages 15 and the two graphs.** LO 3(b) is a whole outcome for this one comparison. The mark-bearing move is the inference: the two curves have the same shape, therefore chlorophyll is the pigment mainly responsible for driving photosynthesis. The gap between the curves in the 450 to 550 nm region is what accessory pigments explain.
- **The three-stage framing on page 17**, because it is the skeleton every longer answer hangs on: light harvesting, light-dependent in the thylakoid membrane, light-independent in the stroma.
- **Non-cyclic photophosphorylation, page 23 to 24, step by step.** This is the core of LO 3(c). Photoexcitation of P680, capture by the primary electron acceptor, photolysis refilling the deficit, electron flow releasing free energy, proton pumping, PSI re-excitation, ferredoxin, NADP reductase, NADPH.
- **Cyclic photophosphorylation, page 24, and specifically what it does not produce.** PSI only, no photolysis, no oxygen, no NADPH, ATP only. The comparison table in Concept Check 2 is the exam shape.
- **Photolysis of water on page 23, with its equation.** It supplies the electrons, the oxygen and a second source of protons for the gradient. All three roles carry marks and the third is the one candidates forget.
- **Chemiosmosis, pages 25 to 26.** LO 3(l) makes this a shared outcome with respiration, so it must be learnable in one form and mapped onto both organelles. The four moves are: membrane impermeable to protons, electron flow drives pumping into the thylakoid space, gradient forms, protons diffuse back through ATP synthase and ATP is made.
- **The Calvin cycle, pages 28 to 29, in the outcome's own three phases.** Fixation with rubisco, reduction of PGA using ATP and reduced NADP, regeneration of RuBP. Plus the ratios: three carbon dioxide gives six triose phosphate of which five regenerate RuBP, two triose phosphate make one hexose, six turns per hexose.
- **The roles of rubisco, ATP and reduced NADP**, named in LO 3(d) in those words. Reduced NADP supplies the hydrogen, ATP supplies the energy, rubisco catalyses the carboxylation.
- **Limiting factors, pages 33 to 36.** LO 3(e) names temperature, light intensity and carbon dioxide concentration. The curve on page 33 with regions A, B and C and the saturation point D is the standard stem, and the multi-curve figure on page 36 is the standard follow-up.

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 The Overview page says one glucose yields 4 CO2. It yields 6, and the chapter's own budget table says so.** Page 5, inside the mitochondrion box:

> "Total: 36/38 ATP molecules **&** **4 CO2 + H2O**"

Page 23, section 5.3.3, the chapter's own budget table:

| Process | CO2 | ATP | NADH | FADH2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glycolysis | - | 2 | 2 | - |
| Link Reaction | **2** | - | 2 | - |
| Krebs Cycle (2 turns) | **4** | 2 | 6 | 2 |
| **TOTAL** | **6** | 4 | 10 | 2 |

Two from the link reaction plus four from the Krebs cycle is six. The Overview has taken the Krebs figure and labelled it as the total. Water is also wrong: complete oxidation of one glucose gives six water molecules, not an unquantified "H2O". `[SCHOOL]` error. This is the first page of substantive content in the chapter and it is the one page a student photographs for revision.

**5.2 The same Overview page gives two different ATP totals, twelve centimetres apart.** The flow chart box reads "**Total: 36/38 ATP molecules**". The boxed summary equation on the right of the same page reads:

> "**30-32** ADP + Pi → **30-32** ATP" above "C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → **6 CO2 + 6 H2O**"

36 or 38 is the legacy figure carried by the rest of the chapter. 30 to 32 is the modern figure, which accounts for the proton cost of transporting ATP, ADP and phosphate across the inner membrane. Both are in print on page 5 with no acknowledgement that they differ. `[SCHOOL]` internal contradiction. Note also that the boxed equation on the right gets **6 CO2 and 6 H2O** correct, which is what makes the "4 CO2" in the left-hand box an error rather than a convention.

**5.3 The proton-to-ATP ratios given on page 29 are inconsistent with each other.** Section 5.5.3:

> "For each pair of electrons stripped from NADH or reduced NAD: **5 pairs / 10 H+** are pumped across the inner mitochondrial membrane into the intermembrane space. Re-entry of H+ into the matrix provides the energy to generate **3 ATP** molecules."
> "For each pair of electrons stripped from FADH2 or reduced FAD: **3 pairs / 6 H+** are pumped... Re-entry of H+ into the matrix provides the energy to generate **2 ATP** molecules."

Ten protons for three ATP is 3.33 protons per ATP. Six protons for two ATP is 3.0. The same enzyme cannot have two different stoichiometries in the same mitochondrion. What has happened is that the chapter has taken the modern proton counts, which are right, and paired them with the legacy ATP yields, which come from a different accounting. With ten and six protons the consistent yields are about 2.5 and 1.5 ATP, which is where the 30 to 32 total on page 5 comes from. `[INFERRED]` on the diagnosis. The chapter itself prints "*** Calculation of total yield of ATP from oxidative phosphorylation is not required ***" immediately above this block, twice, so the whole section is outside the syllabus.

**5.4 Both fermentation equations are unbalanced by one hydrogen.** Page 35:

> Lactic Acid Fermentation: "NADH + C3H4O3 → NAD + C3H6O3"
> Alcoholic Fermentation: "NADH + C3H4O3 → NAD + C2H5OH + CO2"

Pyruvate carries four hydrogens and NADH supplies one, which is five on the left. Lactate carries six and ethanol carries six, on the right. The missing reactant in both is a proton: the correct forms are NADH + **H+** + C3H4O3 → NAD+ + C3H6O3 and NADH + **H+** + C3H4O3 → NAD+ + C2H5OH + CO2. `[INFERRED]` on the correction. Minor, and unlikely to be penalised, but it is an arithmetic error in a Book whose whole difficulty is arithmetic.

**5.5 The inner mitochondrial membrane is said to be permeable to pyruvate on one page and to require active transport on the next.** Page 18, Table 5, inner membrane row: "**permeable to pyruvate**". Page 19, section 5.2: "Upon entering the mitochondrion **via active transport**, pyruvate is first converted to a 2-carbon compound". Page 19, Fig. 15 caption: "Pyruvate is a charged molecule, so in eukaryotic cells it must enter the mitochondrion via **active transport**, with the help of a transport protein." Two against one, and the two are right. Write **active transport through a transport protein**. `[SCHOOL]` contradiction.

**5.6 The chapter counts four stages of aerobic respiration and Fig. 13's caption counts five.** The text on page 16 says "aerobic respiration consists of **4 main stages**" and Fig. 12's caption on the same page numbers them 1 to 4. Fig. 13's caption on page 17 then numbers glycolysis as stage 1, pyruvate oxidation as stage 2, the cycle as stage 3, electron transport and proton pumping as stage 4, and "the synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate" as **stage 5**. Fig. 13 is a borrowed figure that splits oxidative phosphorylation into two. Not a factual error, but it will confuse anyone answering "state the four stages".

**5.7 Acetyl CoA is repeatedly called a two-carbon compound.** Page 19: "pyruvate is first converted to a **2-carbon compound called acetyl CoA**". Page 5 Overview: "**2 Acetyl CoA (2C)**". The **acetyl group** is two carbons; coenzyme A is a large molecule with many more. The chapter's own next sentence gets it right: "The remaining **2C fragment** is oxidised, forming acetate". This shorthand is universal at A-level and will not be penalised, but it matters the moment a question asks a candidate to count carbons through the link reaction, which is exactly what LO 3(g) sets up.

**5.8 The Krebs cycle is named three different ways in one chapter.** "Krebs cycle" throughout, "Citric acid cycle" in the contents and in Table 4, and "**tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle**" in Fig. 13's caption on page 17. All three are correct and all three are in circulation. The 9477 syllabus uses "**Krebs cycle**" and only that, once, in LO 3(g). `[OFFICIAL]` Write Krebs cycle.

**5.9 Lactate and lactic acid are used interchangeably.** The chapter defines the distinction correctly on page 35 ("Lactate is the ionised form of lactic acid") and then prints "Products: **Lactic acid**, NAD" in the same table, and "2 **Lactic acid** (3C)" on page 5. LO 3(j) says "**lactate** in mammals". `[OFFICIAL]` Write lactate.

**5.10 The claim that ethanol's energy is permanently unavailable to yeast is an overstatement.** Page 36:

> "The energy in ethanol is **permanently unavailable to yeast** as 1 carbon has been lost as CO2, hence ethanol cannot be regenerated to pyruvate"

The second half is correct and is the mark-bearing point: ethanol cannot be converted back to pyruvate, which is the real contrast with lactate. The first half is not. Yeast oxidises ethanol aerobically once glucose runs out, which is the diauxic shift. `[INFERRED]` Reproduce the school's contrast, which is what the exam wants, but do not write "permanently".

**5.11 Two small inconsistencies in the fermentation table.** Page 5's Overview assigns alcoholic fermentation to "yeast & plants" and lactic acid fermentation to "bacteria & animals". Page 35's table assigns lactic acid fermentation to "animals and in certain fungi and bacteria" and alcoholic fermentation to "In fungi e.g. yeast" and "In most plant tissues". "Most plant tissues" overstates it; plants ferment to ethanol under anoxia, for example in waterlogged roots. The syllabus restricts LO 3(j) to **yeast and mammals**, so neither list needs learning.

**5.12 There is no Table 1.** The chapter's first numbered table is Table 2 on page 7. The catabolism and anabolism block on page 6 is unnumbered.

**5.13 The temperature section names the same organism two ways in consecutive sentences.** Page 37: "in **white potatoes**, rate of respiration increases if the temperature in lowered to just above freezing point. This increase... which tend to accumulate in **Irish potato** at temperature slightly above 0°C." Also "if the temperature **in** lowered" for "is lowered".

**5.14 The respiratory quotient section gives the method but never the values.** Section 8.3.3 on page 39 defines RQ, derives both calculation cases correctly and stops. It never states the characteristic RQ values by substrate: about 1.0 for carbohydrate, about 0.7 for lipid, about 0.9 for protein. **The question bank asks for exactly those values**, in twelve separate records including a full Paper 4 planning question. `[SCHOOL]` This is a content gap in the notes rather than an error in them, and it will cost marks unless it is filled from elsewhere.

**5.15 Glycolysis and its regulation are taught at a depth the outcome excludes, and the Concept Check tests it.** Section 4.2 on pages 12 to 13 walks all ten steps by name, section 4.4 on page 14 teaches phosphofructokinase as an allosteric enzyme with three regulators, and Concept Check 1 on page 15 is a full glycolytic pathway with ten lettered reactions from glucose-6-phosphate through phosphoenolpyruvate. LO 3(f) says outright that "knowledge of details of the intermediate compounds, names of enzymes and isomerisation is not required". This is not an error in the biology, which is sound, but it is the single largest block of unexaminable content in the Book. See the examiner guide's scope audit.

**Everything else in the chapter checks out, and the checking mattered.** The link reaction equation, the Krebs cycle carbon and coenzyme accounting, the section 5.3.3 budget table, the internal consistency of the 36 and 38 columns in the section 5.6 table, the "almost 90%" claim, the nineteen-fold comparison, the three classes of respiratory poison, the two components of the proton gradient and both respiratory quotient formulae are all correct. Full working is in the numerical audit below.

### 6. Load-bearing versus skippable

**Load-bearing, and this is where every mark in the chapter sits:**

- **Table 5 on page 18, mitochondrial structure to function.** LO 3(a) asks for identification of mitochondrial components and every stage question needs a location. Three rows carry the marks: cristae increase surface area for embedding the ETC and ATP synthase, the inner membrane is impermeable to protons which is what makes the gradient possible, and the matrix holds the link reaction and Krebs cycle enzymes. **9477 Specimen Paper 1 Question 21 is exactly this** and nothing more, and Specimen Paper 3 Question 1(a)(iv) is a six-mark version of it.
- **Glycolysis at outcome depth, page 11.** LO 3(f) names three things and only three: **location, raw materials, products**. The location is the cytoplasm, the raw materials are glucose, ADP, inorganic phosphate and NAD, and the products are two pyruvate, a net two ATP and two reduced NAD. The two mechanisms worth naming are substrate-level phosphorylation and dehydrogenation. Everything else in section 4.2 is excluded in writing.
- **The link reaction, page 19, with its equation.** LO 3(g) says "in terms of dehydrogenation and decarboxylation", so the answer is built from those two words: one decarboxylation releasing carbon dioxide and one dehydrogenation reducing NAD, twice per glucose, in the matrix.
- **The Krebs cycle at outcome depth, pages 20 to 23.** Same three-part frame plus the same two reaction types. Per turn: two decarboxylations, three NAD reductions, one FAD reduction, one substrate-level phosphorylation, oxaloacetate regenerated. Per glucose, double all of it.
- **The section 5.3.3 budget table on page 23.** Six carbon dioxide, four ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation, ten reduced NAD, two reduced FAD, per glucose. This is the one numerical table in the chapter that is fully correct and fully in scope, because it is products formed rather than total ATP yield.
- **The electron transport chain at outcome depth, pages 25 to 26.** LO 3(h) names "the roles of oxygen and the electron transport chain". Carriers of increasing electron affinity giving one-way flow, energy released in steps used to pump protons, oxygen as the final electron acceptor reduced to water, no ATP made directly by the chain itself, and the regeneration of NAD and FAD.
- **Why oxygen matters, which is the hinge of the whole Book.** Without oxygen the chain backs up, reduced NAD is not reoxidised, and the link reaction and Krebs cycle stop. **9477 Specimen Paper 2 Question 7(b) asked precisely this for three marks.**
- **Chemiosmosis, page 27, in the same four moves as chapter 5.1.** LO 3(l) is a shared outcome, so the answer must be portable between organelles. Membrane impermeable to protons, electron flow drives pumping into the intermembrane space, gradient with a chemical and an electrical component, protons return through ATP synthase and ATP is made.
- **Anaerobic respiration and the regeneration of NAD, pages 35 to 36.** LO 3(i) and 3(j) are two outcomes for what is really one argument. Glycolysis needs NAD; without oxygen the ETC cannot regenerate it; so pyruvate is used as the electron acceptor instead; so NAD is regenerated and glycolysis continues; so a small yield of two ATP per glucose is maintained. Lactate in mammals, ethanol and carbon dioxide in yeast.
- **Why lactate is recoverable and ethanol is not**, page 36. Lactate can be reconverted to pyruvate; ethanol cannot, because a carbon has left as carbon dioxide.
- **Section 8 in full, pages 37 to 40.** LO 3(k) names substrate concentration, oxygen concentration and temperature, and the respirometer, the soda lime, the compensation tube, the manometer reading and both respiratory quotient formulae are the apparatus the outcome implies. Add the RQ values by substrate from outside the chapter, since section 5.14 above shows the chapter never gives them.

**Read once and move on:**

- **Section 4.2, the ten steps of glycolysis by name, pages 12 to 13.** Excluded by LO 3(f) in writing. Glucose-6-phosphate, fructose-6-phosphate, fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, dihydroxyacetone phosphate, 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate, 3-phosphoglycerate, 2-phosphoglycerate and phosphoenolpyruvate all go.
- **Section 4.4, regulation of glycolysis, page 14.** Phosphofructokinase, allosteric inhibition by ATP and citrate, stimulation by AMP. No outcome. Note that the underlying **allosteric inhibition** concept does have an outcome, in Core Idea 1 LO (s), so the mechanism is examinable, just not this example.
- **Concept Check 1, page 15.** Built entirely on the excluded named intermediates. Part (a), where glycolysis occurs, is the only part that survives.
- **Section 5.3.2, the eight named steps of the Krebs cycle, page 22.** Citrate, isocitrate, succinate and oxaloacetate. LO 3(g) asks for raw materials and products, not intermediates. Oxaloacetate is the one worth keeping, and only because the chapter uses it to explain why the pathway is a cycle.
- **Section 5.4.2's complexes I to IV, page 25, and Figs. 18, 21 and 22.** LO 3(h) and LO 3(l) both say "names of complexes in the ETC are not required". Ubiquinone and cytochrome c go with them.
- **Section 5.5.2, the ATP synthase rotor, page 28.** "Detailed mechanism of action of ATP synthase is not required", in LO 3(c) and again in LO 3(h). Keep one sentence: protons pass through ATP synthase and their passage drives phosphorylation of ADP.
- **Section 5.5.3 and 5.6, the ATP arithmetic, pages 29 to 30.** The school prints "*** Calculation of total yield of ATP from oxidative phosphorylation is not required ***" at the top of both sections, in bold, twice. Take it at its word. Know that oxidative phosphorylation produces the great majority of the ATP, which Table 6 puts at "almost 90%", and stop there.
- **The shuttle systems, page 31.** Explicitly marked "(Supplementary Reading)" by the school, and it exists only to explain the difference between 36 and 38, which is itself excluded.
- **Section 5.8, respiratory poisons, page 33.** No outcome names them. But this is the one skippable section worth reading properly, because inhibitors are the standard vehicle for testing whether a candidate understands the chain: block electron flow, no gradient, no ATP; inhibit ATP synthase, gradient grows, no ATP; uncouple, gradient collapses, energy lost as heat. Learn the three consequences, forget the compound names.
- **Section 3.1's obligate and facultative classification, page 9.** Vocabulary, no outcome.
- **Fig. 6 on page 9, protein and lipid entry points.** "Respiratory substrate" returns zero syllabus hits. But respiratory quotient questions are built on lipid and protein substrates, so keep the single fact that fats and amino acids feed in at acetyl CoA and at various Krebs intermediates.

## Cross-chapter findings

Seven issues run across the Book rather than sitting inside one chapter.

**A. Book 5 is one Core Idea and twelve consecutive learning outcomes, which is unlike anything before it.** Book 3 spanned three Core Ideas and its questions were scattered across Paper 1 from Q8 to Q22. Book 5 is Core Idea 3 LOs (a) to (l) with nothing else mixed in, and on the 9477 specimen its Paper 1 items landed at **Q19, Q20 and Q21, consecutively**. `[OFFICIAL]` That is the opposite behaviour, and it follows from Paper 1 sweeping the syllabus in learning-outcome order. Practically, it means Book 5 questions arrive in a block and a candidate who is weak here loses several marks in a row rather than one at a time.

**B. Chemiosmosis is taught twice and neither chapter cross-references the other.** Chapter 5.1 pages 25 to 26 teaches it in the thylakoid; chapter 5.2 pages 27 to 28 teaches it in the mitochondrion. The wording differs ("proton gradient" against "proton motive force", "active transport of H+" against "proton pumps"), the diagrams come from different sources, and neither chapter says "this is the same process you have already met". **LO 3(l) is a single outcome covering both**, which means the exam can and will ask for the comparison. That mapping is the most valuable single thing to build out of this Book and neither chapter builds it.

| | Chloroplast | Mitochondrion |
|---|---|---|
| Membrane the gradient sits across | thylakoid membrane | inner mitochondrial membrane |
| Protons pumped from | stroma | matrix |
| Protons pumped into | thylakoid space or lumen | intermembrane space |
| High proton concentration | thylakoid space | intermembrane space |
| Energy source for pumping | electron flow from photolysis and photoexcitation | electron flow from reduced NAD and FAD |
| Second proton source | photolysis of water | none |
| Protons return through | ATP synthase, into the stroma | ATP synthase, into the matrix |
| Final electron acceptor | NADP, or back to PSI in cyclic flow | oxygen |
| Name for the ATP synthesis | photophosphorylation | oxidative phosphorylation |
