> Candidates should be able to:

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 Singapore's net-zero date is out of date and wrong by fifty years.** Page 1: *"Under the Paris Agreement, Singapore targets to peak its emissions at 65 million tonnes by around 2030, reduce that amount by around 50% by 2050, and subsequently achieve **net-zero emissions by 2100**."* Singapore raised its target to **net zero by 2050** in February 2022, and that has been the national commitment since. `[INFERRED]` on the correction. The peak figure of 65 MtCO2e around 2030 and the roughly 50% reduction by 2050 are both correct; only the net-zero date is stale, and it is stale by the old 2020 Long-Term Low-Emissions Development Strategy wording ("as soon as viable in the second half of the century"). If a Singapore-context stem asks for the national target, write **2050**.

**5.2 Tropical rainforest land cover is stated as 2% and the true figure for land surface is about three times that.** Page 16: *"although tropical rainforests cover only **2% of the Earth's land surface**, they are estimated to contain at least 50% of the Earth's known terrestrial plant and animal species."* Two per cent is the figure for the **whole planetary surface** including ocean. As a share of **land** surface the figure is roughly 6%. `[INFERRED]` The 50% species claim is standard and fine. The rhetorical point survives either way, but the number as printed is attached to the wrong denominator.

**5.3 The chapter gives two different figures for how much of Earth's freshwater the polar ice holds, and chapter 8.3 gives a third.** This is the clearest numerical contradiction in the Book.

- 8.1 page 17: *"They are responsible for **70%** of the Earth's supply of freshwater."*
- 8.3 page 50: *"Together, these ice sheets contain more than **99%** of the freshwater on Earth."*
- 8.3 page 55: *"Of that 3%, almost **70%** comes from ice caps and glaciers, making them the largest reservoirs of freshwater"*

The 70% figure is right for **all freshwater**. The 99% figure is right only for **freshwater ice**, meaning the world's glacial ice, and page 50 drops the word "ice", which turns a true statement into a false one. `[INFERRED]` Use 70%.

**5.4 The atmospheric CO2 figure on page 13 contradicts every other CO2 figure in the Book and is badly stale.** Page 13 gives atmospheric composition as *"roughly 78% nitrogen, 20.95% oxygen, 0.93% argon, **0.038% carbon dioxide**"*. That is 380 ppm. Chapter 8.2 Table 2.1 (page 32) gives approximate current CO2 as 392,000 ppb, which is 392 ppm; page 34 gives *"above 394 ppm"*; page 35 gives *"over 400 ppm in 2013"*. The real 2026 figure is above 420 ppm. Four numbers, four different values, in one Book. `[SCHOOL]` Do not memorise any of them as "the current concentration". Memorise the **pre-industrial 280 ppm**, which is stable and which the school states consistently, and describe the present value as "over 400 ppm and rising".

**5.5 The solar-energy split is 29 / 71 on page 21 and 30 / 70 on page 22.** Page 21, Fig. 1.13: *"Much of the incoming radiation from the Sun (**29%**) is reflected back to space, but the remainder (**71%**) is absorbed"*. Page 22: *"Earth's average albedo is about 0.3. In other words, about **30%** of incoming solar radiation is reflected back into space and **70%** is absorbed."* Both are within rounding of the true value and neither is wrong, but they are one page apart and a candidate quoting the figure should quote 30 / 70, because that is the one tied to the albedo of 0.3 which the outcome-adjacent content actually uses.

**5.6 Table 1.1 is titled in per cent while the text defines albedo as a proportion.** Page 22 text: *"Albedo is expressed as a **proportion (0-1.0)**"*. Page 22 table title: *"Albedo (Reflectivity) of surfaces expressed in **percentage**."* Not an error, but a unit switch inside one page, and albedo questions turn on getting the scale right.

**5.7 The chapter says to go to Google Classroom for sections it then prints in full.** Page 10 ends: *"*** Refer to Google Classroom for: Section 1.2: The Complexity of the Global Climate System / Section 1.3: Absorbed Solar Energy Warms Our World"*. Pages 11 to 24 then print both sections in their entirety. `[SCHOOL]` Harmless, but it is evidence that the outline and the body were assembled at different times, which matters for §5.8.

**5.8 The two outlines in this chapter disagree with each other and with chapter 8.5.** The master outline on pages 3 to 4 and the Section 1 outline on page 7 give different page numbers for section 1.2 (11 against 10) and different titles for section 1.3 ("Warms Our World" against "Warms Our Earth"). More consequentially, the master outline on page 4 prints:

> 5.2 Consequences of Increased Environmental Stress to Sustainable Food Supply 111 / 113
> 5.2.1 **Impact of Temperature Changes on Insects**

while chapter 8.5's own outline (page 97) and its body heading (page 113) both read **"5.2.1 Impact of Climate Change on Agro-Ecosystems"**. The master outline has copied the heading of section 4.2.1 into the 5.2.1 slot, and the page numbers 111 and 113 are printed on the wrong rows. `[SCHOOL]` Navigate by chapter 8.5's own outline, not by chapter 8.1's.

**5.9 The greenhouse-effect gloss "absorb (i.e. retain)" is wrong and the next sentence corrects it.** Page 23: *"Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere **absorb (i.e. retain)** and redirect some of this energy downward"*. Absorption is not retention. A greenhouse gas molecule absorbs an infrared photon, is excited, and **re-emits**, in all directions, some of it downward. The chapter's own next paragraph on page 24 says exactly that: *"This absorbed radiation is **emitted downward** toward the surface as longwave radiation"*. Write absorb and re-emit or re-radiate. **Retain is the word that loses the mark**, because it implies the heat stops there.

**5.10 The extinction projection on page 1 restates a real study as something stronger than the study said.** Page 1: *"By the year 2050, climate change is **expected to cause the extinction** of approximately at least one quarter of all species on land."* The figure traces to Thomas and co-workers, 2004, in *Nature*, which projected 15% to 37% of species **committed to** extinction by 2050 under mid-range warming. "Committed to extinction" and "extinct" are different claims separated by decades of extinction debt. `[INFERRED]` Quote it as "at risk of extinction" if you use it.

**5.11 The IPCC temperature projection on page 1 is at the extreme edge and is missing a word.** Page 1: *"The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has predicted that by 2100, **the Earth's surface will rise by up to 6 C** on average."* Two problems. The sentence omits "temperature", so as written the Earth's surface rises by six degrees Celsius, which mixes the units of sea-level rise into a temperature claim. And 6 °C is at the top of the discarded AR4 high-emissions envelope; AR6's very-high-emissions SSP5-8.5 best estimate to 2100 is about 4.4 °C with a likely range to roughly 5.7 °C. `[INFERRED]` Chapter 8.5 quotes a more defensible pair of figures, 3 °C under SSP2-4.5 and 6 °C under SSP5-8.5, via the question bank. Chapter 8.3 page 61 separately uses *"the projected 3 °C to 4 °C increase in temperature by the end of this century"*. Three different projections in one Book.

**5.12 Minor typography.** Page 4 outline row for section 5.2.1 sits below its page number rather than beside it. Page 18 Fig. 1.8 caption: *"The levels of organization **of in** nature."* Page 19: *"Carnivores that **consumer** the herbivores"*. Page 25 Fig. 1.16 caption: *"**accross** the tropical Pacific Ocean"*. Page 24: *"Earth's average surface temperature **would well below** freezing"*, missing "be". Page 10: *"Earth's orbital cycle **and and** volcanic eruptions"*.

Beyond these, the chapter is factually sound. The albedo section, the greenhouse-effect mechanism, the net-radiation argument, the ENSO description and the volcanic-aerosol mechanism are all internally consistent and correct.

### 6. Load-bearing versus skippable

**Say it plainly: chapter 8.1 is almost entirely context, and it is the clearest case of padding in the Book.** Not one of the nine outcomes requires anything defined in this chapter as recall. I checked every term against the syllabus by literal search. **Albedo, biome, greenhouse effect, weather, climate system, endemic species, trophic level, El Niño, ENSO, La Niña, permafrost and food web return zero hits in the whole 9477 syllabus.** `[OFFICIAL]` The words that do appear from this chapter's vocabulary appear only inside outcomes that live in other chapters: *ecosystem*, *habitat*, *food chain*, *niche* and *biodiversity* are all carried by LO (f) or LO (g), which chapters 8.4 and 8.5 serve.

**Load-bearing, and it is a short list:**

- **The greenhouse-effect mechanism, pages 23 to 24, at the level of a causal chain.** Shortwave solar radiation is absorbed by Earth's surface; the warmed surface re-emits longwave infrared; greenhouse gases absorb that longwave radiation and re-emit it, some of it downward; near-surface temperature rises. LO (a) asks candidates to *"identify and explain the human activities... that have contributed to climate change **through accumulation of greenhouse gases**"*, and LO (b) asks for *"the effects of climate change **as a result of greenhouse gas emissions**"*. Both outcomes assume this chain and neither teaches it, so it is required by implication. The question bank confirms it: one prelim mark scheme makes it a **compulsory point** in an otherwise open answer, quoted verbatim as *"greenhouse gases absorb long-wave / infrared radiation, trapping/holding heat within the atmosphere"* `[SCHOOL]`. **This is the single load-bearing paragraph in 26 pages.**
- **Albedo, page 22, but only as the positive-feedback loop, not as a definition.** The syllabus never uses the word. What is examinable is the mechanism it drives, and it is examinable because **9477 Specimen Paper 1 Question 28** hands the candidate a feedback web in which melting sea ice reduces sun reflection, which raises absorption, which raises warming, which melts more sea ice, and asks which arrow reduces warming. `[OFFICIAL]` The candidate needs the loop, not the word and not Table 1.1's values.
- **The four terms LO (f) carries: habitat, ecological niche, food chain, organism.** Page 15's definitions and page 20's habitat-against-niche distinction are the vocabulary of LO (f), which reads *"...on habitats, organisms, food chains and niche occupation"*. Learn these four here so that chapter 8.4 can be read as application. ### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 The chapter gives methane two different shares of total greenhouse gas output, five pages apart.** Table 2.1 on page 32 gives methane's percentage of total anthropogenic GHG emissions as **16%**. Page 37 states: *"Methane (CH4) is the second most important greenhouse gas, accounting for **14%** of our greenhouse gas output."* `[SCHOOL]` Carbon dioxide is consistent at 76% in both places. Quote 76% for CO2 and describe methane as "roughly 15%" rather than committing to either printed figure.

**5.2 Figure 2.1's caption double-counts industry and the sector shares therefore do not partition.** Page 30, Fig. 2.1 caption: *"**Electricity production, transportation, heating and industrial activities** that depend on fossil fuels together produce 50% of our greenhouse gases. Deforestation and agriculture account for another 30%. **The remaining 20% is produced by industry.**"* Industrial activities appear inside the 50% and then again as the whole of the remaining 20%. The three shares sum to 100% only if industry is counted twice. `[SCHOOL]` Do not reproduce this partition.

**5.3 The chapter's global warming potentials are AR4 values and the 9477 specimen uses AR5 values. This one costs marks.**

- School, page 31 and Table 2.1: methane 100-year GWP = **25**; nitrous oxide = **298**. Sourced *"IPCC, 2007"*, which is AR4.
- **9477 Specimen Paper 1 Question 26** prints, in its own table, methane 100-year GWP = **28** and nitrous oxide = **265**. `[OFFICIAL]`

Both are legitimate published values from different assessment reports. The consequence is practical rather than academic: **the specimen supplies the GWP in the stem and expects the candidate to use the supplied number.** A candidate who has memorised 25 and substitutes it into a table that says 28 gets the arithmetic wrong. **Read the GWP off the paper, every time.** The number worth memorising is the shape of the fact, which is that methane's GWP is roughly 25 to 30 times that of carbon dioxide over a century, not the exact integer.

**5.4 The chapter says methane is accumulating faster than CO2 on page 37 and says the opposite on page 32.**

- Page 32: *"Fortunately, methane and nitrous oxide are being emitted at **much slower rates than CO2**, so their overall effect is still less than that of CO2. Consequently, as time passes, the relative importance of CO2 will only increase."*
- Page 37: *"CH4 is 25 times more effective a greenhouse than an equivalent mass of CO2. It absorbs much more energy per gram as CO2 does and is **accumulating at a faster rate than CO2**."*

`[SCHOOL]` As printed these contradict. The reconcilable reading is that methane's **proportional** rise since 1750 is larger (about 150% against about 41%, both stated by the chapter) while its **absolute** emission rate is far smaller. The chapter never states the distinction, so a candidate who quotes page 37 in an answer about total warming contribution will be arguing against page 32.

NO2 is nitrogen dioxide, a different molecule with different chemistry and no comparable GWP. Nitrous oxide is **N2O**, which the chapter writes correctly on pages 29, 31, 32 and 44. `[SCHOOL]` A single-instance typo, but it is a formula error in a formula-sensitive subject.

**Load-bearing:**

- **The three named human activities of LO (a), each with a stated mechanism ending in a named gas.** Burning of fossil fuels linked to increasing energy usage gives **CO2**; clearing of forests gives **CO2** twice over, once by removing the sink and once by releasing the store; food choices, meaning increasing meat consumption, gives **CH4** from enteric fermentation and manure. - **The direct against indirect distinction, page 40.** The carbon-footprint definition states it: *"emissions that are **directly caused** (e.g. from burning fuel in a car...) and those that are **indirectly caused** (e.g., emissions associated with the production, transportation, and disposal of food and goods)."* **9477 Specimen P2 Q8(b)** asks for one anthropogenic activity that **indirectly** contributes and an explanation of how. `[OFFICIAL]` The bank records a prelim scheme rejecting *"enlarging cities / choice of food, as these cause deforestation and so are indirect causes"* when a **direct** cause was asked for `[SCHOOL]`, which is the same distinction tested in the other direction. **This distinction is worth more marks per line than any other node in Book 8.**
- **Carbon sink against carbon source, page 30, and the deforestation double effect, page 29.** The bank's specimen-derived answer runs exactly this way: removing the trees removes a carbon sink, so less CO2 is taken out by photosynthesis, and the atmospheric concentration rises **even though the deforestation itself released nothing**. That "even though" is what makes it an indirect cause.
- **The three factors determining a gas's warming influence, page 31.** Concentration or abundance, atmospheric lifetime, and global warming potential. LO (d) is a comparison outcome and these three are the axes on which the comparison is made. **Specimen P1 Q26** is a pure GWP-arithmetic question: multiply mass released by 100-year GWP to get CO2-equivalent mass, then rank. `[OFFICIAL]`
- **Carbon dioxide equivalent, CO2e, and why the unit exists, page 40 and page 42.** **Specimen P2 Q8(c)(i)** asks *"Suggest why the carbon footprint in Table 8.1 is measured in terms of an equivalent mass of carbon dioxide."* `[OFFICIAL]` The answer is that different gases have different GWPs, so converting every gas to the mass of CO2 that would cause the same warming puts all sources on one comparable scale.
- **Life-cycle assessment, page 42, and specifically why a zero-emission generator has a non-zero footprint.** Raw material extraction, manufacturing, construction and transport all emit even when operation does not. **Specimen P2 Q8(c)(iii)** asks for two reasons wind and hydroelectric power have footprints greater than zero. `[OFFICIAL]` The school's own sentence supplies it: renewables emit *"primarily... **upstream during construction / manufacturing**."*

The crops absorb CO2 as they grow, offsetting combustion emissions, which is why the footprint is low; harvesting, processing and transport still emit, which is why it is not zero. **Specimen P2 Q8(c)(ii)** is this exact question. `[OFFICIAL]` The bank records a scheme rejecting *"burning of biofuel releases large net amount of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere, since biofuel is supposed to be carbon neutral"* `[SCHOOL]`, which punishes a candidate who has half the argument.
- **Figure 2.15's food ranking, page 44, as a ranking.** Lamb 39.2 kg CO2e per kg, then beef, then pork, then chicken, then vegetable proteins at roughly one thirteenth of lamb. Plus the reason: lamb and beef are ruminants generating methane by enteric fermentation, and ruminants need more grazing land, more fertiliser and more energy-intensive feed. LO (d) names *"food production (animal- and plant-based)"* and this figure is the whole outcome in one graphic.
- **The cradle-to-grave breakdown, pages 45 to 46, at the level of which stage dominates.** Production dominates for beef at 90%; processing is 5% for lamb and beef against 24% for chicken; transportation is about 10% and does not vary much between meats; wasted food is at least 20%. The point the chapter makes, and which the bank rewards, is that **transport is a small share and buying locally does much less for meat than public debate suggests**.

These two pairs are the most-quoted numbers in the bank and they are internally consistent across the chapter, unlike the current-concentration figures.
- **The Keeling curve's annual oscillation, page 35.** The 5 to 7 ppm sawtooth reflects Northern Hemisphere seasonal photosynthesis against respiration: CO2 falls when photosynthesis predominates from late spring through early autumn, and rises when respiration predominates. **This is a Core Idea 3 answer inside a Book 8 figure** and it is a stock data-interpretation question.

- **The carbon cycle diagram's gigaton flux numbers, page 33, Fig. 2.4.** The cycle itself is load-bearing as a concept, meaning photosynthesis fixes, respiration and combustion release, sinks store. The specific annual fluxes are not.

Keep one line, that fertiliser use releases N2O with a very high GWP, because it is a legitimate **indirect** activity for Specimen-style Q8(b) and the bank accepts it. Do not build a node on it.

### 2. Content node map

- **Front matter** (p.47–48): outcome block, Section 3 outline, full-page concept map
- **3.1 Impact of global warming** (p.49–50)
  - Global warming defined as a 30-year averaged combined surface air and sea surface temperature relative to 1850–1900
  - The roughly 1 °C above pre-industrial figure and the 0.2 °C per decade rate
  - Land warming faster than ocean; the 20–40% of population already past 1.5 °C in one season
- **3.1.1 Melting of polar ice caps** (p.50–53)
  - Polar ice caps, sea ice, ice sheets and ice shelves distinguished
  - Four impacts: rise in sea level; long-term decline in freshwater resources; accelerated warming from albedo loss; severe threats to polar communities
  - Why melting **sea ice** does not raise sea level
  - The ice-albedo positive feedback loop
  - Three ecological roles of sea ice: insulation of the water beneath, breeding and hunting platform, nutrient release stimulating phytoplankton
  - Polar bear, seal, walrus and nesting-bird case
- **3.1.2 Rising sea levels** (p.53–54)
  - Two causes: thermal expansion of seawater as the main cause, and melting of continental ice
  - Impacts: threats to coastal communities; saltwater intrusion
- **3.1.3 Stress on freshwater supplies** (p.55–57)
  - Distribution of Earth's water across reservoirs
  - Water stress defined
  - Two mechanisms: polar ice melt turning freshwater into seawater and altering streamflow timing; higher evaporation redistributing precipitation, plus runoff quality loss and saltwater intrusion
- **3.1.4 Increase in frequency of extreme weather events** (p.57–60)
  - A. Heat waves: definition, high-humidity against low-humidity types, agricultural and livestock impacts, droughts and wildfire
  - B. Heavy rains: the evaporation-to-precipitation mechanism, runoff and water quality, flooding, regional non-uniformity
- **3.1.5 Stress to coral reef ecosystem** (p.60–63)
  - Coral biology: polyps, nematocysts, calcium carbonate exoskeleton, colony to reef
  - The coral–zooxanthellae symbiosis, three named benefits each way
  - Coral bleaching mechanism and recovery
  - Ocean acidification and hard-coral calcification
- **3.1.6 Stress to seagrass and mangrove ecosystems** (p.64–67)
  - The mangrove–seagrass–reef interconnected network
  - A. Mangroves: definition, area, five ecological roles, four climate stressors
  - B. Seagrass beds: definition, area, four ecological roles including blue carbon, three climate stressors
  - C. Consequences of stress to both
- **3.1.7 Migration of fishes and insects** (p.68–69)
  - A. Fishes: ectothermy, narrow preferred temperature, poleward and deeper shifts, ecological and economic impact, snowmelt-timing disruption
  - B. Insects: ectothermy, low temperature as the range determinant, poleward migration, monarch butterfly case, crop pests and disease vectors moving poleward
- **3.1.8 Release of greenhouse gases from frozen organic matter** (p.70)
  - Permafrost defined, active layer, carbon store
  - Microbial decomposition on thaw releasing CO2 and CH4
  - The permafrost positive feedback loop; the IPCC 20–35% decline projection
- **3.2 Mitigation of impacts of climate change by mangrove ecosystems** (p.71–72)
  - Mitigation defined
  - A. Mitigation of global warming: the four-step blue-carbon burial mechanism

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 The chapter names two different hottest years on record, on the same page.** Page 58 body text: *"**2024 was the hottest year on record globally**, marking the first time the Earth's average temperature surpassed the crucial 1.5°C warming threshold above pre-industrial levels, with 2023 and 2025 also ranking extremely high"*. Page 58 Fig. 3.12 caption: *"**2025 was the hottest year in recorded history**, according to research by NASA and the US NOAA."* `[SCHOOL]` A direct contradiction between body and caption within one page. Do not quote either as a fact; the safe form is that the last decade contains the ten warmest years on record, which the same paragraph also states and which is not in dispute.

**5.2 Ice sheets are said to hold more than 99% of Earth's freshwater, which conflicts with the chapter's own page 55 and with chapter 8.1.** Page 50: *"Together, these ice sheets contain **more than 99% of the freshwater on Earth**."* Page 55: *"Of that 3%, **almost 70%** comes from ice caps and glaciers, making them the largest reservoirs of freshwater"*. Chapter 8.1 page 17 also says 70%. `[SCHOOL]` The 99% figure is true only of the world's **freshwater ice**, and page 50 has dropped the word "ice". **Use 70%.**

**5.3 The freshwater reservoir percentages on page 55 sum to 100% and leave nothing for rivers, lakes or the atmosphere, which the same paragraph then discusses.** Page 55: *"Of that 3%, almost **70%** comes from ice caps and glaciers... The remaining **30%** comes from shallow groundwater – the largest reservoir of usable freshwater. Although **rivers and lakes** are the most heavily used water resources, they represent only a tiny amount of the world's freshwater. Less than 0.05% of water is in the **atmosphere**."* Seventy plus thirty is one hundred, so the paragraph's own "remaining 30%" leaves zero for the rivers, lakes and atmosphere it goes on to name. `[SCHOOL]` The correct split is roughly 68.7% glaciers and ice, 30.1% groundwater and about 1.2% surface and other. Say "roughly 70% and roughly 30%", or say "almost all", and do not use "the remaining".

**5.4 Nematocysts are named as the tentacles rather than as the stinging structures within them.** Page 60: *"A polyp has a sac-like body and an opening, or mouth, encircled by **stinging tentacles called nematocysts**."* A nematocyst is the stinging **organelle** inside a cnidocyte, and a tentacle bears many of them. `[INFERRED]` on the correction. Write **tentacles bearing nematocysts**.

**5.5 The chapter says a coral colony "excretes" its exoskeleton on page 60 and "secretes" it on page 62.** Page 60: *"Each colony **excretes** a calcium carbonate exoskeleton beneath it"*. Page 62: *"a coral polyp **secretes** layer upon layer of calcium carbonate underneath its body"*. `[SCHOOL]` Excretion is the removal of metabolic waste; deposition of a skeleton is secretion. **Secretes** is correct and the chapter says so two pages later. This distinction is enforced elsewhere in H2 Biology and is the kind of word a scheme will police.

**5.6 The ocean acidification mechanism is stated in a form that is chemically wrong, and the mark schemes in the corpus state it correctly.** Page 62: *"Under this condition, **corals cannot absorb the calcium carbonate they need** to create and maintain their exoskeletons and the stony skeletons that support corals and reefs will dissolve."* Corals do not absorb calcium carbonate. They take up calcium ions and hydrogencarbonate or carbonate ions and **deposit** CaCO3. The mechanism the schemes reward, quoted from the bank, is: *"About a quarter of the additional atmospheric carbon dioxide dissolves in seawater and reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which dissociates and releases H⁺ ions, lowering the pH of the ocean. The extra H⁺ ions react with **carbonate ions** to form hydrogencarbonate ions, so the concentration of **free carbonate ions** in the water falls. Corals need carbonate ions to deposit calcium carbonate for their exoskeleton, so the **rate of calcification slows** and existing skeletons begin to dissolve."* `[SCHOOL]` **This is a case where the school's notes are demonstrably weaker than the mark scheme, and it sits on an examined mechanism. Learn the scheme's version.**

**5.7 The 30% ocean acidification statistic is stated in a way that misuses pH.** Page 62: *"**The pH of the ocean is about 30% more acidic** now than it was in 1751."* pH is a logarithmic scale; it did not change by 30%. What rose by about 30% is the **hydrogen ion concentration**, which corresponds to a pH fall of about 0.1 units. `[INFERRED]` A candidate who writes "the pH fell by 30%" is writing something false. Say **the hydrogen ion concentration has risen by about 30%**, or **the pH has fallen by about 0.1 units**.

**5.8 Permafrost is defined as "permanently frozen for at least two years", which is self-contradictory, and chapter 8.1 gives a better version.** Page 70: *"Permafrost is soil that has been **permanently frozen for at least two years**."* Chapter 8.1 page 16: *"permafrost, which is underground soil in which captured water stays frozen for **more than two consecutive years**."* `[SCHOOL]` The standard definition is ground that remains at or below 0 °C for at least two consecutive years. Chapter 8.1's wording is the usable one.

**5.9 The Greenland sea-level contribution is given as 6 m in one section and 5 to 7 m in the next, and the Antarctic figure changes basis without saying so.** Page 51: *"If the Greenland ice sheet melted, scientists estimate that sea level would rise about **6 metres** and if the **Antarctic ice sheet** melted, sea level would rise by about **60 metres**."* Page 53: *"the eventual irreversible melting of the Greenland ice sheet, resulting in roughly **5 m to 7 m** of global sea rise. Melting of the most unstable part of the Antarctic ice sheet (the **West Antarctic** ice sheet) could add an additional **5 metres**."* The Greenland pair is consistent, since 6 lies inside 5 to 7. The Antarctic pair is not comparing like with like: 60 m is the whole Antarctic ice sheet, 5 m is the West Antarctic sheet only. `[SCHOOL]` Not an error, but a candidate quoting "5 metres for Antarctica" has quoted the wrong ice sheet.

**5.10 The chapter gives three different end-of-century warming projections across the Book.** Chapter 8.1 page 1 says *"up to 6 C"*; chapter 8.3 page 61 says *"the projected **3 °C to 4 °C** increase in temperature by the end of this century"*; chapter 8.3 Fig. 3.1 reports observed warming of *"around 1.1°C since 1850–1900"* and page 49 gives *"approximately 1°C... in 2017, increasing at 0.2°C per decade"*. `[SCHOOL]` The observed figures are consistent and are the ones to quote. The projections are not, and none should be memorised as "the" projection.

**5.11 Sections 3 and 4 of Book 8 carry the same title.** Chapter 8.3 is headed SECTION 3 with the title *"EFFECTS OF CLIMATE CHANGE ON THE ENVIRONMENT, ANIMALS AND PLANTS"*, and chapter 8.4 is headed SECTION 4 with that same title. Chapter 8.1's master outline (pages 3 to 4) also lists both, at page 47 and page 73, under the identical title *"EFFECTS OF CLIMATE CHANGE ON THE ENVIRONMENT, PLANTS AND ANIMALS"*. `[SCHOOL]` Two consecutive sections with one title is a navigation problem rather than a content error, but it makes the Book hard to reference and it is worth relabelling them privately as **3 = environment** and **4 = organisms**.

**5.12 The chapter's own defence of the coral bleaching mechanism is correct, and this is worth recording positively.** Page 61 states the order as: photosynthesis in the zooxanthellae is disrupted at higher than usual temperature, they stop producing food, this affects the coral polyp's metabolism, and the polyp then **expels** the zooxanthellae. The corpus records an examiner comment attacking exactly the wrong version: *"many students think zooxanthellae first die or have their enzymes denatured due to heat and are then expelled"*, and that *"is mainly not true, as they are **expelled while still alive**. Death of corals does not lead to bleaching; rather, **prolonged coral bleaching can lead to corals dying**."* `[SCHOOL]` **The school gets this right and most candidates get it wrong.** Reproduce the school's order exactly.

**5.13 Minor typography.** Page 54: *"scientists can make **projects** about future sea level rise"*, for "projections". Page 58: *"very high temperatures (at least 5 oC **above from** average)"*. Page 62: *"the temperature at which the corals **becomes** stressed"*. Page 70: *"A positive feedback loop... **exist**"*. Page 60: *"Corals grow in warm, clear shallow waters"* is followed on the same page by *"most coral species live in waters **close to the warmest temperature they can tolerate**"*, which is the load-bearing half and is easy to skip past.

Beyond these, the chapter is factually sound. The floating-ice displacement argument, the thermal-expansion-as-main-cause claim, the ice-albedo feedback, the three ecological roles of sea ice, the mangrove and seagrass stressor lists, the ectotherm-migration argument and the permafrost feedback are all internally consistent and correct.

### 6. Load-bearing versus skippable

**Chapter 8.3 is the second-highest-yield chapter in Book 8 and it is the one whose structure you can revise straight off the outcome.** LO (b) names eight effects and the chapter supplies one section per effect. LO (c) names mangrove mitigation and the chapter supplies section 3.2. In the question bank, the concept heading covering this chapter's material is **the largest of the eight, at 165 of 517 records**, and mangrove mitigation is tested on the **9477 specimen** directly.

**Load-bearing:**

- **All eight of LO (b)'s named effects, each reducible to a two-link or three-link causal chain.** The examinable unit is the chain, not the page. Written out, and these are the eight answers:
  1. **Melting of polar ice caps**, page 51: higher air and sea temperature melts ice sheets on land, adding water to the ocean, so sea level rises; melting sea ice does **not** raise sea level because floating ice already displaces its own mass.
  2. **Rising sea levels**, page 53: two causes, **thermal expansion of seawater as the main cause**, plus meltwater from continental ice. Consequences are coastal flooding and erosion, and **saltwater intrusion** into groundwater and irrigation.
  3. **Increase in frequency of extreme weather events**, pages 58 to 59: a warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, so precipitation becomes more intense where it falls and evaporation dries the areas where it does not; wet areas get wetter and dry areas drier.
  4. **Stress on freshwater supplies**, page 56: glacier melt gives a temporary increase then a permanent decrease; higher evaporation redistributes rainfall; heavy rain runs off faster than it can be stored and carries pollutants; sea level rise drives saltwater intrusion.
  5. **Stress to coral reef ecosystems**, pages 61 to 62: bleaching from thermal stress plus ocean acidification. Both mechanisms, in full, below.
  6. **Stress to seagrass and mangrove ecosystems**, pages 66 to 67: mangroves stressed by flooding duration and salinity beyond a species-specific physiological threshold, by extreme-weather uprooting and defoliation, by soil salinisation from lower precipitation, and by higher temperature at latitudinal limits; seagrasses stressed by thermal limits, by acidification impairing photosynthesis, and by changing depth, light availability and substrate.
  7. **Migration of fishes and insects**, pages 68 to 69: both groups are **ectotherms**, so their distribution is set by ambient temperature; both shift **poleward or to higher altitude, or in fish also to deeper cooler water**, to stay within their temperature tolerance range.
  8. **Release of greenhouse gases from frozen organic matter**, page 70: permafrost thaws, microbes decompose the previously frozen organic matter, releasing **CO2 and CH4**, which warms the atmosphere further and thaws more permafrost.
- **The two positive feedback loops, page 52 and page 70, stated as loops.** Ice-albedo: melting exposes dark water, albedo falls, absorption rises, warming rises, more ice melts. Permafrost: warming thaws permafrost, decomposition releases greenhouse gases, warming rises, more permafrost thaws. **9477 Specimen P1 Q28** hands a feedback web containing both and asks which arrow **reduces** warming, so the candidate must also be able to identify the **negative** feedback in the same diagram, which is increased evaporation giving more summer cloud. `[OFFICIAL]` The bank confirms this is a recurring MCQ shape, with four separate prelim MCQs on positive-feedback identification, one of which is reprinted across four different schools' papers `[SCHOOL]`.
- **Why melting sea ice does not raise sea level, page 51.** One sentence, and it is the most reliable distractor-killer in the chapter. Floating ice already displaces a volume of water equal to its own mass.
- **The coral–zooxanthellae symbiosis, page 61, both directions.** Zooxanthellae supply glucose, glycerol and amino acids from photosynthesis, providing most of the coral's nutrition. Coral supplies inorganic nitrogen and phosphorus from its metabolic waste, and shelter including protection from ultraviolet radiation.
- **The coral bleaching mechanism in the school's own order, page 61.** Higher than usual temperature disrupts photosynthesis in the zooxanthellae; they stop producing food; the coral's metabolism is affected; the coral **expels the zooxanthellae alive**; the white calcium carbonate skeleton shows through; without the algal photosynthate the coral starves and, if the stress persists, dies. And the recovery clause: bleached coral **can** recover if the water cools, because zooxanthellae repopulate the host cells. See §5.12 for why the order matters.
- **The ocean acidification mechanism, in the scheme's version rather than the school's, page 62 plus §5.6 above.** CO2 dissolves, forms carbonic acid, which dissociates releasing H⁺; H⁺ reacts with carbonate ions to form hydrogencarbonate, lowering free carbonate concentration; corals need carbonate to deposit CaCO3, so calcification slows and existing skeletons dissolve.
- **The 1 °C to 2 °C thermal margin, page 62.** *"Coral reefs generally occur in areas where average water temperature is only 1 °C to 2 °C below the temperature at which the corals becomes stressed and undergo bleaching."* This one sentence explains why a small warming produces a large biological effect, and it is transferable to every narrow-tolerance organism in chapters 8.4 and 8.6.
- **The whole of section 3.2, pages 71 to 72, for LO (c).** The blue-carbon burial mechanism in four steps: dense canopies and woody biomass capture carbon above ground; roots slow tidal water and trap carbon-rich sediment; fallen leaves and decaying roots accumulate in those sediment layers; the soils are **waterlogged and oxygen-poor, which slows decomposition**, so the carbon stays locked away for centuries. **9477 Specimen P1 Q27 is exactly this mechanism**, and the correct option is the one naming *"oxygen-poor sediments that limit further decomposition"*. `[OFFICIAL]` The three wrong options are worth studying as a set, because each encodes a real misconception: that trees take up **organic** carbon through roots rather than fixing atmospheric CO2 by photosynthesis; that mangrove litter is swept to the deep ocean rather than retained in situ; and that mangroves slow the **rate of sea level rise** rather than buffering its consequences.
- **The climate-resilience half of LO (c), page 72.** Wave height reduced by more than 66%; interlocking roots interrupt storm surge; roots stabilise shorelines and trap sediment so shorelines rebuild after storms; roughly 15 million people shielded from flooding and over $65 billion in property damage prevented each year. The distinction the specimen tests is that this is **adaptation to consequences**, while blue carbon is **mitigation of the cause**. Both are in LO (c)'s scope because the outcome says *"mitigate the impacts"*, not "mitigate warming".

### 6. Load-bearing versus skippable

**Chapter 8.4 is the chapter where "named case studies and real data are load-bearing" is most literally true.** LO (f) says **discuss**, and LO (h) says **explain**, and both are application outcomes: the examiner supplies an unfamiliar organism and expects the candidate to run the general mechanism on it. **Load-bearing:**

- **The definition of stress and its two causes, page 76.** *"any environmental change that acts to reduce the fitness of organisms"*, with abiotic causes (temperature, climatic factors, chemical components) and biotic causes (competition, predation, parasitism). LO (f) is built on the phrase **increased environmental stress**, so this is the outcome's own opening move. The word **fitness** is doing the work: it is what links climate to Core Idea 4.
- **The four features of the observed warming trend, page 76.** Warming has roughly doubled in rate over the century; it is **spatially uneven, with the greatest warming in the polar regions and the Arctic warming twice as fast as elsewhere**; it is **seasonally uneven, greatest in winter**; and the **diurnal range has narrowed because minimum temperatures are rising at about twice the rate of maximum temperatures**. Those four are the reason every downstream effect in this chapter is asymmetric, and the last one is quietly the most useful, because it explains why **night-time warming** drives the rice-yield result in chapter 8.5 and the overwintering results in section 4.2.
- **The endotherm and ectotherm distinction, page 79, as a mechanism.** Endotherms control core temperature by internal heat production; ectotherms depend on the environment. Everything about insect and fish responses to warming follows from ectothermy, and everything about body-size shrinkage follows from endothermy.
- **The body-size argument, page 79, in full.** Larger body size reduces the **surface area to volume ratio**, so it reduces heat loss, so mass-specific metabolic rate falls with increasing body size; therefore average body size increases with decreasing mean annual temperature and increasing latitude; therefore warming has been accompanied by a **documented decrease in average body size across bird and mammal species**, because smaller size is more energetically efficient in a warmer climate. `[OFFICIAL]` **The 9477 syllabus's own recommended reading list for Climate Change names Sheridan and Bickford, 2011, "Shrinking body size as an ecological response to climate change", *Nature Climate Change*.** That is the syllabus pointing directly at this section, and it is the strongest single signal in the whole Book that a specific school section is examinable.
- **Phenology, page 80, definition and mechanism.** The timing of seasonal activities; spring activities have advanced since the 1960s; the mechanism is that different species use **different environmental cues**, so warming shifts some and not others. The word **phenology** returns zero syllabus hits, but the biology is carried by LO (f)'s *"food chains"* clause. See the scope note below.
- **The trophic mismatch mechanism and the caribou data, pages 83 to 84.** This is the highest-value named example in Book 8 and it is worth reproducing in full because it is the model answer to any LO (f) food-chain question:
  - Caribou reproduction is cued by **day length, which does not vary between years**.
  - The onset of the plant growing season is cued by **mean spring temperature, which rose by 4.6 °C over the study period**.
  - Result: the onset of the plant growing season **advanced by 14.8 days** while the onset of caribou reproduction **advanced by only 1.28 days**.
  - Consequence: **calf mortality rose** as the mismatch grew, and **annual calf production fell, varying fourfold** between the lowest and highest levels of mismatch.
  - The general principle: successful reproduction depends on synchronising offspring production with the peak of resource quality, and **plant nutritional content and digestibility peak soon after emergence and decline rapidly thereafter**, so a two-week shift is enough to break the match.
  **The examinable idea is that a mismatch arises because two interacting species use different cues, one of which tracks temperature and one of which does not.** That transfers to any predator-prey or plant-pollinator pair the examiner invents.
- **The niche-competition mechanism and the Arctic fox data, page 85.** Warming replaces tundra with boreal forest, which is unsuitable for the Arctic fox; its lemming and vole prey decline in both regularity and number as winters shorten and soften; and the red fox, **a superior hunter**, encroaches northward into its range. Two mechanisms in one example, **competition and a changed predator-prey relationship**, which is exactly the pair LO (f) asks about under *"niche occupation"*.
- **Geographical distribution, page 81, as a rule.** Temperature falls with increasing latitude and with increasing altitude; a species' **upper altitudinal and poleward latitudinal boundaries are set by minimum temperatures**; warming moves those minimum-temperature isotherms upward and poleward; species track them **to the extent that dispersal and other environmental factors allow**. That last clause is the one that generates the "and if they cannot" answers in chapter 8.5.
- **The two range-shift named examples with their numbers, pages 81 to 82.** Santa Rosa Mountains: a 0.4 °C warming over thirty years, and the **average elevation of the dominant plant species rose by 65 m**, with all but one of ten species shifting upslope. Northern Alaska: repeat photography from 1948 to 2001 shows **alder, willow and dwarf birch expanding northward into tundra**, corroborated across Canada, Scandinavia and Russia.
- **The whole of LO (h)'s two-mechanism structure, pages 86, 93 and 94.** This is the outcome stated as a pair and the chapter answers each half:
  - **Increased metabolism.** Insects are poikilothermic and ectothermic, so body temperature follows ambient temperature; **within certain limits, the higher the temperature, the faster metabolic reactions proceed**, because enzyme-catalysed reaction rates rise. Metabolic rate is measured by **oxygen consumption or carbon dioxide production**. The lesser grain-borer's CO2 production rises roughly linearly to 30 °C. High metabolic rate then gives **rapid growth and shorter generation times as long as food is abundant**.
  - **Narrow temperature tolerance.** There is an optimal temperature or narrow tolerance for development; **outside those limits the rate of development falls sharply because extreme temperatures damage the enzymes that regulate metabolism**; and beyond the upper and lower **lethal limits**, death occurs. The pea weevil's development time reaches a **minimum at about 30 °C and rises again above it**, with a pupa taking **5 days at 30 °C and almost 15 days at 40 °C**. The pine false webworm's eggs **fail to hatch below 5.8 °C and above 30.3 °C**.
  **The enzyme sentence on page 86 is the load-bearing bridge to Core Idea 1's LO (q) on the effect of temperature on enzyme-catalysed reaction rate, and it is the sentence that makes this a biology outcome rather than an ecology one.**
- **The five effects of warming on insects, section 4.2.1, as a five-item list with one mechanism each.** A, extended geographical ranges, *"however, ranges could be diminished if conditions exceed the upper lethal limit or become too dry"*. B, increased overwintering success, because warming raises **winter** temperatures more than summer ones and **shortens winter by up to 3 weeks**. C, increased population growth rate, more annual generations and longer development seasons, via earlier emergence from winter dormancy and shorter life cycles. D, changed synchrony in pest-host relations, because heat-stressed host plants become more vulnerable to outbreaks. E, increased insect migration poleward to stay within temperature tolerance ranges. **This five-item list is the skeleton of any LO (h) extended answer.**
- **The three best-quantified insect results, because they are what a data question looks like.** Svalbard aphid: a **2.8 °C** elevation gave a dramatic rise in reproductive output and an **11-fold increase in overwintering eggs**. Migratory Lepidoptera: **an increase of 14 species for each 1 °C** rise in southwest Europe. Southern pine beetle, Table 4.1: two independent models predicting outbreak risk increases of **396% and 508%**, with the counter-intuitive value row explained in §5.7.
- **The honeybee and small white result, page 89**, because it is the only insect **phenology** example in the printed notes and phenology is LO (f) territory: both *Apis mellifera* and *Pieris rapae* appeared **earlier in spring as mean February-to-April temperature increased**, consistently since the 1970s.

### 6. Load-bearing versus skippable

**Chapter 8.5 is the chapter with the widest gap between what it teaches and what its two outcomes ask for.** LO (e) and LO (g) are both **discuss** outcomes, the only two in Book 8 alongside LO (f), and discuss outcomes reward a structured argument with named evidence. The chapter supplies the evidence but buries it in general environmental-science exposition. In the question bank the two concept headings covering this chapter are **the smallest and third-smallest of the eight**, at 44 records for food supply and 13 for tropical biodiversity and biomedicines, out of 517. **That is a real finding and it cuts both ways: the corpus under-tests this chapter, and the corpus is a prelim corpus, so it may simply be that these outcomes are hard to set as short structured questions and turn up as essays instead.**

**Load-bearing:**

- **The three levels of biodiversity with one climate impact each, pages 99 to 106.** This is the chapter's spine and the page 98 concept map states it in three columns:
  - **Genetic diversity**, the variation in DNA composition among individuals within a species. Climate change reduces it because **directional selection and rapid range shifts shrink populations, so gene pools shrink**. Members that cannot adapt perish.
  - **Species diversity**, the number or variety of species in a region, with **richness** and **evenness** as its two components. Climate change reduces it through **phenological mismatch between plants and pollinators**, and through **reduction in the amount and availability of habitat**.
  - **Ecosystem diversity**, the number and variety of ecosystems. Climate change alters it by shifting **biome integrity**: Amazonian rainforest replaced by tropical savannah, tundra and alpine communities replaced by expanding boreal and alpine forest.
- **The genetic-diversity argument in full, page 100, because it is the bridge to Core Idea 4.** Genetic diversity *"provides the raw material for adaptation to local conditions"*; populations with little of it *"may happen to lack genetic variants that would help them adapt to novel conditions"*; low diversity also raises vulnerability to disease and to inbreeding depression. **This is 9477 LO 4(a) and 4(b) applied to a climate context, and it is the reason LO (g) is a biology outcome.**
- **Why the tropics specifically, page 101 and chapter 8.1 page 16.** Most of the world's biodiversity concentrations are near the equator, especially tropical rainforests and coral reefs; only 10% to 15% of all species live in North America and Europe; the Malaysian Peninsula has at least 8000 flowering plant species against Britain's 1400 in twice the area. LO (g) names the tropics and these are the numbers that justify the word "rich".
- **The tropical-ectotherm vulnerability sentence, page 105.** *"Climate change is likely to have a particularly large impact on tropical ectotherms, even taking into account behavioural thermoregulation, because they are relatively sensitive to temperature change and are **living very close to their optimal temperature**."* This is the mechanistic answer to why tropical biodiversity is more at risk than temperate biodiversity, and the question bank confirms it is tested and poorly answered: an examiner comment records that *"a handful of students mentioned that the **temperature tolerance range of species is narrower in tropical regions**"* and that most did not `[SCHOOL]`. It is also the same argument as chapter 8.3's coral thermal margin and chapter 8.4's insect tolerance limits. **One mechanism, three chapters.**
- **Loss of biomedicines, pages 106 to 108, with at least two named examples and one loss.** The rosy periwinkle from Madagascar producing **vinblastine and vincristine**, anticancer alkaloids treating Hodgkin's disease and a fatal leukaemia. **AZT** for HIV-AIDS, patterned after compounds from the marine sponge *Cryptotethya crypta*. The **gastric brooding frogs** *Rheobatrachus vitellinus* and *R. silus*, whose young neutralised their mother's stomach acid and which went extinct in the 1980s, taking a possible ulcer treatment with them. Plus the scale claim: **the value to society of as-yet-undiscovered drugs from tropical rainforests is estimated at around US$150 billion**, and chapter 8.1's **25% of all prescription drugs originally derived from tropical plants**. That set is enough for any LO (g) biomedicine question.
- **Loss of food security, pages 109 to 110, in its four-step argument.** Globally **90% of food comes from just 15 crop species and 8 livestock species**; monocultures are susceptible to pest attack; **wild relatives and crop ancestors carry natural genetic resistance because they have faced changing conditions and only the fittest survived, while modern varieties were selected for productivity rather than resistance and have lost those genes**; therefore wild relatives are **biological insurance**, and helpful genes can be transferred by crossbreeding or genetic engineering. And the closing link that makes it LO (g): **most of the wild relatives of our crop plants live in the tropics**, which are the part most threatened by climate change. **This four-step chain is the single best-constructed argument in Book 8.**
- **Food security's full definition and four dimensions, page 111.** Availability, accessibility, utilisation, stability. LO (e) says *"consequences to the **sustainable food supply**"*, and these four give the structure for a discuss answer. Plus the definition of **sustainability**: *"The ability to use resources without using them up faster than they are renewed."*
- **The direct against indirect framework for agro-ecosystems, page 113.** Direct impacts are caused by a modification of **abiotic factors**: rising mean, maximum and minimum temperatures; changes in the frequency, duration and intensity of dry spells and droughts; changes in the timing, duration, intensity and location of rain and snowfall; more frequent and intense storms and floods; greater seasonal variability and shifted growing seasons. Indirect impacts act **through other species**: pollinators, pests, disease vectors, invasive species. **This is the same direct-against-indirect distinction as LO (a)'s in chapter 8.2, reapplied, and it is the cleanest way to structure an LO (e) answer.**
- **The rice heat-stress result, pages 114, with its numbers.** Rice is highly susceptible to heat stress **during the reproductive and ripening stages**; extremely high temperatures **even for a few hours during flowering can cause complete sterility**; high temperatures during ripening reduce grain filling and milling quality; and **higher night-time minimum temperatures reduce yield by as much as 5% for every 0.5 °C increase, while maximum daytime temperatures have little impact**. The 2003 Yangtze River Valley loss was **5.18 million tons across about 3 million hectares**. This is the only fully quantified crop result in the chapter and it connects straight back to chapter 8.4's finding that **minimum temperatures are rising at about twice the rate of maximum temperatures**. **Those two facts together are a complete exam answer and neither chapter joins them up.**
- **The three indirect crop impacts, page 117.** Pests: warming expands the range of agricultural pests and **reduces winter mortality of aphids, enabling earlier and larger dispersion**; aphids and weevil larvae also respond positively to higher CO2. Weeds: many thrive under higher temperature, rainfall and CO2, and compete for light, water and nutrients. Pathogens: the aflatoxin case, with *Aspergillus* contamination of maize driven by changing rainfall patterns, and 125 food-related deaths in Kenya in 2004.
- **Livestock impacts, page 118, three mechanisms.** Animal productivity: warming **alters heat exchange between animal and environment**, so feed intake, growth, reproduction, maintenance, mortality and production are all affected; the thermal comfort zone for temperate adult cattle is **5 °C to 15 °C**; heat stress induces behavioural and metabolic changes including **reduced feed intake and therefore reduced productivity**, with an estimated milk loss of up to **5.5 kg per cow per day** in southern Europe and a **60% increase in cattle mortality in Italy in 2010**. Feed and forage: higher temperature and lower precipitation cut yields, and **increased temperature increases lignification of plant tissues, reducing digestibility and degradation rate**, so nutrient availability falls even where biomass rises. Animal health: **rising temperatures increase winter survival of vectors and pathogens**.
- **Fisheries impacts, pages 120 to 121, two mechanisms plus a redistribution.** Most aquatic animals are **ectotherms**, so metabolic rate is strongly affected by temperature and available oxygen. Temperature changes affect **reproductive cycles, growth rate, age at sexual maturity and spawning timing**. Reduced oxygen availability from surface warming **decreases maximum body weight of fish species globally**, lowering catch potential, and hypoxia-intolerant species such as tuna lose habitat. And the redistribution: fish are migrating poleward, giving **an average 30% to 70% increase in catch potential in high-latitude regions and a drop of up to 40% in the tropics**. **That last pair of figures is the most examinable number in the chapter for LO (e), because it converts a biological mechanism into a food-security consequence with a tropical bias, which is exactly what LO (e) and LO (g) together ask for.**
- **The bluetongue mechanism, page 119, but only the mechanism.** *Culicoides* midges are sensitive to water and temperature; warming extended the vector's range northward; the extension came with **longer seasonal vector activity**, which increased **virus persistence through winter** and raised the risk of transmission by indigenous European midge species; the result was BTV-8 spreading across northern and western Europe in 2006 and 2007. **This is LO (i)'s poleward-vector argument demonstrated on a non-mosquito vector and a livestock host, and it is a strong transferable precedent.**

**Context:**

- **Section 5.1.2's opening two pages, 102 to 103**, and Figs. 5.3 and 5.4. Generic environmental-science framing about biodiversity being a global challenge for humankind. The one load-bearing sentence is the **two-way relationship** on page 102 and the **fitness-decrease-to-extinction** ladder on page 103. Everything else is preamble.

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**This is the cleanest chapter in Book 8.** Twelve pages produced four findings, and two of them are currency rather than error.

**5.1 Malaria is said to be caused by one of four *Plasmodium* species, and there are five that infect humans.** Page 133: *"It is caused by **one of four species of the Plasmodium parasite** transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes."* The four classical species are *P. falciparum*, *P. vivax*, *P. ovale* and *P. malariae*. **A fifth, *Plasmodium knowlesi*, has been recognised as a human pathogen since 2004 and is the dominant cause of human malaria in parts of Malaysian Borneo**, which makes it a Southeast Asian omission in a Singapore syllabus. `[INFERRED]` Not likely to be penalised, since most textbooks still print four, but worth knowing.

**5.2 The claim that 10 °C kills Aedes eggs is not supported by the chapter's own data and sits oddly beside its own desiccation-resistance claim.** Page 133: *"**Lower temperatures at or below 10 C kill Aedes eggs, larvae and adults.**"* Page 128 states that *"their eggs can survive dry conditions for several months"*, and section 6.2.3's own study reports that *"Eggs of Ae. aegypti **hatched across all tested temperatures ranging from 15 °C to 40 °C**"*, meaning the study never tested below 15 °C and so cannot support a claim about 10 °C. `[SCHOOL]` The chapter's other 10 °C claims are about **transmission and adult survival** and are consistent: page 127's *"winter temperature is no colder than 10°C"* and page 133's *"when temperatures exceed 10 °C, the transmission of dengue by Aedes mosquitoes occurs"*. **Quote the 10 °C figure as the limit for transmission and adult survival, not for eggs**, which are the desiccation-resistant and cold-tolerant stage.

**5.3 Section 6.2.3 states that the life cycle completes between 15 °C and 35 °C and then reports egg hatching up to 40 °C, one line apart, without reconciling them.** Page 130: *"Ae. aegypti **completed its life cycle between 15 °C and 35 °C**"*, then *"Eggs of Ae. aegypti **hatched across all tested temperatures ranging from 15 °C to 40 °C**"*, then *"the lowest larval survival rate was observed at 40 °C, where **larvae failed to survive beyond the first instar**"*. `[INFERRED]` These are reconcilable and the reconciliation is the point of the study: at 40 °C the eggs still hatch but the larvae die, so the **life cycle** does not complete even though the **first stage** succeeds. **That is exactly the sort of two-step reading a data question rewards, and the chapter does not spell it out.** The same logic explains why 35 °C shows the lowest pupal survival while 40 °C does not appear in the pupal panel at all: there are no pupae at 40 °C.

**5.4 Minor typography.** Page 132: *"In the **Unites** States"*. Page 129 uses filled-square bullet glyphs that extract as empty boxes, so the four life-cycle stages lose their numbering in any text extraction. Page 124's concept map has the same "ed" fragment problem as chapter 8.4's, where increase and decrease arrows are lost.

Beyond these, the chapter is factually sound and internally consistent. The vector definition, the blood-meal mechanism, the ectotherm sensitivity claim, the four-stage life cycle with its aquatic and terrestrial phases, the temperature thresholds, the humidity argument, the non-monotonic rainfall argument and the extrinsic-incubation shortening argument are all correct.

### 6. Load-bearing versus skippable

**Chapter 8.6 has the best outcome-to-page ratio in Book 8. Twelve pages carrying one and a half outcomes, with almost no padding.** It is also the chapter with the clearest school-supplied scope marking anywhere in the Book.

**The school marks its own out-of-scope content, and that is worth recording.** The page 124 concept map labels the entire DENV branch, meaning the virus genome, the single-stranded positive-sense RNA, the three structural and seven non-structural genes, and the four serotypes DENV-1 to DENV-4, with the words **"FYI: not in 9477 syllabus"**. `[SCHOOL]` The map also labels the host-response branch, meaning viremia, keratinocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, Langerhans cells and monocytes, innate and adaptive immunity, and the acute, critical and recovery phases, with **"KIV: ETA (Infectious Diseases)"**, which is the school saying that material belongs to Extension Topic A rather than to Book 8. Both markings are correct and both should be acted on.

**Load-bearing:**

- **The four life-cycle stages of *Aedes aegypti* with their phase, page 128.** Egg, larva, pupa, adult. **Aquatic phase: larva and pupa. Terrestrial phase: egg and adult.** LO (h) names the life cycle explicitly, so this is one of only two places in Book 8 where the syllabus asks for a named organism's biology by name.
- **The stage-by-stage detail that a question can turn on, pages 128 to 129.** Eggs are laid **above the waterline on the inner wet walls of water-holding containers**, and **survive dry conditions for months**, which is why elimination is so hard. Larvae **hatch when water inundates the eggs** after rain or human water addition, feed on microorganisms and particulate organic matter, and pass through **four instars**, moulting three times. The **fourth instar triggers metamorphosis into the pupa**, which is aquatic and **does not feed**. The adult emerges after about two days by breaking the pupal skin and is terrestrial. **The whole cycle takes roughly 8 to 10 days at room temperature.**
- **Fig. 6.8's three-panel temperature result, page 130, in full.** This is LO (h)'s Aedes clause and it is the only place the school quantifies it.
  - **Hatching:** eggs hatched at every temperature from 15 °C to 40 °C, with a **significantly higher rate at 15–30 °C than at 35 and 40 °C**, and an **optimum at 25 °C**.
  - **Larval survival:** **highest at 25 °C**, **lowest at 40 °C where larvae failed to survive beyond the first instar**. **Development time shortened dramatically at warmer temperatures: 7 days at 35 °C against 39 days at 15 °C.**
  - **Pupal survival:** **highest at 25 °C, lowest at 35 °C.**
  - **The significance, stated by the school:** warmer conditions **accelerate the entire life cycle, potentially giving more generations in a season**, and while extreme heat kills mosquitoes, **moderate warmth boosts populations and accelerates disease transmission**.
  **The shape of this result is what matters and it is the same shape as chapter 8.4's pea weevil: an optimum with a fall-off on both sides, not a monotonic increase.** A candidate who writes "warmer means more mosquitoes" has missed the outcome, because LO (h) says **narrow temperature tolerance**.
- **The four effects of increased temperature on mosquito vectors, page 133.** This is LO (i)'s core and it is a four-item list:
  1. **Increased mosquito activity.**
  2. **Increased frequency of mosquito bites.** *"Female mosquitoes bite more frequently at higher temperatures. This increase in feeding rate will lead to a greater capacity to produce offspring."*
  3. **Improved survival of eggs and adult mosquitoes**, within the survivable range, with **warmer temperatures increasing feeding rate and reproduction**, and **a rise in water temperature reducing the time taken for larvae to mature**. Bounded below by roughly 10 °C and above by roughly 34 °C.
  4. **Expanded geographic range in both latitude and altitude.** *"When temperatures exceed 16 C, the transmission of malaria by Anopheles mosquitoes occurs. Similarly, when temperatures exceed 10 °C, the transmission of dengue by Aedes mosquitoes occurs. This favor winters that will enable mosquitoes to survive in areas that were formerly too cold."*
- **The pathogen-side mechanism, page 133, which is the half most answers omit.** Warming does not only act on the vector, it acts on the pathogen inside the vector. *"For dengue, **the time taken for the virus inside the mosquito to develop and become infective is shorter**. This results in a faster viral replication rate leading to the mosquitoes becoming dangerous to humans more rapidly."* And for malaria: *"The increased temperatures allow **malarial parasites to mature more quickly** and mosquitoes can transmit the disease faster."* **This is the extrinsic incubation period, and shortening it means a larger fraction of a mosquito's life is spent infective. Vector effect plus pathogen effect is a two-mark structure where most candidates write one.**
- **The three named climate drivers and their directions, page 132.** *"vector abundance and disease incidence rise measurably with increases in **temperature, precipitation (i.e. rainfall) and humidity**"*, plus **wind and daylight duration** as secondary factors. And the humidity mechanism: *"an increase in humidity will increase vector survival"*.
- **The non-monotonic rainfall argument, page 132, because it is the chapter's one piece of genuine nuance.** *"Not all conditions of rainfall favor mosquitoes' survival and their reproduction. **Torrential rainfall and flooding may have negative effects** on increasing the density of their population because this causes runoff water and can also destroy the eggs and larvae of mosquitoes. Conversely, **lower rainfall can create habitat by causing rivers to dry into pools resulting in dry season malaria. Lower rainfall can also increase container-breeding mosquitoes by forcing increased water storage.**"* **Both extremes of rainfall can raise transmission, by different routes. That is an evaluate-level point and it is the answer to any question asking whether more rain always means more dengue.**
- **The distribution limits of *Ae. aegypti*, page 127.** **35°N to 35°S; winter temperature no colder than 10 °C; typically not above 1000 m altitude.** LO (i) says **beyond the tropics**, and these three numbers are what "beyond" is measured against. Warming pushes all three boundaries outward.
- **The people-move-the-virus point, page 132.** *"most female Ae. aegypti may spend their lifetime in or around the houses where they emerge as adults. This means that **people, rather than mosquitoes, rapidly move the virus** within and between communities."* This is the non-climate driver, and it is what stops a candidate from attributing all spread to warming. The corpus rewards it: a prelim scheme's model answer for the global dengue distribution includes *"potential spread into neighbouring regions... due to **warming temperatures and human movement introducing dengue viruses into non-endemic zones**"* `[SCHOOL]`.
- **The closing summary, page 133, as a written conclusion.** *"for the mosquito vectors, global warming have **shortened the lifecycle period**, which in turn **increases mosquito numbers**. Global warming has also **increase the speed of development of adult mosquitoes**, which in turn **increase disease transmission rates**. These favour the **resurgence and the redistribution** of diseases such as malaria and dengue fever worldwide."* Ungrammatical as printed, but it is the outcome's own shape in four links.
- **Vector-borne disease and vector defined, pages 125 to 126, and the blood-meal mechanism.** *"bloodsucking insects, which **ingest disease-producing microorganisms during a blood meal** from an infected host and later **inject it into a new host during their subsequent blood meal**."* Plus the one-line reason the whole outcome exists: *"**Arthropod vectors are ectotherms and thus especially sensitive to climatic factors.**"*

**Context, and the school marks most of it itself:**

### 5. Three errors that sit directly on examined mechanisms

Most of the findings above are numerical or typographical. **Three are mechanism errors, and all three sit where the outcome is:**

1. **Ocean acidification, chapter 8.3 page 62.** The school says corals *"cannot absorb the calcium carbonate they need"*. Corals do not absorb calcium carbonate; they deposit it. The mark schemes give the correct chain through **H⁺ reacting with carbonate ions, lowering free carbonate concentration, so calcification slows**. **This is the one place in Book 8 where the mark scheme is demonstrably better than the notes, and it is on a chain LO (b) requires.**
2. **The greenhouse mechanism gloss, chapter 8.1 page 23.** *"absorb (i.e. retain)"* loses the re-emission step, which is the whole mechanism. The chapter's own page 24 corrects it. A prelim scheme makes the correct version a **compulsory point**.
3. **"The pH of the ocean is about 30% more acidic", chapter 8.3 page 62.** pH is logarithmic. What rose by 30% is the **hydrogen ion concentration**; the pH fell about 0.1 units. A candidate who writes "the pH fell by 30%" writes something false.

Two further errors are terminological rather than mechanistic but would be policed: **nematocysts named as the tentacles** (8.3 p.60) rather than as the stinging structures they bear, and **a colony "excreting" its exoskeleton** (8.3 p.60) where secretion is meant, which the same chapter gets right two pages later.

**And one place where the school is right and most candidates are wrong, which is worth as much as any error finding.** Chapter 8.3 page 61 states the bleaching sequence correctly: photosynthesis is disrupted, the algae stop supplying food, the coral's metabolism is affected, and the coral **expels the zooxanthellae alive**. The corpus records an examiner attacking the reverse: *"many students think zooxanthellae first die or have their enzymes denatured due to heat and are then expelled"*, and that *"is mainly not true, as they are expelled while still alive."* `[SCHOOL]` **Reproduce the school's order exactly.**
