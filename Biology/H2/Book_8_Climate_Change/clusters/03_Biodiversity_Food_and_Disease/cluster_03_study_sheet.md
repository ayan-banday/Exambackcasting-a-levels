## A. Biodiversity has three levels, and climate change reaches each by a different route

**A1. The three levels, defined.** Biodiversity is the **variety of life across all levels of biological
organisation**, and it decomposes into three. **Species diversity is the number or variety of species in
a region**, with two components: **species richness**, the number of different species present, and
**species evenness**, how close in numbers each species is. **Genetic diversity is the variation in DNA
composition among individuals within a species.** **Ecosystem diversity is the number and variety of
ecosystems.**

**A2. Genetic diversity falls because populations shrink.** `[Core Idea bridge → CI 4 LOs a, b, e]`
Climate change reduces it through **directional selection and rapid migration**. The chain: members that
**cannot adapt perish** → **population size falls** → **gene pools become smaller** → genetic diversity
falls. Why that matters is the outcome's whole point: genetic diversity **provides the raw material for
adaptation to local conditions**, and a population with little of it **may lack the variants that would
help it adapt to novel conditions**. Low diversity also raises **disease vulnerability** and
**inbreeding depression**, where genetically similar parents produce weak offspring.

**A3. Species diversity falls by two named mechanisms.** **Phenological shifts in flowering plants and
insect pollinators** cause **mismatches** that can drive **both partners to extinction**. And
**reduction in the amount and availability of habitat** removes species directly. The named case is the
**golden toad**, extinct at Monteverde in Costa Rica, lost to climate change plus **an introduced
chytrid fungus whose spread warming favoured**.

**A4. Ecosystem diversity changes because biomes move.** Warming alters **biome integrity**: **Amazonian
rainforest replaced by tropical savannah**, and **tundra replaced by expanding boreal and alpine
forest**.

**A5. The ladder runs from fitness to extinction.** The notes frame every impact as a **fitness
decrease**, of which **species extinction is the most extreme and irreversible form**. That word is the
one LO (f) uses to define stress, which is why the two outcomes share a mechanism.

## B. LO (g): the tropics specifically, and the two consequences it names

**B1. Why the outcome says tropics and not biodiversity.** Most biodiversity concentrations are **near
the equator**, especially **tropical rainforests and coral reefs**. Only **10% to 15% of all species
live in North America and Europe**; **tropical rainforests hold at least 50% of known terrestrial
species**; and **about 25% of all prescription drugs were originally derived from tropical plants**.
Those numbers justify the word rich.

**B2. Tropical species are vulnerable because they already sit at their optimum.** The notes say climate
change has a particularly large impact on **tropical ectotherms**, because they are **relatively
sensitive to temperature change and living very close to their optimal temperature**. A given warming
therefore pushes **a larger fraction of tropical species past their optimum**, while temperate species
have wider margins. An examiner comment records that only a handful of candidates mentioned **the
temperature tolerance range being narrower in tropical regions**. The wrong version, that the tropics
warm more, is backwards: **the tropics warm less than the poles**.

**B3. Loss of biomedicines, with the named examples.** The **rosy periwinkle** from Madagascar produces
**vinblastine and vincristine**, treating **Hodgkin's disease and a fatal leukaemia**. **AZT**, used
against HIV and AIDS, was **patterned after compounds from the marine sponge *Cryptotethya crypta***.
The **gastric brooding frogs *Rheobatrachus vitellinus* and *R. silus***, whose young **neutralised the
mother's stomach acid**, went **extinct in the 1980s**, taking a possible ulcer treatment with them.
Undiscovered tropical drugs are valued at about **US$150 billion**.

**B4. Genetic diversity for food, in four steps.** This is the best-built argument in the Book.

1. **90% of our food comes from just 15 crop species and 8 livestock species**, so a failure in any one
   is costly.
2. **Monocultures are susceptible to pest attack.** A **monoculture** is the practice of growing a
   **single crop or livestock variety in a farming system at a time**.
3. **Wild relatives possess natural genetic resistance**, because wild populations **face constantly
   changing conditions such as disease, drought and frost, and only the fittest survive**, while
   **modern varieties were selected for productivity rather than resistance and have lost those genes**.
4. Wild relatives are therefore **biological insurance**, and the useful genes move into crops **by
   crossbreeding or genetic engineering**.

The link that makes it LO (g): **most wild relatives of our crop plants live in the tropics**, the
region most threatened by climate change.

## C. LO (e): what environmental stress does to the food supply

**C1. Food security has four dimensions, and they structure the answer.** It exists when **all people,
at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food**. The four
dimensions are **availability, accessibility, utilisation and stability**. **Sustainability is using
resources no faster than they are renewed.**

**C2. Direct against indirect, reapplied.** **Direct impacts modify abiotic factors**: **mean, maximum
and minimum temperatures**; **dry spells and droughts**; **the timing, duration, intensity and location
of rain and snowfall**; **storms and floods**; and **shifted growing seasons**. **Indirect impacts act
through other species**: **pollinators, pests, disease vectors and invasive species**. This is LO (a)'s
distinction a second time, and it is the cleanest way to structure a discuss answer.

**C3. Rice is the quantified crop result.** It is **susceptible to
heat stress during the reproductive and ripening stages**; **a few hours of extreme heat during
flowering can cause complete sterility**; and **higher night-time minimum temperatures cut yield by as
much as 5% per 0.5 °C**, while **maximum daytime temperatures have little impact**. The **2003 Yangtze
Valley loss was 5.18 million tons over about 3 million hectares**. Now join it to chapter 8.4:
**minimum temperatures are rising about twice as fast as maxima**, so the temperature climbing fastest
is exactly the one that cuts rice yield. Neither chapter makes that join.

**C4. Three indirect crop impacts.** **Pests**: warming expands pest ranges and **reduces winter
mortality of aphids, enabling earlier and larger dispersion**. **Weeds**: they thrive under higher
temperature, rainfall and CO2 and **compete for light, water and nutrients**. **Pathogens**: changing
rainfall drove ***Aspergillus* contamination of maize** with **aflatoxin**, killing **125 people in
Kenya in 2004**.

**C5. Livestock, three mechanisms with their numbers.** **Productivity**: warming **alters heat exchange
between animal and environment**, and **heat stress cuts feed intake and therefore productivity**. The
**comfort zone for temperate adult cattle is 5 °C to 15 °C**, with **milk loss up to 5.5 kg per cow per
day** and a **60% rise in cattle mortality in Italy in 2010**. **Feed and forage**: **higher temperature
increases lignification, reducing digestibility**, so nutrient availability falls even where biomass
rises. **Animal health**: **rising temperatures increase winter survival of vectors and pathogens**.

**C6. Fisheries, two mechanisms and a redistribution.** Most aquatic animals are **ectotherms**, so
temperature affects **reproductive cycles, growth rate, age at maturity and spawning timing**. **Reduced
oxygen availability from surface warming decreases maximum body weight**, cutting catch potential, with
**hypoxia-intolerant species such as tuna losing habitat**. The redistribution is the chapter's most
examinable pair of numbers: **catch potential rises 30% to 70% at high latitudes and falls by up to 40%
in the tropics**, converting a mechanism into a food-security consequence with a tropical bias.

**C7. Bluetongue is LO (i)'s argument rehearsed on another vector.** ***Culicoides* midges are sensitive
to temperature and water**; warming **extended the vector's range northward**; and that brought
**longer seasonal vector activity**, which **increased winter virus persistence**, so **BTV-8 spread
across northern and western Europe** in 2006 and 2007. Keep the mechanism; the serotypes, restriction
zones and reassortment are virology with **zero syllabus hits**.

## D. LO (h), second half: the *Aedes aegypti* life cycle

**D1. Four stages, split across two phases.** **Egg, larva, pupa and adult**, with the **aquatic phase
being larva and pupa** and the **terrestrial phase egg and adult**. The whole cycle takes **about 8 to
10 days at room temperature**, depending on feeding.

**D2. The stage detail a question can turn on.** **Eggs are laid above the waterline** on container
walls and **survive dry conditions for several months**, which is why the mosquito is so hard to
eliminate. **Larvae hatch when water inundates the eggs**, feed on microorganisms, and pass through
**four instars**, an **instar** being a phase between two moults. **The fourth instar triggers
metamorphosis into the pupa**, which is **aquatic and does not feed**. The **adult emerges after about
two days**.

**D3. The temperature result, in three panels.** **Hatching**: eggs hatched at **every temperature from
15 °C to 40 °C**, significantly **higher at 15 to 30 °C than at 35 and 40 °C**, with an **optimum at
25 °C**. **Larval survival**: **highest at 25 °C**, **lowest at 40 °C, where larvae failed to survive
beyond the first instar**, and development shortened from **39 days at 15 °C to 7 days at 35 °C**.
**Pupal survival**: **highest at 25 °C, lowest at 35 °C**.

**D4. The two-step reading the chapter leaves implicit.** The notes say the mosquito **completed its
life cycle between 15 °C and 35 °C** and, one line later, that **eggs hatched from 15 °C to 40 °C**.
Both are true: at 40 °C the **eggs hatch but the larvae die**, so **the life cycle does not complete
even though the first stage succeeds**. The same logic explains why 35 °C shows the lowest pupal
survival while 40 °C is absent from the pupal panel, because **there are no pupae at 40 °C**.

**D5. The shape is the pea weevil's.** **An optimum with fall-off on both sides, not a monotonic rise.**
Writing that warmer means more mosquitoes answers only half of LO (h), because the outcome also says
**narrow temperature tolerance**. The school's own conclusion: **warmer conditions accelerate the whole
life cycle, giving more generations in a season**, and while extreme heat kills mosquitoes, **moderate
warmth boosts populations** and speeds transmission.

## E. LO (i): the spread beyond the tropics

**E1. The vector, and the line that justifies the whole outcome.** **Vectors are living organisms that
transmit infectious diseases between humans or from animals to humans.** The mechanism: bloodsucking
insects **ingest disease-producing microorganisms during a blood meal from an infected host** and
**inject them into a new host during a subsequent blood meal**. And the reason climate matters at all:
**arthropod vectors are ectotherms and therefore especially sensitive to climatic factors.**

**E2. The three limits that define beyond the tropics.** ***Ae. aegypti* lives mainly between 35°N and
35°S**, where **winter temperature is no colder than 10 °C**, and **typically not above 1000 m
altitude**. Warming pushes all three outward, in **latitude and altitude**. The outcome is not about
more dengue in the tropics, so **every answer must end outside them**.

**E3. Four temperature effects on the vector.** **Increased activity.** **Increased biting frequency**,
since females bite more often when warm, giving **a greater capacity to produce offspring**. **Improved
survival of eggs and adults** within the survivable range, with **warmer water shortening larval
maturation**. And **expanded geographic range in latitude and altitude**. The thresholds: **dengue
transmission above 10 °C**, **malaria transmission above 16 °C**, and **death generally above 34 °C**.

**E4. The pathogen-side mechanism is a separate mark.** Warming acts on the pathogen inside the vector,
not only on the vector. For dengue, **the time the virus needs inside the mosquito to become infective
is shortened**, so **a larger fraction of the mosquito's life is spent infective**. For malaria,
**higher temperature lets the parasites mature more quickly**. This is the **extrinsic incubation
period**, and vector effect plus pathogen effect is a two-mark structure where most candidates write
one.

**E5. Three climate drivers, and rainfall is not monotonic.** **Vector abundance and disease incidence
rise with temperature, precipitation and humidity**, and **humidity is proportionate to vector
survival**. But rainfall cuts both ways: **torrential rainfall and flooding reduce populations by
washing out eggs and larvae**, while **lower rainfall can raise transmission by leaving rivers as pools
and by forcing households to store water**. **Both extremes can raise transmission by different
routes**, which is the Book's clearest evaluate-level point.

**E6. People move the virus, not mosquitoes.** Most female *Ae. aegypti* **spend their lifetime in or
around the houses where they emerge**, so **people, rather than mosquitoes, move the virus between
communities**. Attributing all spread to warming leaves out a driver the schemes reward.

**E7. State the change, not the baseline.** A scheme accepts **warmer winter temperature in the
temperate region** and states **reject the temperate region is cooler**. The chapter's closing chain:
**global warming shortens the life cycle → mosquito numbers rise → adult development speeds up →
transmission rates rise → malaria and dengue resurge and redistribute worldwide.**

## F. How a discuss or comment question is marked in this Book

**F1. Take a stance, then bound it.** The scheme requires an **explicit stance substantiated with
relevant data**, and penalises **contradictory data offered in an attempt to discuss both sides**. Use
counter-evidence to limit your claim, not to reverse it: the same scheme rejects the absolute **cannot
help to reduce ocean acidification** in favour of the qualified **to a limited extent**.

**F2. Quote the data at the granularity the scheme wants.** **Merely lifting values from the graph is
insufficient for full credit**, and stronger responses **made comparisons across different
temperatures**. Copy compound units exactly, and use the figure's own statistical word: schemes have
rejected **mean** where a box plot showed a **median**, and **richness** where **number of species** was
wanted.

**F3. Name the specific process, and expect the honest answer to be sceptical.** A scheme tells
candidates to **name specific processes such as carbon fixation and light-dependent reactions**, not
just photosynthesis. On judgement, Specimen P3 Q2(c)(ii) hands over a calculated offset of about
**0.59%** and asks for a comment on planting urban trees. The answer that scores says **urban trees
cannot by themselves offset the emissions** and that **reducing emissions at source matters far more**,
qualified by the point that the calculation **counts one year of fixation while the carbon stays in the
wood for the tree's life**, and by the other benefits such as **evaporative cooling, particulate
trapping and habitat**. Saying trees are good for the environment has not answered it.

## H. Errors in your notes, and the corrected version to learn

1. **Malaria is said to be caused by one of four *Plasmodium* species.** **Five infect humans**:
   *falciparum*, *vivax*, *ovale*, *malariae*, plus ***P. knowlesi*, recognised since 2004 and dominant
   in parts of Malaysian Borneo**, which is a Southeast Asian omission in a Singapore syllabus.
2. **Page 133 says 10 °C and below kills *Aedes* eggs, larvae and adults**, and the chapter's own data
   do not support the egg half: the study **tested only down to 15 °C**, and page 128 says eggs
   **survive desiccation for months**. **Quote 10 °C as the limit for transmission and adult survival**,
   not for eggs, which are the cold-tolerant stage.
3. **The life cycle is said to complete between 15 °C and 35 °C** one line before eggs are reported
   hatching to 40 °C. These reconcile, and §D4 is the reconciliation. The chapter never spells it out.
4. **The mountaintop-species sentence on page 104 is reversed.** It reads that the ringtail possum,
   microhylid frogs and golden bowerbird are **restricted to 600 metres above elevation**, a point
   rather than a range. The sense is **restricted to elevations above 600 m**, so that **the band of
   suitable habitat shrinks upward until it disappears**.
5. **The gastric brooding frog binomial is misspelled** as *Rheobatrachus vitellins*, for
   ***vitellinus***, and **chythrid** should be **chytrid**, from ***Batrachochytrium***.
6. **Food security is defined twice, eleven pages apart, in two different forms.** Learn the **page 111
   World Food Summit version with its four dimensions**, which is what an extended LO (e) answer is
   built on.
7. **Chapter 8.1's master outline gives section 5.2.1 the title of section 4.2.1.** Chapter 8.5's own
   heading reads **Impact of Climate Change on Agro-Ecosystems**, and 8.5 is correct.
8. **The ethics sentence on page 106 has no outcome behind it** and is cut, along with the rest of this
   Book's ethics and advocacy framing. The next sentence supplies the examinable version: **losing
   biodiversity is a problem for human society because of the many tangible, pragmatic ways that
   biodiversity benefits people.** That sentence is LO (g).
