### Question 2

**(a)** *Which statement correctly describes the effect over a 100-year period of the emission of greenhouse gases in the UK in 2021?* **[1]** [INFERRED: no official key]

**1.** **C**

**(b)** *Explain your answer, taking each of the four statements in turn.* **[4]** [INFERRED: no official key]

**1. Work out the CO2 equivalent for every gas first.** **CO2 = 3.4 × 10⁸ × 1 = 3.4 × 10⁸ tonnes.** **CH4 = 2.0 × 10⁶ × 28 = 5.6 × 10⁷ tonnes.** **N2O = 7.2 × 10⁴ × 265 = 1.91 × 10⁷ tonnes.** **SF6 = 1.8 × 10¹ × 23 500 = 4.23 × 10⁵ tonnes.** **NF3 = 2.1 × 10⁻² × 16 100 = 3.4 × 10² tonnes.**

**2. C is correct.** **1.91 × 10⁷ ÷ 5.6 × 10⁷ = 0.341**, which is **just over one third**, since one third is 0.333.

**3. A is wrong**, because **carbon dioxide at 3.4 × 10⁸ contributes far more than methane at 5.6 × 10⁷**. A very large mass at a GWP of 1 beats a small mass at a GWP of 28.

**4. B is wrong.** SF6 does have the highest GWP of the five, but **4.23 × 10⁵ ÷ 3.4 × 10⁸ × 100 = 0.124%**, which is **more than 0.1%**, not less.

**5. D is wrong.** **4.23 × 10⁵ ÷ 16 100 = 26.3 tonnes of NF3**, which is **a little under 27 tonnes, not a little over**.

**6.** Note the GWP values the paper supplies: **methane 28 and nitrous oxide 265**, which are AR5 values. **The school teaches methane 25 and nitrous oxide 298**, which are AR4 values. Both are published and neither is wrong. **A candidate who substitutes a memorised 25 into a table that says 28 gets the arithmetic wrong.** Memorise only the shape, that methane is roughly 25 to 30 times as potent as CO2 over a century.

**2. Mass of carbon in this = 2 424 000 × 27/100 = 654 480 tonnes year⁻¹.**

The stance.** In terms of carbon the benefit is **very small**: the urban trees offset only about **0.59%, which is less than 1%, of the carbon released by cars alone**, and cars are only one of many sources of emissions, so **planting urban trees cannot by itself mitigate Singapore's contribution to climate change** and **reducing emissions at source matters far more**.

### Question 4

*Discuss the effects of climate change (as a result of greenhouse gas emissions).* **[13]**

**1.** **Emission of greenhouse gases results in global warming,** **which causes the melting of ice sheets and sea ice.**

**2. Rise in sea level, any two.** **Melting of ice sheets results in extra water entering the ocean, leading to an increase in sea level.** **Melting of sea ice can lead to a positive feedback loop which further accelerates the melting of polar ice caps.** **As oceans become warmer, the water also expands, increasing the volume of the ocean water and leading to a further rise in sea level.** **Low-lying islands and coastal regions may become submerged.**

**3. Stress on fresh water supplies, any one pair.** **Many island nations will have their supplies of drinking water reduced because sea water will invade their freshwater aquifers.** **Melting of fresh water, that is ice sheets, into the sea also turns the already scarce fresh water into salt water, decreasing the freshwater availability.** **Global warming also results in more evaporation, which leads to an increased amount of water in the atmosphere and hence heavier rainfall,** **which leads to more rapid movement of water from the atmosphere back to the oceans, reducing our ability to store and use it.** **At higher temperature, more precipitation will occur as rain rather than snow.** **Reservoirs fill quickly to maximum capacity, and any excess rain water is lost as runoff which cannot be stored, so less water will penetrate the ground surface, causing reduced soil moisture and reduced groundwater replenishment.**

**4. Heat waves and heavy rains, any two.** **A warmer climate creates an atmosphere that can collect, retain and drop more water, changing weather patterns.** **This causes wet areas to become wetter and dry areas to become drier, and can lead to heat waves and heavy rain.** **Heatwaves associated with low humidity may result in wildfire.** **Heavy rain increases the amount of runoff into rivers and lakes, washing sediment, nutrients, pollutants, trash, animal waste and other materials into water supplies, making them unusable, unsafe, or in need of water treatment.**

**5. Death of coral reefs, any two.** **Heat stress can cause coral bleaching, in which coral polyps expel the zooxanthellae.** **This is because at higher temperatures zooxanthellae photosynthesis is disrupted and the zooxanthellae produce more toxic compounds.** **If temperatures remain above the bleaching threshold for prolonged periods of time, corals will eventually die from starvation and disease.** **Ocean acidification affects hard corals as they cannot absorb the calcium carbonate to maintain their skeletons, and the stony skeletons that support corals will dissolve.**

**6. Migration of fishes and insects, any two.** **Rising ocean temperatures can directly affect the metabolism, life cycle and behaviour of marine species.** **Many fish species, especially their young and larvae, have highly specific temperature ranges and will move to cooler waters to survive.** **Warmer temperatures may disrupt the migratory behaviour and timing of several fish species, for example by impeding their ability to orient themselves for effective navigation.** **Insect distribution and migration are also greatly influenced by increased temperatures, as they are cold-blooded and will move to habitats with temperatures that are optimal for their growth and reproduction.** **These insect migrations have ramifications for many ecosystems, because insects play important ecological functions such as pollination, acting as vectors of diseases and parasites, killing other pest insects, or being crop pests themselves.**

**7. Release of greenhouse gases in frozen organic matter.** **Global warming leads to accelerated melting of permafrost.** **As permafrost thaws, the frozen organic matter starts to decay and is digested by microbes, and the digestion releases carbon dioxide and methane.**

**8. QWC:** **obtain at least 1 mark each from 3 different categories of effects of climate change.**

**9.** Note the one line in this scheme that its own Book contradicts. Point 5's last clause says corals **cannot absorb the calcium carbonate** they need. That is the school's phrasing and it is chemically wrong: corals **deposit** calcium carbonate from **calcium ions and carbonate ions**, and what acidification removes is the **free carbonate**. Write the chain from Question 2(d), not this clause.

**Source:** SAJC Prelim 2017 P3 Section B Q4b, stem and the entire mark scheme verbatim including every category heading, every "any two" restriction and the QWC line, at its real 13 marks. This is one of three open "discuss the effects of climate change" sweeps of LO (b) among the 17 essay-scale Book 8 stems in the papers.
**Archetype:** the LO (b) sweep. The outcome names eight effects and the examiner samples from the list, so the examinable unit is **the chain**, not the label. Note how the scheme is organised: five categories, each requiring one or two chains, and a QWC condition that forces breadth across at least three categories.
**Trap:** listing the eight effects as labels with no causal links, which scores nothing; going deep on coral and never reaching three categories, which fails QWC; letting melting sea ice raise sea level; and reproducing the scheme's own faulty calcium carbonate clause.
**LOs:** (b) in full, all eight named effects; reach-back Core Idea 3 decomposition in the permafrost category and Core Idea 1 enzymes in the migration category.
**Concepts fused:** (1) greenhouse emission to warming as the common first link; (2) ice sheet melt against thermal expansion; (3) the ice-albedo positive feedback; (4) saltwater intrusion into aquifers; (5) a warmer atmosphere holding more water vapour; (6) wet areas wetter and dry areas drier; (7) runoff outpacing storage and carrying pollutants; (8) bleaching by expulsion of zooxanthellae; (9) acidification against the skeleton; (10) narrow thermal tolerance driving fish and insect range shifts; (11) insects as pollinators, vectors and pests; (12) permafrost thaw and microbial release of CO2 and CH4.

**1.** **From 1950 to 2000 there was an increase in the average global temperature, from 13.8 °C to 14.8 °C, due to global warming.**

**2.** **The migration of the robins was delayed by about 1 month, from September to October in 1950, to October to November in 2000.**

**3.** **Global warming resulted in warmer winters or a delayed onset of winter, during which food resources remain available, and this might have postponed the robins' migration or departure from southern Canada.**

**(b)** *Explain why the caterpillars are found in abundance earlier as shown in Fig. 3.3.* **[2]**

**1.** **The increase in global temperature can increase enzymatic activity or the metabolic rate of the insect, which speeds up developmental time.**

**2.** **This can result in the shortening of the life cycle of the insect, resulting in caterpillars emerging from the egg much sooner, from September to October in 1950 to August to September in 2000.**

**3.** A bonus route in one of the two schemes, not required: **the rise in global temperatures may also cause plants to produce new leaves sooner, which provides food for caterpillars earlier in the season, leading to their earlier abundance.**

**(c)** *Discuss how the changes shown in Fig. 3.2 and Fig. 3.3 can impact the American robins.* **[3]**

**1.** **Global warming causes caterpillars to become abundant earlier, while robins migrate later, leading to a mismatch in timing.**

**2.** **By the time the robins arrive in southern Canada, their main food source is already depleted, as the caterpillars might have pupated or metamorphosed into adult butterflies, reducing the robins' chances of survival and successful breeding.**

**3.** **As a result, the robins may be forced to fly further south or rely on alternative food sources to survive.**

**4.** The parallel scheme for the same question splits the reasoning into five separately creditable points: **the robins migrate later while the caterpillars are in abundance earlier**; **this results in the lack of a food source for robins as they arrive at south Canada**; **the lack of food source can result in reduced viability of the robins at south Canada**; **robins might need to fly further south to find a region with a food source**; **robins might need to find other food sources in the region in order to survive.**

**5.** The general rule this case rehearses: a **phenological mismatch arises because two interacting species use different environmental cues**, one tracking temperature and one not. That transfers to any predator-prey or plant-pollinator pair an examiner invents.

**(d)** *Suggest and explain how climatic change has reduced the survival rate of snowshoe hares in mountain habitats.* **[2]**

**1.** **Snow melts earlier, or there is less snow, so there is less camouflage.**

**2.** The **hares which have yet to moult, the white hares, are more easily seen, eaten or killed by predators**, or by any named example of a predator.

**Archetype:** phenology read off two timelines that move in opposite directions. This is the shape the papers like most on LO (f) and it is the one where reciting the caribou case from the notes earns nothing.
**Trap:** explaining the robin shift and the caterpillar shift separately and never putting them together, which is the whole of (c); assuming both species respond to the same cue, when the point is that they do not; and giving "less camouflage" in (d) without the predation consequence.

### Question 7

**(a)** *Explain how the stomatal response shown in Fig. 3.1 would allow I. pes-caprae to survive the effects of climate change.* **[3]**

**1.** An **increase in CO2 concentration from 350 or 400 µmol mol⁻¹ to 900 µmol mol⁻¹ causes the mean width of stomatal aperture to decrease from 2.0, 1.9 or 1.75 µm to 0.5 µm.**

**2.** **Less water is lost, evaporated or transpired through the stomata.**

**3.** This **compensates for, or allows survival in, low water availability or dry conditions.**

**4. Examiner comment, verbatim.** **Students who did well made reference to the trend in the graph and quoted values correctly; most correctly linked the concept of stomatal closure to the advantage of being able to limit water loss and correctly related it to the question stem which stated that climate change may reduce water availability.**

**(b)** *Suggest why there might not be a significant increase in growth of I. pes-caprae plants despite the increase in atmospheric CO2 concentrations.* **[2]**

**1.** Any matched pair.

**2.** **Temperature, increased due to global warming, is below the optimal temperature of enzymes**, which **leads to an increased rate of both cellular respiration and photosynthesis, hence loss of carbon dioxide in cellular respiration is equal to gain of carbon dioxide in photosynthesis, hence net carbon gain is zero, hence mass does not increase.** **Stating that the temperature is above optimum and enzymes denature is rejected, because growth would then decrease.**

**3.** Or an **increase in atmospheric CO2 concentration leads to a decrease in stomatal aperture size**, **hence even if environmental CO2 increases in concentration, the concentration of CO2 within the leaves does not increase significantly, hence the rate of photosynthesis does not increase and growth of plants does not increase.**

**4.** Or **global warming causes increased loss of water from guard cells**, so the **guard cells become less turgid and the stomata close, hence CO2 does not necessarily enter the plant to increase photosynthesis to cause an increase in mass.** **Water being limiting or reduced water availability is rejected, as the plant would wilt and die.**

**5. Examiner comment, verbatim.** **Many students who read the question in its entirety gave balanced responses that summarised the net effect of reduced stomatal aperture, a lower rate of CO2 intake, despite a steeper CO2 concentration gradient. Vague responses made reference to temperature without stating that the temperatures are below optimal, or erroneously stated that the temperatures are above optimal, which is not acceptable because the question clearly stated the plant did grow, it just did not grow as much as hypothesised. Very few students linked the function of the stomata to gaseous exchange, which includes oxygen, and even fewer were able to articulate that plant growth is not a direct consequence of photosynthesis but an overall result: photosynthesis makes glucose or TP, which is used in cellular respiration where ATP is made for cell division to create more plant cells for growth, or the glucose is used to make cellulose cell walls or glycoproteins that are building blocks of cells.**

**(c)** *With reference to Fig. 3.2 and Table 3.1, discuss the likely impact of the predicted changes in carbon dioxide concentration, global temperatures and rainfall patterns on the global distribution of C3 and C4 plants.* **[3]**

**1.** **An increase in carbon dioxide concentration will likely increase temperature.**

**2.** **At a high carbon dioxide concentration of 500 parts per million and a high temperature of 25 °C, the rate of photosynthesis for C4 plants or sugar cane at 34 µg m⁻² h⁻¹ is higher than that of C3 plants or barley at 20 µg m⁻² h⁻¹.** Accept **maximum rate of photosynthesis**; **reject "peak"**. Accept any high value of carbon dioxide concentration above 400 ppm.

**3.** **Hence both plants will grow well, but C4 plants are better adapted than C3 plants in hotter areas and their population will likely increase.** Accept reference to latitude, tropical or temperate.

**4.** **Increased temperatures may result in lower rainfall in some places.** Accept higher rainfall.

**5.** **C4 plants absorb between 204 and 350 g of water, which is less than C3 plants at between 542 and 682 g.** **Reject average values.**

**6.** **Hence C4 plants are better adapted than C3 plants in drier areas and their population will likely increase.**

**7.** **However, the predicted change in temperature over the next century is only small, therefore it may not make a lot of difference.** That last point is the bounded counter-argument, and it is what turns a description into a discussion.

TJC Prelim 2023 P3 Q3c, stem, figure, Table 3.1 and the full key verbatim including every accept and reject line (c).
**Archetype:** LO (e) reached through Core Idea 5. Every mark in this question is photosynthesis, gaseous exchange or water relations wearing a climate costume, which is exactly what the syllabus means by assimilation of the Core Ideas. Note that Paper 4 of the specimen was **entirely photosynthesis**, and that **six of the seventeen essay-scale Book 8 stems in the papers are explicit photosynthesis or respiration crossovers**.
**Trap:** in (b), reaching for denaturation when the stem says the plant did grow, only less than hypothesised, which the scheme rejects by name; treating growth as a direct consequence of photosynthesis rather than an outcome of respiration and biosynthesis; using averages in (c) where the scheme rejects them; and describing the C3 and C4 difference without ever reaching a distributional conclusion.
**LOs:** (e) consequences to the sustainable food supply; (f) geographical distribution; reach-back Core Idea 5 photosynthesis and carbon fixation, and Core Idea 1 LO (l) water movement and stomatal control.
**Concepts fused:** (1) stomatal aperture responding to CO2 concentration; (2) transpirational water loss through open stomata; (3) the trade-off between water saving and CO2 entry; (4) internal against external CO2 concentration; (5) net carbon gain as photosynthesis minus respiration; (6) growth as an outcome of respiration and biosynthesis, not of photosynthesis alone; (7) C3 against C4 carbon fixation efficiency at high temperature; (8) saturation of the CO2 response curve; (9) water requirement per crop as a distribution constraint; (10) bounding a prediction with the size of the predicted change.

### Question 8

*Discuss whether you agree or disagree that the impacts of human activity on climate change will lead to the greater spread of mosquitoes. Comment if this matter requires both urgent and global action.* **[15]**

**1. Section A, impacts of human activity on climate change.** **Human activities contribute to increased production of greenhouse gases**, for example **human-induced burning of fossil fuels releasing CO2**, an **increase in decomposing materials, for example in landfills or sewage, increasing CO2 release**, **industrial processes such as cement works releasing CO2**, **burning land and vegetation to clear land for agriculture**, and **food choices that have a greater carbon footprint for production**. **Students must give 2 examples of human activities to score one mark.**

**2.** **These activities have led to an increase in greenhouse gas emissions since 1750, the start of the industrial revolution.** **Greenhouse gases such as CO2 and methane or nitrous oxide allow short-wave radiation from the sun to pass through to heat the Earth's surface and lower atmosphere.** **The heated surfaces radiate outgoing long-wave radiation, infrared or heat, which is absorbed and re-emitted by the greenhouse gases back to the Earth's surface.** **Increased concentration of greenhouse gases leads to increased trapping of radiation or heat, causing more warming.**

**3. Section B, agree that this will lead to the spread of *Aedes* or *Anopheles* mosquitoes.** There is an **increase in global temperatures at higher latitudes and altitudes**, which **become more optimal for insects including *Aedes* mosquitoes.**

**4.** At **higher temperatures, the duration from first instar to emergence is shorter as the rate of enzyme-catalysed reactions increases**, and **this leads to a faster development rate and decreased length of reproductive cycles.**

**5.** **Climate change could result in more rainfall in certain areas, creating more pools of stagnant water for mosquito breeding**, or the reverse argument, that **less rainfall or drought in some areas means less standing water for mosquitoes to breed.**

**6. Section C, disagree, since climate change is not the only factor and populations may be reduced in the tropics.** **Changes in land use and human population growth and migration may also lead to greater spread of the *Aedes* mosquito.**

**7.** **Poor mosquito control measures can also lead to the spread, for example less use of insecticides, lack of education or awareness, and enforcement of measures.**

**8.** **Population increase, greater density and urbanisation, together with poor control measures, can lead to more breeding grounds.**

**9.** An **increase in global temperatures may cause the tropics to be uninhabitable due to the small thermal safety margins of most insects**, which is a **range shift rather than a spread or expansion.**

**10. Section D, requires urgent and global action.** ***Aedes aegypti* or the female *Anopheles* mosquito is a vector for human disease like dengue fever and malaria**, so there is **greater disease transmission.**

**11.** **Increased temperature also results in a shorter extrinsic incubation period, as the virus or *Plasmodium* parasite can reproduce faster within the mosquito vector.**

**12.** **Dengue fever is caused by the spread of dengue virus through the bite of an infected female *Aedes aegypti* mosquito**, and the **highest risk is in highly populated regions, the human host, with rainy seasons, the breeding ground for the mosquito vector.** **Dengue virus infects cells of the immune system and can be potentially fatal with secondary dengue infection.**

**13.** **Malaria is caused by the spread of the *Plasmodium* parasite through the bite of an infected female *Anopheles* mosquito.** **Both diseases, while mainly prevalent in tropical and sub-tropical regions, can affect human populations globally due to migration and human travel.**

**14.** **Climate change cannot be mitigated through the action of certain countries or regions only.** Accept: **climate change requires a global effort involving all countries.**

**15. Quality of written communication:** **1 point from B or C, plus D.**

**16.** Two notes to carry into this essay. First, the school's own page 133 says **malaria is caused by one of four *Plasmodium* species**; **five infect humans**, the fifth being ***P. knowlesi*, recognised since 2004 and dominant in parts of Malaysian Borneo**, which is a Southeast Asian omission in a Singapore syllabus. Second, a scheme elsewhere in this Book requires that a discuss answer **take an explicit stance and substantiate it with relevant data**, and records that **weaker responses included contradictory data in an attempt to discuss both sides of the argument, but this would not satisfy the requirements of the question**. Section C here is not a reversal of your stance; it is the boundary you put on it.

**Source:** RI Prelim 2023 P3 Q4a, stem and the entire mark scheme verbatim including all four lettered sections, the two-example requirement in Section A and the QWC condition, at its real 15 marks. This is one of five *Aedes* and dengue essays among the 17 essay-scale Book 8 stems in the papers, and the only one that asks for a judgement rather than a description.
**Archetype:** the evaluate essay. Discuss, comment and evaluate all demand a judgement, and three of the nine outcomes use the word discuss in their own wording. Note how the scheme is organised: cause, agree, disagree, and then a separate section on the call for action, with QWC requiring the last of those plus one of the middle two.
**Trap:** writing only the agree case and scoring nothing for Section C, or writing a balanced both-sides answer with no stance at all; giving one example of a human activity in Section A when the scheme demands two for the mark; and leaving out the extrinsic incubation period, which is the pathogen-side half of the mechanism.
**LOs:** (i) in full; (a) through Section A; (h) through Section B; reach-back Extension Topic A for the transmission cycle and Core Idea 1 for the enzyme clause.
**Concepts fused:** (1) named anthropogenic activities and their gases; (2) shortwave in, longwave out, absorbed and re-emitted; (3) higher latitudes and altitudes becoming optimal; (4) first instar to emergence shortened by enzyme kinetics; (5) rainfall cutting both ways for breeding sites; (6) land use, urbanisation and human migration as non-climate drivers; (7) failing control measures; (8) thermal safety margins making the tropics uninhabitable, giving a shift rather than an expansion; (9) the extrinsic incubation period; (10) two named diseases with their two named vectors; (11) global travel; (12) mitigation as a collective-action problem.
