## A. Variation is the raw material, and it comes from three places

**A1. Without variation there is no selection.** Every individual would respond **identically to the
selection pressure**, so none could survive or reproduce better than any other. Variation is what makes
**differential survival and reproductive success** possible, and that is the phrase to write. Do not
answer with *more adapted to the environment*, which is explicitly rejected: it names the **outcome** of
selection, not the **role** of variation. **Genetic variation is a pre-requisite for natural selection.**

**A2. The outcome names three sources, so a full answer names three.** LO 4(a) prints **mutation**,
**meiosis** and **sexual reproduction** in its own bracket. Split them by what they actually contribute:

| Source | What it contributes | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Mutation** | **new alleles** | a **permanent change in the nucleotide sequence** of the DNA, either a **gene mutation** or a **chromosomal mutation / aberration** |
| **Meiosis** | **new combinations of existing alleles** | **crossing over at prophase I** and **independent assortment at metaphase I** |
| **Sexual reproduction** | **new combinations of existing alleles** | **random fertilisation**, so any gamete may fuse with any other |

**A3. Mutation is the only source of new alleles, and it is not the only source of variation.** Meiosis
and fertilisation shuffle alleles that already exist; only mutation creates one that never existed
before. Write **mutation is the only source of new alleles**. Your notes say something different on page
45 and it is wrong; see section L.

**A4. Variation arises spontaneously, before the environment changes.** Mutations are **not
goal-directed**, since they **do not arise as a result of, or in anticipation of, environmental
necessities**. The variations **occur spontaneously before a change in the environment** and are **NOT
formed as a result of the new environment** to make individuals better adapted. This single point
separates Darwin from Lamarck and it is the most heavily penalised framing error in the topic.

## B. Natural selection, defined with both of its riders

**B1. The definition.** Natural selection is **the process by which certain individuals that are better
adapted to an environment survive to reproduce**, which is **differential survival and reproduction**.
There is **reproductive success of fitter individuals over those that are less fit**. It **increases the
frequency of favourable genotypes / alleles in the gene pool**, and **the resultant population becomes
adapted to its particular environment**.

**B2. Rider one: selection acts on alleles that are already there.** It **does not cause genetic changes
in individuals but acts on existing alleles in the population's gene pool, which were generated by
mutations prior to the selection event**. Put the same idea the school's other way round: **natural
selection is a process of editing, not a creative mechanism. A drug does not create resistant pathogens;
it selects for resistant individuals that are already present in the population.**

**B3. Rider two: selection acts on the phenotype.** **The entity that is subjected to natural selection
is the whole organism, not the underlying genotype.** Selection therefore acts **more directly on the
phenotype than on the genotype**, and **on the genotype only indirectly, via how the genotype affects
the phenotype**. The environment **does not direct the changes in DNA, but acts upon phenotypes** that
arise through random changes in DNA.

**B4. The environment is the selection pressure, and fitness is counted in offspring.** The
**environment acts as the selection pressure to determine the direction and results of selection**, and
**the intensity and direction of that pressure varies in both space and time**. What selection rewards
is **relative fitness**, **the contribution an individual makes to the gene pool of the next generation
relative to the contributions of other individuals**. Because pressure varies, **what is beneficial in
one situation may be useless or even harmful in another**.

**B5. Lamarck against Darwin is the examinable contrast.** Lamarck proposed **use and disuse of organs**,
in which **a need for a structure induced its development**, plus **inheritance of acquired characters**,
so a change acquired in one lifetime passed to the offspring. Darwin reverses the causal order: variation
exists first, and **the environment merely selects those individuals that happen by chance to be better
adapted**. Lamarck was right that **evolution occurs** and that **diversity is linked to adaptation**.

**B6. Darwin's argument runs as four observations and two inferences.** Learn it as a chain, because the
chain is the answer to any question about the role of selection.

1. **Great reproductive potential**: far more offspring are produced than can survive.
2. **Environmental restriction and constancy of numbers**: resources are limited, so population size
   stays roughly constant.
3. **Inference: a struggle for existence.**
4. **Variation exists within the population**, arising spontaneously and before the environmental change.
5. **Inference: survival of the fittest by natural selection.**
6. **Differential reproduction**, and because **like produces like** the favourable traits are inherited,
   giving **adaptation**, an **evolutionary modification that improves the chances of survival and
   reproductive success in a given environment**.
7. **Formation of new species** needs more than one favourable trait: **the inheritance of one
   characteristic alone may lead to a new subspecies, breed, variety or strain but not a new species**,
   so speciation needs the **accumulation of modifications** plus **reproductive or genetic isolation**.

**B7. Neo-Darwinism differs from Darwinism in exactly one respect.** **The Modern Synthesis incorporates
principles of Mendelian genetics and knowledge of molecular biology**, meaning **mutations and allele
frequency changes**, which were unknown to Darwin. That one sentence is the whole of the syllabus
content line on the neo-Darwinian revolution.

## C. The five-link chain from environmental change to allele frequency change

Each link is a separate mark. Run it forwards, never skip a link, and never let the environment create
the variation.

**Variation already exists in the population → the environmental factor acts as a selection pressure →
individuals with the favourable phenotype survive and reproduce more → they pass the favourable alleles
to their offspring → the frequency of those alleles increases in the gene pool over generations.**

A **Suggest** question hands you an organism you have never met, so practise the chain with the organism
and the pressure swapped out, and always name the **agent** doing the selecting rather than the
background change.

## D. Environmental factors as forces of selection, with the two named examples

**D1. The environment splits into abiotic and biotic components.** **Abiotic** factors are the
non-living ones such as **temperature, light, water availability and soil composition**. **Biotic**
factors are the living ones: **predators, pathogens, competitors** and **competition within a species**
for the same limited resources. Both can act as selection pressures.

**D2. Industrial melanism in the peppered moth, and the pressure is bird predation.** Before
industrialisation the **pale form was camouflaged against lichen-covered bark** and **melanic moths were
conspicuous**. Soot **darkened the tree trunks and killed the lichen**, so the **melanic form became
camouflaged and the pale form conspicuous**, and the **frequency of black melanic forms increased**,
which is what **industrial melanism** names. After clean-air legislation the pale form recovered. The
mark-bearing point is the agent: **the bird predator exerts the selection pressure**, by **feeding on
moths differentially depending on the colour of the background environment**. Soot changed the
background; **birds did the selecting**. Naming pollution as the pressure loses the mark.

**D3. Antibiotic resistance, and the pressure is the antibiotic.** A few bacteria already carry a
**resistance allele generated by random mutation before the antibiotic is applied**. The antibiotic
**kills the susceptible cells**, the resistant ones **survive and reproduce**, and because bacteria have
**very short generation times**, the **resistance allele frequency rises quickly**. The antibiotic
**selected** the resistant bacteria; it never **caused** them.

**D4. LO 4(b) says with examples and names none**, so the school's two are the ones to have cold, each
with its selection pressure identified.

## E. The three curve shapes, read off a distribution

None of the mode names appears in the 9477 syllabus, so you will not be asked to define one. The
**shapes** are examinable under LOs 4(b) and 4(c), because the standard stem hands you a distribution
curve before and after a selection event and asks you to describe and explain the change.

| Mode | What happens to the curve | What produces it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Directional** | **favours one extreme of the phenotype range and shifts the population mean**; the other extreme is **eliminated and its alleles become less frequent** | **a changing environment** | peppered moth; antibiotic resistance |
| **Stabilising** | **favours the existing mean**, **both extremes are selected against**, giving a **reduction in variance**; the commonest phenotype becomes **even more common** | **an unchanging environment where competition is not severe** | **human birth mass** |
| **Disruptive** | **eliminates intermediate phenotypes and favours extreme phenotypes** | two distinct resources or habitats within one range | black-bellied seedcrackers |

**Disruptive selection has a consequence the other two do not.** Because **the gene pool may become split
into two distinct gene pools**, it is the mode that can lead towards **two distinct populations** and so
towards speciation.

## G. Anatomical homologies, and the analogy that looks like one

**G1. Homology is similarity from shared ancestry.** It is **the presence, in two or more species, of a
structure derived from a recent common ancestor**, so **homologous structures reflect evolutionary
relationships between species**. The named case is the **pentadactyl limb**, **a limb with five fingers
or toes** possessed by the early land vertebrates and now modified into a human arm, a whale flipper, a
bat wing and a horse leg. **Homologous characters form a nested pattern**: all life shares the deepest
layer and **each successive smaller group adds its own homologies** to those it shares with larger
groups, which **is exactly what descent with modification from a common ancestor predicts**.

**G2. Divergent evolution is what homology demonstrates.** The **same inherited structure allows
different species to evolve different functions in order to occupy different environments**.

**G3. Analogy is similarity without shared ancestry.** It is **a characteristic that superficially
appears homologous but is actually independently acquired**, so **the similar structure is not derived
from a recent common ancestor** and is **usually due to adaptation to similar environments**.
**Analogous structures do not reflect evolutionary relationships between species.** They arise by
**convergent evolution**, in which **similar environmental pressures and natural selection produce
similar adaptations in organisms from different evolutionary lineages**.

| | **Homologous structures** | **Analogous structures** |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | inherited from a **recent common ancestor** | **independently acquired** in separate lineages |
| Cause of the similarity | **shared ancestry** | **similar selection pressures** in **similar environments** |
| Type of evolution shown | **divergent** | **convergent** |
| Use in reconstructing relationship | **does** indicate relationship | **does not** indicate relationship |
| Worked case | the **pentadactyl limb** | **marsupial and placental mammals** filling matching niches on separate continents |

**G4. The bat wing against the bird wing is the case with the subtlety.** Both are forelimbs used for
flight, so the wings look homologous. They are not. **The underlying skeletal systems of bats and birds
are homologous, but their wings are not**, because **the bird wing is supported by digit 2 and the bat
wing by digits 2 to 5**, so the two flight surfaces arose independently. Answer at the level of the
structure compared, not the animal.

## H. Molecular homologies, and the accumulation argument

**H1. The genetic code is universal.** All organisms **share the same genetic code, the same DNA and RNA
chemistry, and the same fundamental proteins**, which is only expected if they descend from a common
ancestor.

**H2. Differences accumulate with time since divergence.** When **an ancestral species gives rise to two
or more descendants, these descendants will initially exhibit fairly high overall similarity in their
DNA**, but as they **evolve independently they accumulate more and more differences**. Two consequences
follow, and they are the reasoning behind almost every molecular question in this Book:

- **The greater the similarity in the primary sequences of two species, the more closely related they
  are.**
- **The greater the number of differences, the longer since the two diverged from a common ancestor.**

**H3. The named comparison molecules are cytochrome c and haemoglobin.** **Homologous proteins** are
**evolutionarily related proteins that usually perform the same function in different species**, and the
conclusion drawn is that **the number of amino acid residues that differ in homologous proteins from any
two species is in proportion to the phylogenetic difference between those species**. Some positions are
**invariant residues**, occupied by the same residue in every species because **evolution selects against
any change at those positions**; others are **variable residues**.

## I. The fossil record

**I1. What a fossil is, and why there are so few.** Fossils are **the preserved remains of once-living
organisms typically left in sedimentary rocks**, and are **at least 10 000 years old**. They are rare
because **formation and preservation require that an organism be buried under conditions that slow or
prevent the decay process**, which most organisms never meet.

**I2. Two observations and two conclusions.** These are the school's own evidence-and-conclusion pairs
and they are already written in answer shape.

| Observation | Conclusion it supports |
|---|---|
| **A greater variety of fossils in younger rocks**, with **new species emerging and others going extinct** | this **indicates the times at which species originated and became extinct** |
| **Progressive change in the structures of organisms** through the record, with **increasing complexity in younger rocks** | **descent with modification through changes in homologous structures due to environmental selection pressures** |

**I3. The horse lineage, at one line.** Across the sequence from **Hyracotherium to Equus** the record
shows **increasing body size, reduction in the number of toes, and teeth modified for grazing**, tracking
a shift from **forest to grassland**. Learn the three trends and the driver, not the genus list.

## J. Biogeography, and Wallace

**J1. What biogeography is.** It is **the study of the range and geographical distribution of extinct
and modern species of organisms in different places throughout the world**. Its physical basis is
**continental drift, the slow movement of Earth's continental plates over time**, which separated
lineages that were once together. **Wallace** is named in the outcome, is the **father of biogeography**,
and gives his name to the **Wallace Line** separating the Asian and Australian faunas.

**J2. Island biogeography, with its conclusion.** **Islands have many endemic species, found nowhere else
in the world**, and **most island species are closely related to species from the nearest mainland or
neighbouring island** rather than to species on distant islands with similar climates. The conclusion is
that **these species could have descended with modification from a common mainland ancestor**. Climate
alone cannot explain the pattern; ancestry can.

**J3. Continent biogeography, with its conclusion.** **Australian marsupials resemble the placental
mammals occupying similar ecological niches elsewhere in the world**, having **evolved in isolation
after Australia separated from the other continents**. That resemblance is **convergent evolution** and
therefore **analogy**, not evidence of close relationship. The distribution is explained by **where the
continents were, not by where the climates are alike**.
