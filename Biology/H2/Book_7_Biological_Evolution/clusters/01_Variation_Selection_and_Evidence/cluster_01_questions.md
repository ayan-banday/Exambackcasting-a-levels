### Question 1

**(a)** *Explain why variation is important in natural selection.* **[2]**

**1.** **Variation describes the differences in characteristics shown by individuals belonging to the same species due to presence of different alleles in the individuals.**

**2.** **Variation is the raw material, presence of different alleles leading to difference in characteristics, for natural selection to act on.**

**3.** **Resulting in differential reproductive success.**

**4.** A second scheme setting the same question adds that **variation gives rise to different phenotypes**, that **without variation a population of individuals is genetically identical**, and that **variation allows for differential reproductive success to occur / selection pressure acts differently on the individuals / some individuals will have higher reproductive success if they are selected for / all individuals will die if all are selected against**. That scheme then prints **(reject "more adapted to environment")**.

**(b)** *Describe the causes of genetic variation in a population.* **[6]**

**1.** **During prophase I of meiosis, crossing-over takes place between non-sister chromatids.**

**2.** **Resulting in recombination of segments of non-sister chromatids between homologous chromosomes.**

**3.** **This leads to the formation of new combinations of alleles in gametes.**

**4.** **At metaphase I, independent assortment of homologous chromosomes.**

**5.** **The orientation of the bivalents with respect to the poles is random.**

**6.** **Hence a 50% chance that a daughter cell gets a paternal chromosome or a maternal chromosome.**

**7.** **Random fusion / fertilisation of gametes during sexual reproduction.**

**8.** **Carrying different combinations of chromosomes adds to genetic variation of the zygote formed.**

**9.** **Mutations occur to generate new alleles.**

**10.** Note for LO 4(a), which prints its three sources in a bracket. **Mutation is the only source of new alleles**; meiosis and random fertilisation supply only **new combinations of alleles that already exist**. Your own chapter 7.3 p.45 calls beneficial mutation the only source of genetic variation, which contradicts its own p.44 and p.47 and contradicts the outcome. Write **mutation is the only source of new alleles**.

**(c)** *State the type of variation shown by intestine length and list the factors that lead to it.* **[2]**

**1.** This is **continuous variation**.

**2.** **The intestine length is controlled by multiple genes.**

**3.** **The additive effect of each gene gives rise to a range of phenotypic variation.**

**4.** **The environment also affects the phenotypes.**

**(d)** *Explain the impact of low genetic variability on the survival of the population.* **[2]**

**1.** The **individuals within the population are very similar genetically / there is a small gene pool**, **often resulting from a small population size or inbreeding**.

**2.** There is a **reduced ability to adapt to changing environments / it makes a population more vulnerable to random catastrophic events**.

**3.** A **lack of genetic diversity means that unique and beneficial traits may be lost over time**.

**Trap:** answering (a) with *more adapted to the environment*, which the ACJC scheme rejects by name because it describes the outcome of selection rather than the role of variation in it; giving mutation alone in (b) when the outcome names three sources; naming crossing over without the stage, or naming independent assortment without saying the orientation is random; calling intestine length discontinuous because there are three named forms.
**LOs:** 4(a) throughout, all four parts. Reach-back to Book 2 LO 2(k) gene mutation and chromosomal aberration in (b) point 9, and to Book 3 LO 2(s) and 2(t) crossing over and independent assortment in (b) points 1 to 6.
**Concepts fused:** (1) variation as the raw material for selection; (2) differential reproductive success as the mark-bearing phrase; (3) crossing over at prophase I; (4) independent assortment at metaphase I and the random orientation of bivalents; (5) random fertilisation; (6) mutation as the source of new alleles; (7) continuous against discontinuous variation; (8) polygenic additive effect plus environment; (9) gene pool size and adaptability; (10) inbreeding and small population size as causes of low variability.

### Question 4

*Explain the three modes of natural selection and discuss which mode or modes might lead to the formation of new species, including the circumstances under which this may take place.* **[12]**

**1.** On the three modes of natural selection, maximum 6. **Accept a diagram if it accurately depicts the definition.**

**2.** Stabilising selection **occurs when the environment remains constant**.

**3.** The **selection pressure selects for the intermediate phenotype, while the extreme phenotypes are selected against.**

**4.** For example, **birth weight in humans. Babies born with very low or very high birth weights are at greater risk of health problems, so the average birth weight remains relatively stable.**

**5.** Directional selection occurs when **there are gradual changes in environmental conditions**.

**6.** The **selection pressure tends to select for phenotypes at one extreme of the range of phenotypes.** **Reject "select for one extreme phenotype".**

**7.** For example, the **evolution of longer necks in giraffes. As trees became taller, giraffes with longer necks were better able to reach the leaves and therefore more likely to survive and reproduce, and over time the population shifted towards longer-necked individuals.**

**8.** Disruptive selection occurs where the **selection pressure selects for phenotypes at both extremes**. **Reject "select for 2 extreme phenotypes".** It **occurs when selection pressure is exerted on the middle range of variation for a particular trait.**

**9.** For example, **dark and light-coloured oysters, as both have camouflage advantages: light-coloured oysters blend with the rocks and dark-coloured oysters can hide under the rock shadow.**

**10.** On selection leading to speciation, at least one point. **Disruptive selection is most likely to lead to speciation, as it involves the change in environment or selection pressure, which selects for two extreme populations.**

**11.** **This increases the rate of allele frequency changes and the chance of accumulation of genetic differences.**

**12.** **Both directional and disruptive selection result in the change in allele frequencies of the population.**

**13.** On circumstances required to lead to speciation, at least one point. **Geographical isolation, where populations are separated by a physical barrier.**

**14.** **Behavioural isolation, which occurs when two populations are capable of interbreeding but have differences in courtship rituals or other behavioural strategies that prevent mating.**

**15.** **Habitat isolation, where populations are located in the same geographical area but are separated or confined to their habitats or niches.**

**16.** **Physiological isolation leading to reproductive isolation, where the existence of biological factors (barriers) impedes members of two populations from interbreeding and producing viable, fertile offspring.** Maximum 2 elaborations, from **mechanical isolation, the incompatibility of the anatomical structure of the reproductive organs preventing the transfer of gametes between species**; **gametic isolation, the inability of sperm from one species or population to recognise the egg from another**; **reduced hybrid viability or fertility**; and **temporal isolation, where different populations have different mating seasons or flowering seasons, or may become sexually mature at different times of the year, hence decreasing the possibility of reproduction.**

**17.** **These isolation mechanisms reduce gene flow between the isolated populations.**

**18.** **Over time, when sufficient genetic differences or mutations accumulate, and the separated populations can no longer interbreed to produce fertile and viable offspring, speciation has occurred.**

**19.** **Geographical isolation separating the populations can lead to allopatric speciation, while other isolation mechanisms separating the population can lead to sympatric speciation.**

**20.** **On the other hand, stabilising selection reduces the genetic variation within the population, as it favours intermediate phenotypes, limiting the rate of natural selection.**

**Source:** TMJC Prelim 2023 P3 Q4(b), Section B free-response essay. Stem and the whole key verbatim, including both reject lines, the accept-a-diagram note, the maximum-6 cap on the first half and the maximum-2 cap on the isolation elaborations.
**Archetype:** evaluate and discuss, which holds 37 stems worth 10 marks or more. The Book 7 essay is outcome-led: this one is LO 4(b) and LO 4(c) expanded, with a bridge into LO 4(j).
**Trap:** the scheme rejects "select for one extreme phenotype" for directional and "select for 2 extreme phenotypes" for disruptive, because selection acts on a **range** of phenotypes at one end, not on a single value. None of the three mode names appears anywhere in the 9477 syllabus under any spelling, so do not hang an answer on the vocabulary; the **shapes** and **what produces each** are what LOs 4(b) and 4(c) actually carry. The giraffe example in point 7 is where the Lamarckian slip appears: the necks did not grow, the population's mean shifted because longer-necked variants already present survived and bred.
**LOs:** 4(b) and 4(c) in points 2 to 12, 4(j) in points 13 to 19. This is the one place in the drill the modes are set out in full, which is why nothing else in the set repeats them.
**Concepts fused:** (1) stabilising selection and the constant environment that produces it; (2) directional selection and the gradually changing environment; (3) disruptive selection and pressure on the middle of the range; (4) a worked example attached to each mode; (5) why disruptive is the only mode that can split a gene pool; (6) allele frequency change as the common outcome of directional and disruptive; (7) the four isolation categories; (8) the elaborations under physiological isolation; (9) gene flow reduction as the mechanism the barriers share; (10) the accumulation requirement before speciation is complete; (11) the allopatric against sympatric split; (12) stabilising selection as a variation-reducing process.

### Question 5

*Explain how anatomical and molecular homology support Darwin's theory of evolution on descent with modification.* **[10]**

**1.** Definition. **Homology refers to similar characteristics (anatomical or molecular) found in different species due to common ancestry.**

**2.** Anatomical homology, maximum 7. **Organisms with anatomical homology have anatomical structures such as bones, organs and gross structural features that they share with a common ancestor and thus supports Darwin's theory of descent with modification.** **Accept morphological for anatomical.**

**3.** Named example: the **pentadactyl limb structure in forelimbs**.

**4.** **Of all tetrapods, humans, cats, whales**, or any valid example naming a few organisms or a group of organisms.

**5.** **Forelimbs have same arrangement of bones but have different functions and superficially look different.**

**6.** For example, **legs for walking in cats, flippers for swimming in whales**, any two examples.

**7.** **The 5 digit pentadactyl limb structure in the common ancestor was altered by natural selection or different selection pressures in different organisms to suit specialised functions or environments, resulting in variations of the pentadactyl limb structure.**

**8.** **Anatomically homologous structures that are greatly reduced in size or have little to no function, as they were selected against, are called vestigial structures.**

**9.** **Organisms with vestigial structures share common ancestry with organisms in which the structure is still functional and thus supports Darwin's theory of descent with modification.**

**10.** Named example: **hind limbs (femur) and hips (pelvic bones) in whales are reduced to small bones as they are no longer beneficial to whales which swim; however, their presence in whales suggests common ancestry with tetrapods, terrestrial ancestors with four legs.** Or, the **appendix in humans is also a vestigial structure as it is reduced from the caecum of its primate ancestors which was involved in digestion of plant material, so its presence in humans suggests common ancestry with primates.** Or, **fossil records showing modification of homologous structures with time.**

**11.** Molecular homology. **Organisms with molecular homology have similar DNA, RNA and amino acid sequences as they share a common ancestor that had these molecules.**

**12.** Named example: **cytochrome c, p53 or haemoglobin are homologous genes.**

**13.** **Homologous genes share significant sequence homology and when expressed, produce proteins that have the same function in all organisms that possess them, and thus supports Darwin's theory of descent with modification.**

**14.** **Nucleotide sequences in ancestral genes were modified due to accumulation of mutations that occurred over many generations that were selected for.**

**15.** **The greater the sequence similarity between homologous genes, the more closely related the 2 species are.**

**16.** **Quality of written communication: 1 anatomical and 1 molecular homology.**

**17.** Scope note on point 8. **Vestigial structures are not in the learning outcomes. The marking points are reproduced here because the scheme awards them, but 9477's LO 4(g) names only **biochemical data (molecular homologies)**, **the fossil record (anatomical homologies)** and **biogeography (supported by Wallace's findings)**. Under 9477 the three lines of evidence, not the vestigial argument, are what the outcome asks for.

**Source:** EJC Prelim 2022 P3 Q5(a), Section B free-response essay. Stem and the whole key verbatim, including the maximum-7 cap on the anatomical half and the quality-of-written-communication line. Point 17 is added by this build and is not part of the school key.
**Archetype:** evaluate and discuss at essay length. LO 4(g) compressed into one sentence is the commonest essay shape in this the papers after the variation essay, appearing verbatim or near-verbatim in EJC 2022, RI 2017, DHS 2015, SAJC 2016, NJC 2014 and NYJC 2014.
**Trap:** giving only the fossil record, or only anatomy, when the outcome names three lines of evidence and an attribution. Reversing homology and analogy: homology means shared ancestry and demonstrates **divergent** evolution, analogy means independent acquisition under similar selection pressures and demonstrates **convergent** evolution. Stating similarity without saying what it lets you infer, since every piece of evidence in this outcome is an observation followed by the conclusion drawn from it, and the conclusion line is the one that corroborates the theory.
**LOs:** 4(g) throughout, both the molecular and the anatomical halves. 4(f)'s **descent with modification** is the phrase the whole essay is built to reach, and it appears in points 2, 9 and 13.
**Concepts fused:** (1) homology defined by ancestry rather than by appearance; (2) the pentadactyl limb as the named anatomical case; (3) same arrangement, different function, as the observation; (4) modification by different selection pressures as the inference; (5) the universal chemistry argument behind molecular homology; (6) cytochrome c and haemoglobin as the named comparison molecules; (7) homologous proteins performing the same function in different species; (8) mutation accumulation over generations as the source of sequence difference; (9) the similarity-to-relatedness inference; (10) descent with modification as the conclusion both halves converge on.

### Question 8

*In eukaryotes, a gene mutation can give rise to a new allele. Outline how the frequency of a gene mutation can increase in a population. Consider both prokaryotes and eukaryotes.* **[10]**

**1.** Prokaryotes. The gene passes **from parent to offspring via binary fission**.

**2.** **Semi-conservative DNA replication ensures offspring are genetically identical to parents.**

**3.** **Competent cell transformed during uptake of naked piece of DNA containing the mutated gene.**

**4.** **With help of bacteriophages, that mistakenly packaged a piece of bacterial host DNA containing the mutated gene and insert into another bacterium during subsequent infection.**

**5.** **During conjugation, when an F⁺ bacterium transfers the mutated gene, found on the F plasmid, to an F⁻ bacterium.**

**6.** **Reference to genetic recombination.**

**7.** **Prokaryotes with the mutated gene that confers a selective advantage in an environment will be more likely to survive and divide.**

**8.** Eukaryotes. **Mutation of the gene should be in a gamete.**

**9.** **Random fusion of gametes allows the allele to be passed on to offspring.**

**10.** **In the event that a trait related to the allele is being selected for, the offspring is at a selective advantage to survive and reproduce, passing on the allele.**

**11.** **Maximum 9.** **Note: points 7 and 10 are of the same concept. Right use of terms (bolded) allows 2 marks to be awarded, otherwise only 1 mark should be awarded.** **Quality of written communication: scoring at least 3 points.**

**Source:** DHS Prelim 2022 P3 Q4(a), Section B free-response essay. Stem and the whole key verbatim, including the numbering, the maximum of 9 and the note on points 7 and 10. Also banked under Genetics of Bacteria.
**Archetype:** mechanism explain-chain at essay length, run once through each of two inheritance systems. This is the full-cycle question of the drill: a new allele appears by mutation, is transmitted, is selected, and its frequency rises.
**Trap:** stopping at transmission. The frequency does not rise because the allele is passed on, it rises because the individuals carrying it out-reproduce those that do not, which is why points 7 and 10 exist and why the scheme flags them as the same concept scored twice only when the terms are used properly. The second trap is direction: the mutation is not caused by the environment that later favours it. The mutation arises spontaneously, **prior to the selection event**, and the environment selects among what is already there.
**LOs:** 4(a) in point 8, the mutation source; 4(c) in points 7 and 10, the role of natural selection; and the outcome-level idea behind 4(d), that what changes across generations is the frequency of the allele in the gene pool, not any individual. Reach-back to Book 4 in points 3, 4 and 5, transformation, transduction and conjugation, and to Book 2 in point 2, semi-conservative replication.
**Concepts fused:** (1) binary fission as vertical transmission in prokaryotes; (2) semi-conservative replication guaranteeing the copy; (3) transformation; (4) transduction; (5) conjugation and the F plasmid; (6) genetic recombination into the host genome; (7) selective advantage in prokaryotes; (8) the germ-line requirement in eukaryotes; (9) random fertilisation as the eukaryotic transmission step; (10) selection acting on the resulting phenotype; (11) the rise in allele frequency as the endpoint shared by both routes.
**Shared marking point, declared:** random fusion of gametes appears once in Q1(b) point 7 as a **source of variation** and once here in point 9 as a **route of transmission**. Those are different marking points in different schemes and the two answers do not collapse into each other, but it is the only content in this drill touched twice.
