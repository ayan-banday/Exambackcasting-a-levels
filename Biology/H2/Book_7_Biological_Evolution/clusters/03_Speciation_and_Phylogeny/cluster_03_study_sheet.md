## A. The biological species concept

**A1. The definition has two halves and both carry marks.** A species is **a population or group of
populations whose members have the potential to interbreed in nature and produce viable, fertile
offspring**, and which **cannot produce viable, fertile offspring with members of other populations**.
Three words do work. **In nature** rules out hybrids that occur only in captivity. **Viable** rules out a
zygote that dies early. **Fertile** rules out the mule. Giving *fertile* alone misses hybrid inviability;
giving *viable* alone misses hybrid sterility.

**A2. What keeps species apart is reproductive isolation.** **Each species has a gene pool separate from
that of other species**, and **reproductive barriers restrict individuals of one species from
interbreeding, or exchanging genes, with members of other species**. Those barriers are **reproductive
isolating mechanisms (RIMs)**, **biological factors that impede two species from producing viable,
fertile hybrids**. One rider matters: **a single barrier may not block all genetic exchange, but a
combination of several barriers can effectively isolate a species' gene pool**.

**A3. The concept's stated strength** is that its **focus on reproductive barriers directs attention to
how speciation occurs, by the evolution of reproductive isolation**.

**A4. The four limitations are half of LO 4(h), so they are four discrete marks.**

1. **There is no way to evaluate the reproductive isolation of fossils.**
2. **It does not apply to organisms that reproduce asexually all or most of the time**, such as
   **prokaryotes and self-fertilising species**, since there is no interbreeding to test.
3. **Species are designated by the absence of gene flow**, yet many **morphologically and ecologically
   distinct** species still exchange genes, forming rare hybrids.
4. **Some individuals of the same species rarely interbreed**, as with geographically separated
   populations of the same **grass frog** species.

## B. Reproductive isolating mechanisms

Learn these in the school's text grouping, because it uses **physiological isolation** as an umbrella and
that is the word LO 4(j) prints.

**B1. Prezygotic barriers act before a zygote exists.** They **prevent formation of zygotes by blocking
fertilisation**, either by impeding mating or by hindering fertilisation.

| Barrier | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Habitat isolation** | **species occupy different habitats, so they never come into contact** |
| **Temporal isolation** | **different mating or flowering seasons or times of day**, or **sexual maturity at different times of year** |
| **Behavioural isolation** | **sexual attraction between males and females of different species is limited by differences in courtship**: song, call, display, colour |
| **Physiological: mechanical** | **morphological features such as size and incompatible genitalia prevent interbreeding** |
| **Physiological: gametic** | **gametic transfer occurs but the gametes fail to unite**: they fail to attract, cannot fuse, or are **inviable in the female tract of another species** |

**B2. Postzygotic barriers act after fertilisation**, and **occur when prezygotic ones are overcome**.
They **prevent the hybrid zygote from developing into a viable, fertile adult**.

| Barrier | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Physiological: hybrid inviability** | **the fertilised egg fails to develop past the early embryonic stages** |
| **Physiological: hybrid sterility** | **the hybrid survives but is sterile**. The named case is the **mule**, from a **male donkey and a female horse** |
| **Hybrid breakdown** | **the F1 hybrid is viable and fertile, but succeeding generations become increasingly inviable**, through **less fit genotypes formed by genetic recombination** |

## C. Speciation, and the four-stage model

**C1. Speciation is the process by which one species splits into two or more species.** It is not
automatic: **nothing in natural selection itself churns out new species**, and external conditions must
be right.

**C2. The four stages.** This model is written for the geographical case, so treat it as the allopatric
spine.

1. **A single population** shares one gene pool and interbreeds freely.
2. **A barrier develops** and **gene flow between the separated populations is interrupted**.
3. **The populations differentiate**, because each is now free to follow its own evolutionary destiny
   under its own environmental demands. **Different selection pressures** and **genetic drift** push the
   two gene pools apart, and **RIMs begin to develop**.
4. **The barrier disappears and reproductive isolation is tested.** If the populations are now **unable
   to produce fertile, viable offspring**, speciation is complete; if they still interbreed successfully,
   **the process halts and a single species remains**.

**C3. Stopping at separation describes divergence, not speciation.** Stage 2 is geography; the boundary
is set at Stage 4, and needs **accumulation of sufficient RIMs, adaptations and genetic diversity**.

## D. Allopatric speciation

**D1. The definition.** It is **the formation of new species when one population becomes geographically
separated from the rest of the species and subsequently evolves by natural selection and/or genetic
drift**. The barrier works because **any physical barrier preventing individuals from meeting prevents
them from interbreeding**, that is, **blocks gene flow**. Note the rider on the two mechanisms: **both
change allele frequencies, but only in natural selection is the change adaptive**.

**D2. Small isolated populations speciate faster**, because **genetic drift causes rapid changes in
allele frequencies in small, isolated populations**, and **the different selection pressures of the new
environment further accentuate that divergence**. It is also why **allopatric speciation accounts for
almost all evolution of new animal species**.

**D3. Two examples.** The **antelope squirrels on opposite rims of the Grand Canyon** is the tightest,
because geography, divergence and the interbreeding test are visible in one case. **Darwin's finches**
is the one a stem is likelier to use: repeated island colonisation, divergence, then reinvasion.

## E. Sympatric speciation

**E1. The definition, and the structural difference.** A **new species evolves within the same geographic
region as the parental species, or in geographically overlapping populations**. The difference is timing:
**reproductive isolating mechanisms evolve at the start of the process**. In allopatric speciation
geography comes first and barriers follow; here the barrier starts the split.

**E2. The mechanisms the outcome names are behavioural and physiological isolation.** LO 4(j) prints
**behavioural or physiological isolation within the same geographical location**, so the answer comes
from the tables in section B, not from chromosome counting. A subpopulation that **mates at a different
time, in a different microhabitat, or after a divergent courtship display** is isolated while standing in
the same place as its parents.

**E3. Polyploidy, at three lines.** **Polyploidy is the condition of possessing more than two of the
haploid chromosome set.** **Reproductive isolation occurs in a single generation**, because the
polyploid's **gametes have a different chromosome number from those of its parent**, so the two cannot
form fertile hybrids. It is therefore **instantaneous speciation** in the same place, commonest in plants.

**E4. The comparison, point for point.**

| | Allopatric | Sympatric |
|---|---|---|
| Geography | geographically separated | same geographic region |
| What blocks gene flow first | a physical barrier | a reproductive barrier |
| When RIMs evolve | **after** separation, during divergence | **at the start** of the process |
| Main driver | different selection pressures plus genetic drift | behavioural or physiological isolation |
| Where it dominates | almost all new animal species | especially common in plants |

## F. Biological classification

**F1. The definition, with the clause that separates it from phylogeny.** Biological classification is
**the systematic arrangement of organisms into groups based on particular shared characteristics, mainly
morphology**. The rider is the examinable half: **it may not take into consideration evolutionary
relationship between species**. That clause is why LO 4(i) and LO 4(k) are separate outcomes.

**F2. Two terms needed to read a question.** **A character is any structural, chromosomal or molecular
feature that distinguishes one group from another**, and **a taxon is a formal grouping of organisms at
any given level**. The ranks run **domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species**, each
**more inclusive than the one below**. Learn the ranks as vocabulary, not as a list to reproduce.

## G. Phylogeny, and how evolutionary relationship is established

**G1. Phylogeny defined.** It is **the organisation of species according to particular characteristics
which takes into consideration the evolutionary relationship between species**, and it **represents a
hypothesis about patterns of evolutionary relationships**, so it is testable and revisable.

**G2. Similarity is not relatedness, so homology must be sorted from analogy first.** **Similarity of
organisms alone does not necessarily correlate with evolutionary relatedness, because evolutionary change
is not constant in rate and direction.** So **biologists first sort homologous features, which reflect
evolutionary relationship, from analogous features, which do not.** Convergence is why an unsorted
similarity count misleads.

**G3. Shared derived characters, not shared ancestral ones, mark the branch points.**

| | Shared ancestral characters | Shared derived characters |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | **present in an ancestral species and in all groups descended from that more distant ancestor** | **novel traits originating in a recent common ancestor and present in its descendants** |
| What they tell you | that the group sits in some larger group, not how it branches | **which populations belong to each descendant lineage**, so they **identify the branch points** |
| Pattern | shared broadly | **nested**, since **each branching event adds one or more of them** |

**Whether a character counts as ancestral or derived depends on the level.** **A backbone is a shared
ancestral character among vertebrates, because their common ancestor had it, but a shared derived
character at the deeper branch point separating all vertebrates from other animals.** The character has
not changed; the comparison has.

**G4. The outgroup method decides which characters are derived.** **An outgroup is a species or group of
species from a lineage known to have diverged before the lineage containing all the species being
studied**, which is the **ingroup**. A character **present in the outgroup is ancestral**; one **found in
some ingroup members but absent from the outgroup is derived**, and it groups exactly those members.
Order the derived characters from the one shared by most members to the one shared by fewest.

## H. Reading a phylogenetic tree

**H1. The four components.**

| Part | What it represents |
|---|---|
| **Root** | **the base of the tree, the common ancestor to all species on it** |
| **Branch** | **each distinct line, representing evolution over time** |
| **Node** | **a branch point where the tree splits**, the **divergence of two or more species from a common ancestor** |
| **Tip** | **the end point of a branch**, a taxon present today or extinct. **All tips arising from a node are descendants of the common ancestor at that node** |

**H2. Finding the most recent common ancestor.** **Trace backward until the branches leading to those
species converge**, and the deeper the branch point, the greater the divergence.

**H3. Rotation does not change a tree.** **Any node, or any combination of nodes, can be rotated without
changing the structure of the tree.** The same phylogeny redrawn with two branches swapped is the
standard distractor.

**H4. Two things a tree cannot tell you, both stock traps.**

1. **Branching order does not indicate the actual, absolute age of a species.** **Unless you are told
   what the branch lengths mean, for example that they are proportional to time or genetic change,
   interpret the diagram solely in terms of patterns of descent.**
2. **A taxon does not evolve from the taxon next to it.** Two neighbouring tips both descend from the
   common ancestor at their shared node.

## I. Reconstructing phylogeny from molecular data

**I1. The core inference, in both directions.** **The greater the degree of similarity in the primary
sequences of two species, the more closely related they are considered to be.** Run the other way,
**more differences means more time has elapsed since the two diverged from a common ancestor**.

**I2. The molecular clock assumption is the premise a limitation question attacks.** Turning a difference
count into a divergence time **assumes that the rate of accumulation of mutations has remained relatively
constant over time**.

**I3. Multiple sequence alignment is named in the outcome.** After sequencing, **the first step is to
align comparable sequences from the species being studied**, and that is **multiple sequence alignment**.
Two facts carry marks:

- **Closely related species probably differ at only one or a few sites**, so alignment is easy.
- **Distantly related species have different bases at many sites and may have different lengths, because
  insertions and deletions accumulate over long periods**, and those **may not align cleanly, making
  comparison very difficult**. An indel shifts everything downstream, so sequences must be realigned
  before differences are counted.

**I4. The method, because the specimen asked candidates to explain their own.** **Count the number of
base differences between each species and the reference sequence, then rank the species by that count**,
on the principle that **more differences means longer since divergence**. LO 4(l) says **nucleotide and
amino acid**, so the same counting runs on **amino acid substitutions** in a protein alignment. Saying
two sequences look similar, with no count, does not demonstrate the method.

**I5. The advantages LO 4(l) asks you to state.** The school lists nine; four carry the argument.

1. **Molecular data is genetic**, with **a clear genetic basis that is easy to interpret**, unlike a
   morphological trait whose genetic basis is unknown.
2. **It can be used with all organisms**, since **all living organisms possess nucleic acids and
   proteins**, including those **for which there is no fossil record at all**.
3. **It is quantifiable**, being **precise, accurate and easy to quantify**, which **allows objective
   assessment of evolutionary relationships** rather than a judgement about how similar two structures
   look.
4. **It reaches relationships that cannot be determined by non-molecular methods such as comparative
   anatomy**, because distantly related organisms still share ribosomal RNA and fundamental proteins when
   they share no visible structure.

One line each for the rest: it applies to a huge amount of genetic variation, reveals the process of
evolution, draws on a growing database, and **different genes evolve at different rates**.

**I6. The one stated limitation.** **Findings are often inconclusive where several taxa diverged at
nearly the same time in the distant past**, because **the differences may be apparent but not the order
of their appearance**.

**I7. Match the molecule to the timescale.** **DNA coding for ribosomal RNA changes relatively slowly**,
so it resolves **taxa that diverged hundreds of millions of years ago**. **Mitochondrial DNA evolves
relatively rapidly**, so it resolves **recent evolutionary events**.

**I8. Cytochrome c is the named worked case.** It is a **homologous protein**, one **performing the same
function in different species**, and the conclusion is that **the number of amino acid residues that
differ between two species is in proportion to the phylogenetic difference between them**. **Horse and
yeast differ at 48 residues; duck and chicken differ at 2.**
