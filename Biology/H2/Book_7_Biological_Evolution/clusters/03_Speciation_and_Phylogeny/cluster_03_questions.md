### Question 1

**(a)** *Explain why orangutans and gorillas are considered to be different biological species.* **[2]**

**1.** `[INFERRED: no official key; the specimen papers ship without one.]`

**2.** Under the **biological species concept, a species is a group of organisms of similar morphology and physiology that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.**

**3.** Orangutans and gorillas **do not interbreed in the wild and could not produce fertile offspring if they did, so they are reproductively isolated.**

**4.** **There is no gene flow between them and their gene pools remain separate.** This is reflected in the differences in their DNA base sequences.

**5.** Wording note. The school's own definition is **a population or group of populations whose members have the potential to interbreed in nature and produce viable, fertile offspring**, and which **cannot produce viable, fertile offspring with members of other populations**. Three words do work. **In nature** rules out hybrids that occur only in captivity. **Viable** rules out a zygote that dies early. **Fertile** rules out the mule. Giving *fertile* alone misses hybrid inviability; giving *viable* alone misses hybrid sterility.

**(b)** *State two limitations of the biological species concept.* **[2]**

**1.** **Cannot be applied to fossils.**

**2.** **Cannot be applied to asexual organisms as they do not interbreed.**

**3.** **There can still be gene flow between species that are morphologically distinct.**

**4.** **There can still be gene flow between species that are ecologically distinct.**

**5.** The outcome's own count. LO 4(h) says **explain the biological concept of the species and its limitations**, so limitations are half the outcome, and the school lists four: fossils cannot be tested for reproductive isolation; the concept does not apply to organisms that reproduce asexually all or most of the time, including prokaryotes and self-fertilising species; species are designated by the **absence** of gene flow yet many morphologically and ecologically distinct species still exchange genes; and some individuals of the same species rarely interbreed, as with geographically separated populations of the same **grass frog** species.

**(c)** *Describe how it is possible to confirm, over a longer period of time, whether B. pratorum and B. terrestris belong to different species.* **[2]**

**1.** **Interbreed both species *B. pratorum* and *B. terrestris*.**

**2.** **If offspring are viable and fertile, they are considered as the same species.**

**3.** **If offspring are infertile, then they are considered as different species.**

**4.** **According to the biological species concept.**

### Question 2

**(a)** *Suggest how repeated changes in climate between the warm phase and cool phase may lead to the evolution of new species and greater species diversity.* **[5]**

**1.** `[INFERRED: no official key; the specimen papers ship without one.]`

**2.** In the **cool phase the rain forest extends to low altitude and the forest on the two mountains is joined into one continuous habitat**, so the organisms form **one population with gene flow between them**.

**3.** In the **warm phase the forest is restricted to the tops of the mountains, so the populations are separated into isolated patches** with unsuitable, hotter lowland between them. They are **geographically isolated, interbreeding stops, and there is no gene flow between them.**

**4.** **Different mutations arise at random in each isolated population**, and because the **environmental conditions and selection pressures on each mountain are slightly different**, **natural selection favours different alleles in each population**. **Genetic drift also acts, because the isolated populations are small.**

**5.** Over **many generations the allele frequencies of the two gene pools diverge**, and the populations become increasingly different genetically and phenotypically.

**6.** Eventually the differences are so great that **even when the cool phase brings the forests back together the two populations can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring**. They are **reproductively isolated and are therefore separate species**. This is **allopatric speciation**.

**7.** Because the **cycle of warm and cool phases is repeated many times**, the process happens **over and over and in many separate mountain groups**, so **more and more new species accumulate in the same region and species diversity increases.**

**8.** Why this part matters more than its five marks. It is the **highest-mark undifferentiated Book 7 part on the whole specimen**, with **no (i) and (ii)** to supply the structure, and the command word is **Suggest**, not Explain, so there is no recall answer. The word **geographical** never appears in the stem; you have to see that a mountaintop forest patch is a geographical isolate. It maps almost exactly onto the school's four-stage model.

**9.** The four-stage model it maps onto. **(1) A single population** shares one gene pool and interbreeds freely. **(2) A barrier develops** and **gene flow between the separated populations is interrupted**. **(3) The populations differentiate** under **different selection pressures** and **genetic drift**, and **reproductive isolating mechanisms begin to develop**. **(4) The barrier disappears and reproductive isolation is tested.** If the populations are now **unable to produce fertile, viable offspring**, speciation is complete; if they still interbreed successfully, **the process halts and a single species remains**.

**(b)** *Name the process by which these species of bees are formed.* **[1]**

**1.** **Sympatric speciation.**

**(c)** *Using Fig. 2.2, explain how an ancestral species might have evolved into the two species.* **[4]**

**1.** **Random mutations give rise to different genetic variation within the ancestral population.**

**2.** **Selection pressure present in the overlapped geographical location.**

**3.** **Individuals with favourable phenotypes, or which have a selective advantage, will be selected for.**

**4.** They have a **higher probability of surviving and reproducing fertile and viable offspring.**

**5.** They **pass down favourable alleles to offspring, increasing the frequency of favourable alleles in the population.**

**6.** **Presence of a reproductive barrier, different breeding season.**

**7.** Quoted from the figure: **different breeding seasons**, with *B. pratorum* worker numbers peaking in June and *B. terrestris* peaking in July.

**8.** **No interbreeding between individuals in the populations, gene flow is disrupted, resulting in the emergence of different species.**

**Source:** 9477 Specimen Paper 2 Q8(a), stem and Figure 2.1 verbatim (a); the key is `[INFERRED]`, and points 8 and 9 are this build's notes from the specimen audit and the school's chapter 7.4 pp.80 to 82. TJC Prelim 2025 P2 Q9(b)(i), stem and key verbatim (b), with the overlapping-distribution sentence carried in from the paper's own Q9(b) preamble. TJC Prelim 2025 P2 Q9(b)(ii), stem, Fig. 2.2 and all eight marking points verbatim (c).
**Archetype:** mechanism explain-chain run end to end, twice, on two different barrier types. **This is the full-cycle question of this drill**: part (a) runs allopatric speciation from one population through a geographical barrier to a tested reproductive isolation and back again, and part (c) runs the same model with the barrier supplied by timing rather than geography.
**Trap:** stopping at geographic separation. The four-stage model exists because separation alone is **Stage 2 of 4**, and chapter 7.4 p.81 is explicit that if the reunited populations still interbreed successfully **the process of speciation halts and a single species remains**. An answer that ends at "the populations are separated and adapt differently" has described **divergence**, not speciation. In (c), the second trap is answering sympatric speciation with **polyploidy**: LO 4(j) names **behavioural or physiological isolation within the same geographical location**, and **polyploid is not in the learning outcomes, so the answer comes from the barrier tables, not from chromosome doubling. The third trap in (c) is not quoting the figure; the scheme has a marking point for the two peak months.
**LOs:** 4(j) throughout, both halves of it, **geographical isolation (allopatric speciation)** in (a) and **behavioural or physiological isolation within the same geographical location (sympatric speciation)** in (b) and (c). 4(d) in (a) point 4, genetic drift in small isolated populations.
**Concepts fused:** (1) reading a habitat as continuous or fragmented off an altitude profile; (2) a physical barrier blocking gene flow; (3) independent mutation in each isolate; (4) different selection pressures driving divergence; (5) drift accelerating divergence in small isolates; (6) the reunion as the test of reproductive isolation; (7) repetition of the cycle generating diversity, not just one species; (8) temporal isolation as a barrier that needs no geography; (9) quoting two peak months as the evidence for it; (10) the structural difference between the two modes, which is when the reproductive barrier appears.

### Question 3

**(a)** *Suggest and explain two reasons why interbreeding was not possible between different cat species.* **[3]**

**1.** Compulsory point. **Different species within the cat family have accumulated genetic differences due to various factors such as mutation, natural selection, and genetic drift.**

**2.** Then any two of the following. **Differing geographical location or barrier leading to no opportunity for interaction.**

**3.** The species **have specific mating behaviours and courtship rituals that are essential for successful reproduction**, and **these behaviours differ significantly between species.**

**4.** **Physical differences in reproductive organs can prevent successful mating between different species.**

**5.** **Temporal isolation occurs when different species have different mating seasons or times.**

**6.** **These genetic differences can lead to incompatibility between the DNA of different species, making it impossible for their gametes to successfully combine and create viable embryos.**

**(b)** *Explain how the reduced production of seeds by the F1 hybrids can act as a post-zygotic isolating mechanism.* **[3]**

**1.** Any three. **F1 hybrids have fewer F2 offspring / reduced reproductive fitness.** **Reject fewer seeds without further elaboration.**

**2.** **Hence they are outcompeted by the parent species.**

**3.** **Parent species or phenotypes are better adapted or more successful.** Accept that the **F1 hybrid has a selective disadvantage and is selected against**, but only if reference is made to the parents.

**4.** Reference to **disruptive selection**.

**5.** **Hybrid breakdown / hybrid line not sustained long-term.** **Reject explanations pertaining to extinction.**

**(c)** *Using Table 10.1 and Table 10.2, explain why hybrid males do not pass on their genes to their nestlings.* **[2]**

**1.** **As 0% of hybrid males produce normal sperm, hybrid males are not able to fertilise any eggs, despite forming pairs with *F. albicollis* or *F. hypoleuca* females.**

**2.** **In pairs with hybrid males, all offspring, 100%, are fathered by other males**, that is, all nests with a hybrid male in the male-female pair were parented by another male of the same species as the female.

The wider trap is losing the prezygotic and postzygotic split: prezygotic barriers **prevent formation of zygotes by blocking fertilisation**, postzygotic barriers **act after fertilisation and prevent the hybrid zygote developing into a viable, fertile adult**. ### Question 4

**(a)** *Explain the relationship between classification and phylogeny.* **[2]**

**1.** **Classification is the organisation of species into groups based on shared characteristics**, and it uses a naming system.

**2.** **Classification may not take into consideration the evolutionary relationship between the species.**

**3.** **Phylogeny is the organisation of species according to particular characteristics, mainly molecular homology, which takes into consideration the evolutionary relationship between the species**, and each organism is assigned a position on the phylogenetic tree.

**4.** Examiner's comment: **correct points required in the explanation are mirrored in the syllabus.**

**(b)** *Describe the principles which scientists use to classify organisms into taxonomic groups.* **[2]**

**1.** Compulsory marking point. **Scientists nest or place smaller groups within bigger groups in a hierarchical manner.**

**2.** The grouping is **based on morphological, anatomical or physiological similarities.**

**(c)** *State two pieces of evidence that can be used in determining the phylogenetic history of a species.* **[1]**

**1.** **The phylogenetic history of a species can be obtained by comparing homologous morphological structures and homologous molecular sequences with those of other organisms.**

**(d)** *State one reason why the genetic classification is a better representation of evolutionary relationships than the morphological one.* **[1]**

**1.** **Molecular methods can be used for all organisms, living or dead.** **All living organisms have nucleic acids and proteins so comparisons can be made**, and **molecular methods can also be applied to dead or extinct organisms as long as DNA or protein can be obtained, for example DNA extracted from fossils.**

**2.** Or, **molecular methods are objective.** **DNA sequences in the form of A, T, C and G are unambiguous, so differences between organisms can be determined objectively**, **unlike comparing morphological structures such as shape, which are hard to distinguish and give subjective interpretations.**

**3.** Or, **molecular methods are quantitative.** **Molecular data can be converted to numerical form for computation and analysis**, so the **degree of relatedness can be inferred by calculating the number of nucleotide or amino acid differences**, and **the data can be uploaded to online databases for retrieval anywhere**, which is **not possible for a morphological method that requires physical specimens.**

**4.** Or, **molecular methods are more accurate in establishing evolutionary relationships.** They **avoid the pitfalls of convergent evolution, where organisms appear morphologically similar because of adaptation to similar habitats but do not share a common ancestor, for example the flying squirrel and the sugar glider**, and **conversely they can establish the relationship between organisms that look very different but are closely related, because major phenotypic differences may rest on small genetic differences.**

*There are many methods to classify organisms. Describe some methods and explain their limitations.* **[15]**

**1.** Method 1, comparing morphological features. **Several morphological features can be visually compared between organisms.**

**2.** **Organisms with similar structures are classified within the same group.**

**11.** Conversely, **structures might look too different to be considered homologous, for example a whale's flippers and a bat's wings.**

**12.** **Evolutionary relationships of organisms classified under the same clade may not be identified based on morphological traits**, that is, **it is not possible to tell which organism within the same clade is more closely related to another by merely comparing morphological traits**, for example **humans, chimpanzees, gorillas and orangutans are all primates but it is difficult to classify further which is more closely related by morphology alone.**

**15.** **The more differences in sequence, the more distantly related the organisms are**, **hence they are classified under different clades evolved from different common ancestors in a phylogenetic tree.**

**16.** **Certain bases or amino acids might be conserved at certain positions; these are likely to indicate common ancestry and place the organisms in the same group.**

**17.** Limitations of method 2. **It is difficult to classify organisms with no fossil records, or with fossil records so poorly preserved that DNA cannot be extracted.**

**18.** **Current technology only allows the comparison of short sequences; whole genome comparison is still not available, so the classification might be based on one or a few genes only, which might not be accurate.**

**21.** Limitation of method 3. **Organisms which are similar might be found on different continents due to continental drift and be mistakenly classified into different groups or taxa.**
