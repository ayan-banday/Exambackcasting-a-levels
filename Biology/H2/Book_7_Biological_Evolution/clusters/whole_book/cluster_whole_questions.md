# Mark Schemes

### Question 1

**(a)** Both.

**Individuals in a population differ, so when the environment changes some individuals happen to be better suited to the new conditions than others.**

**Those individuals survive and reproduce more successfully, so their alleles increase in frequency. With no variation there is nothing for selection to act between.**

**(b)** All three, and the second half of each is the mark.

**Mutation, which produces a genuinely new allele by changing the base sequence.**

**Meiosis, by crossing over between non-sister chromatids at the chiasmata in prophase I and by independent assortment of bivalents at metaphase I, both of which produce new combinations of existing alleles.**

**Random fertilisation, which combines two genetically different gametes, producing a new combination of existing alleles.**

**(c)** All three.

**The statement is wrong because the antibiotic did not cause the resistance; the resistance allele arose earlier, by random mutation, and was already present in a small number of bacteria before the antibiotic was applied.**

**The antibiotic acts as a selection pressure: it kills the non-resistant bacteria, so the resistant ones survive.**

**The survivors reproduce, by binary fission, and pass the resistance allele on, so the frequency of the resistance allele in the population increases.**

Gap check. This is the single most repeated error in the whole book: **selection acts on pre-existing variation and does not create it.** If you wrote anything that has the environment producing the variation, the outcome is not secure.

**Source:** LO 4(a) and 4(c), assembled from the marking points used across HCI Prelim 2014 P2 Q8(c), CJC Prelim 2023 P2 Q9(b) and JJC Prelim 2017 P2 Q7(b).

### Question 2

**(a)** All three.

**Individuals within a population show variation, and more offspring are produced than can survive, so there is competition for resources.**

**Individuals with alleles conferring a selective advantage in that environment are more likely to survive to reproductive age and reproduce, passing those alleles to their offspring.**

**Over generations the frequency of the advantageous allele in the gene pool increases, and this change in allele frequency is evolution.**

**(b)** All three, name plus circumstance.

**A is directional selection: one extreme is favoured, which occurs when the environment changes consistently in one direction.**

**B is stabilising selection: the intermediate is favoured and both extremes selected against, which occurs in a stable, unchanging environment.**

**C is disruptive selection: both extremes are favoured and the intermediate selected against, which occurs where the environment offers two distinct favourable conditions.**

**(c)** Any one.

**Competition for food, light, water or mates; predation; disease; or human activity such as pesticide or antibiotic use.**

**Source:** LO 4(b) and 4(c). Fig. 2.1 is carried as a description because the three-curve figure is standard across papers and no single one is in the archive as a PDF.

### Question 3

**(a)** Both.

**An individual's genotype is fixed at fertilisation and cannot change during its lifetime.**

**What changes over time is the frequency of alleles in the gene pool of a population, so it is the population that evolves.**

**(b)** All three.

**In the founder effect a small number of individuals leaves and establishes a new population; they carry only a subset of the original gene pool, so allele frequencies in the new population differ from the parent population.**

**In the bottleneck effect a sharp reduction in population size, from a catastrophe, disease or hunting, kills individuals regardless of genotype, so the survivors are a chance sample and allele frequencies shift.**

**In both, the change in allele frequency is due to chance rather than to any survival advantage, which is genetic drift.**

**(c)** Both.

**The alleles lost in the bottleneck are gone from the population entirely, and population growth only copies the alleles that remain.**

**New alleles can only be restored by mutation, which is very slow, or by gene flow from another population.**

**(d)** Both.

**Constructive: migration introduces alleles from another population, increasing genetic variation in the receiving population.**

**Constraining: by making two populations more genetically similar it opposes their divergence, and so opposes speciation.**

**Source:** LO 4(d), marking points from RVHS Prelim 2021 P2 Q9(a), NJC Prelim 2018 P2 Q9(b) and School (HCI) Prelim 2023 P3 Q1(b).

### Question 4

**(a)** Both.

**A diploid individual carries two alleles at each locus, so a harmful recessive allele is masked in the heterozygote, which shows the normal phenotype.**

**Selection cannot act against the allele while it is hidden, so it persists in the gene pool at low frequency.**

**(b)** All four.

**HbS/HbS individuals suffer severe sickle cell anaemia and have reduced survival, so selection acts against them.**

**HbA/HbA individuals are fully susceptible to malaria, so in a malarial region selection acts against them too.**

**HbA/HbS heterozygotes have resistance to malaria without severe anaemia, so the heterozygote has the highest fitness of the three genotypes.**

**Because the heterozygote is fittest, both alleles are maintained in the population at appreciable frequency. This is balancing selection, giving a balanced polymorphism.**

Reject **stabilising selection**; the mechanism is **balancing selection**.

**(c)** One.

**Frequency-dependent selection, in which the fitness of a phenotype depends on how common it is, and the rarer form is favoured.**

**Source:** LO 4(e), marking points from SAJC Prelim 2024 P3 Q5(a) and ACJC Prelim 2024 P2 Q9(d).

### Question 5

**(a)** Both.

**It predicts the allele and genotype frequencies of a population that is not evolving, that is, one in which those frequencies stay constant from generation to generation.**

**Its usefulness is as a baseline: if the observed frequencies differ from the predicted ones, some factor is acting on the population, and the difference shows that evolution is occurring.**

**(b)** Any three, name plus reason.

**No mutation, because mutation introduces new alleles and alters frequencies.**

**Random mating, because non-random mating changes how alleles are combined into genotypes.**

**No natural selection, because selection causes some genotypes to reproduce more than others.**

**A large population, because in a small population genetic drift alters frequencies by chance.**

**No gene flow, that is no migration into or out of the population, because migrants bring different allele frequencies.**

**(c)** All four.

**q² = 320 / 2000 = 0.16**

**q = √0.16 = 0.4**

**p = 1 − q = 0.6**

**2pq = 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.48, so the number of heterozygotes = 0.48 × 2000 = 960**

Gap check. The order **q² → q → p → 2pq** is the whole method, and it starts from the recessive phenotype because that is the only genotype identifiable by sight. **Do not forget to multiply the final frequency by the population size** when the question asks for a number of individuals.

**Source:** LOs 4(m) and 4(n). Method and marking points follow School (HCI) Prelim 2024 P3 Q3(a) and School (HCI) Prelim 2023 P3 Q1(h); the numbers here are set so the square root is exact.

### Question 6

**(a)** Both.

**They are homologous structures, sharing the same basic underlying plan while performing different functions.**

**Similar structure with different function is evidence that they were inherited from a common ancestor and subsequently modified, which is evidence of common ancestry.**

**(b)** Both.

**These are analogous structures: they have the same function but entirely different underlying structure, so they were not inherited from a common ancestor.**

**They arose independently because the two groups met a similar selection pressure, which is convergent evolution, and so they are evidence of similar selection pressure rather than of relatedness.**

**(c)** Both.

**Differences accumulate in a sequence over time as mutations occur, so the longer since two species diverged, the more differences will have accumulated.**

**The number of differences is therefore proportional to the time since they shared a common ancestor: more differences means a more distant common ancestor.**

**(d)** One. **Descent with modification.**

**Source:** LOs 4(g) and 4(f), marking points from NYJC Prelim 2025 P2 Q11(a), VJC Prelim 2017 P2 Q7(a) and 9477 Specimen P1 Q23.

### Question 7

**(a)** Both.

**A group of organisms with similar morphology and physiology whose members can interbreed to produce fertile, viable offspring.**

**They are reproductively isolated from other such groups.**

**(b)** Any three, situation plus reason.

**Asexually reproducing organisms, including bacteria, because they never interbreed so the criterion cannot be applied.**

**Extinct organisms known only from fossils, because whether they could interbreed cannot be tested.**

**Groups that hybridise naturally, because the boundary the concept draws does not match what is observed.**

**Geographically separated populations that never meet, because interbreeding cannot be tested in practice.**

**(c)** Both.

**Allopatric speciation, caused by geographical isolation.**

**The river prevented gene flow between the two populations; different selection pressures acted on each, and different mutations arose and drift acted independently, so their gene pools diverged until the changes were great enough that they could no longer produce fertile offspring, and they had become separate species.**

**Source:** LOs 4(h) and 4(j), marking points from MI Prelim 2021 P2 Q7(b), TJC Prelim 2025 P2 Q9 and 9477 Specimen P2 Q8(a).

### Question 8

**(a)** Both.

**Phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a species or group.**

**A phylogenetic tree is a statement about relatedness and shared ancestry, showing how recently groups shared a common ancestor.**

Note what a tree is **not** a statement about: it does not show time elapsed on the tip axis, and **no tip evolved from another tip** — tips share ancestors, which are the nodes.

**(b)** Both.

**The sequences of the same gene or protein from several species are lined up so that corresponding positions fall in the same column; this can be done on nucleotide or on amino acid sequences.**

**The number of differences between each pair of sequences is then counted, position by position, and the pair with fewest differences is most closely related.**

Count **differences**, not similarities, and **state the count**.

**(c)** One of each.

Advantage, any one: **it is quantitative, giving a number rather than a judgement; it is objective, so different workers reach the same conclusion; it can compare organisms with no shared morphology at all; it uses a very large data set; and it is not misled by convergent evolution.**

Limitation, any one: **a small sample of genes may not represent the whole genome; it assumes a roughly constant rate of change, which does not hold for every gene or lineage; and where divergences occurred close together in time the differences are too few to resolve the order.**

**Source:** LOs 4(i), 4(k) and 4(l), marking points from 9477 Specimen P3 Q1(b) to (d), SAJC Prelim 2023 P3 Q1(e) and School (HCI) Prelim 2025 P2 Q9(c).
