## A. The five definitions every other outcome is written in

Mark schemes reject loose paraphrase of the middle three in particular.

| Term | The school's wording |
|---|---|
| **Population** | **a group of individuals of the same species that live in a defined geographic area**, whose members are **capable of interbreeding and producing fertile offspring** |
| **Gene pool** | **the total collection of all genes, that is all the alleles at all gene loci, of all breeding individuals in a population at any one time** |
| **Allele frequency** | **the relative proportion of the alleles of a gene present in a population** |
| **Genotype frequency** | **the relative proportion of a particular genotype present in a population** |
| **Population genetics** | **the study of the genetic composition and variability of a population** |

**The gene pool is the thing that evolves**, and everything below states what happens to the frequencies
inside it.

## B. The population is the smallest unit that can evolve

**B1. Individuals do not evolve.** An individual's genotype is **fixed at fertilisation and does not
change during its lifetime**. What changes across generations is **the relative proportion of each allele
in the gene pool**, and a gene pool belongs to a population. **Thus a population is the smallest unit
that can evolve.**

**B2. The Grants' finch study, and the marks are in the contrast.** In the 1977 drought on Daphne Major
the small soft seeds ran out and only **large hard seeds** remained. Birds with **deeper, stronger beaks**
could crack them, survived and bred, and **the mean beak depth of the population increased in one
generation**. The mark-bearing sentence is what did **not** happen: **no individual bird's beak grew
larger during the drought**. Writing that the finches evolved larger beaks answers the outcome wrongly.

**B3. Microevolution is that change, named.** It is **a change in allele or genotype frequencies in the
gene pool of a population over generations**.

---

## C. Three processes change allele frequencies, and only one of them adapts

**C1. Natural selection is the only mechanism that consistently leads to adaptive evolution**, because it
is the only one whose direction is set by the environment rather than by chance or by migration.

**C2. Genetic drift is change by chance alone.** It is **the random change of allele and genotype
frequencies, as a result of chance alone, which can differ from generation to generation in a small gene
pool**. Four points define it, each a separate mark:

1. **Drift is significant only in small populations.** Chance events occur at every size but **alter
   allele frequencies substantially only in small ones**, because losing one or two individuals from a
   small sample removes a large share of the alleles.
2. **Drift changes allele frequencies at random.** Natural selection **in a given environment
   consistently favours some alleles over others**; drift has no direction.
3. **Drift leads to loss of genetic variation within populations and creates genetic divergence between
   populations.**
4. **Drift can cause harmful alleles to become fixed**, since alleles fixed by drift are **as likely to
   be harmful as beneficial or of no effect**. Nothing about fitness is doing the choosing.

**C3. Founder effect and bottleneck effect are both drift.** The difference is what happened to the rest
of the original population.

| | **Founder effect** | **Bottleneck effect** |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | **one or a few individuals colonise a habitat isolated from their place of origin** | **natural disasters, diseases or predators kill large numbers of individuals**, causing a **drastic short-term reduction in population size** |
| Why frequencies shift | **the alleles the founders carry become a significant fraction of the gene pool** | **the survivors represent a small random portion of the original gene pool**, so alleles may be **under-represented, over-represented or eliminated** |
| The original population | continues to exist elsewhere | is the one that crashed |
| Lasting cost | a gene pool skewed from the outset | **even when the population recovers its original size, a portion of its genetic diversity remains lost** |
| Named case | the Amish and **Ellis-van Creveld syndrome** | the **greater prairie chicken** of Illinois |

Answering a bottleneck question with *the population got smaller* stops at the cause. The marks are that
survivors are a **random** sample, that **diversity is lost**, and that **the loss persists after
recovery**.

**C4. Gene flow moves alleles between populations.** It is **the movement of genes or alleles from one
population to another** by **the migration of fertile individuals and breeding in that new population**,
and it **tends to reduce differences between populations that have accumulated as a result of natural
selection or genetic drift**. It cuts two ways:

- **Constructive**, when it **spreads a beneficial mutation that arises in one population to other
  populations**.
- **Constraining**, when it **impedes adaptation within a population by the continual flow of inferior
  alleles from other populations**. **Copper-tolerant bent grass** on mine tailings is the case:
  **wind-blown pollen carrying non-tolerant alleles** keeps arriving, so tolerance never reaches 100% on
  the mine despite strong selection for it, which is gene flow **opposing** natural selection.

## D. How genetic variation, including harmful recessive alleles, is preserved

**D1. Diploidy hides recessive alleles from selection.** Because **most eukaryotes are diploid, a
considerable amount of genetic variation is hidden from selection in the form of recessive alleles**.
Alleles **less favourable than their dominant counterparts, or even harmful in the prevailing
environment, can persist because they are propagated in heterozygous individuals**. **This latent
variation is exposed to natural selection only when both parents carry the same recessive allele and
combine two copies in one zygote**, which **happens only rarely if the frequency of the recessive allele
is very low**. So **the rarer the recessive allele, the greater the degree of protection from natural
selection.** LO 4(e) prints the bracket **including harmful recessive alleles**, so lead with this.

**D2. Polymorphism, and the selection that balances it.** **Polymorphism** is the condition in which a
species has **two or more genetically distinct forms contained within the same interbreeding
population**. **Balancing selection occurs when natural selection maintains stable frequencies of two or
more phenotypic forms in a population, a state called balanced polymorphism.** Two mechanisms produce it,
**heterozygote advantage and frequency-dependent selection**, and both **maintain genetic variation**.

**D3. Heterozygote advantage is defined by genotype.** **If individuals who are heterozygous at a
particular gene locus have greater fitness than the homozygotes, they exhibit heterozygote advantage**,
and the school adds that it is **defined in terms of genotype, not phenotype**. Because **both homozygous
conditions have lower fitness than the heterozygote**, the result is **the stable coexistence of both
alleles in the population**.

**D4. Sickle cell is the worked case, and the answer is two opposing selection pressures.** In malarial
regions, individuals **homozygous for the sickle-cell allele** suffer **sickle-cell anaemia** and are
strongly selected against; individuals **homozygous for the normal allele** have **no protection against
malaria** and are strongly selected against; and **heterozygotes are fitter than either homozygote**,
being **protected against malaria without suffering severe anaemia**. **The action of the two opposing
selection pressures thus retains the sickle-cell allele in the human population**. The supporting
evidence is that the allele
is **most common exactly where malaria is widespread**.

> **This is filed wrongly in your notes and the correction is worth a whole outcome.** Chapter 7.3 page
> 53 teaches sickle cell heterozygote advantage under **stabilising selection**. Page 67 of the same
> chapter then lists **stabilising selection among the processes that reduce genetic variation** and
> **heterozygote advantage among those that maintain it**. Both cannot be true. **Stabilising selection
> eliminates both extremes and reduces variance; heterozygote advantage holds two alleles at stable
> frequencies and maintains variation**, which is the opposite job. It is **balancing selection**. Learn
> it from pages 67 to 68, not from page 53.

**D5. Frequency-dependent selection is the second maintaining mechanism.** It is **a type of balancing
selection that maintains two different phenotypic forms in a population**, in which **the relative
fitness of different genotypes is related to how frequently they occur**, so **the fitness of a genotype
declines if it becomes too common**. Rarity is itself the advantage, so neither form is driven out. The **scale-eating cichlids** of Lake Tanganyika, with jaws twisted left or right, are the case.

**D6. Sort the processes by what they do to variation.** This is your notes' own page 67 list.

| **Reduce genetic variation** | **Maintain genetic variation** |
|---|---|
| genetic drift | diploidy |
| stabilising selection | balanced polymorphism, by heterozygote advantage |
| directional selection | and by frequency-dependent selection |

**D7. Sickle cell is examinable in two Books with two different answers.** Under Book 2's LO 2(l) on gene
mutations causing disease, the answer is **the base substitution in the beta-globin gene, glutamic acid
replaced by valine, and the sickling of red blood cells**. Under Book 7's LO 4(e) it is **the two
opposing selection pressures**.

## E. The Hardy-Weinberg model, and the conditions it operates in

**E1. What the model claims.** The **Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium is a principle stating that the genetic
variation in a population will remain constant from one generation to the next in the absence of
evolutionary mechanisms**, so that **the frequencies of alleles and genotypes remain constant provided
only Mendelian segregation and recombination are at work**.

**E2. It describes a population that does not exist.** The model **describes a hypothetical population
that is not evolving**, **an idealised state which rarely occurs in reality because disruptive factors,
such as natural selection, commonly occur in nature**. Its value is that **in real populations allele and
genotype frequencies do change over time, and the equilibrium provides a benchmark against which to
measure such changes**. Treat it as a null expectation to compare against, never as a description of a real
population.

**E3. Hardy-Weinberg is not about individuals.** It is **not concerned with the genotypes or phenotypes
of individuals but with the composition of the entire gene pool**.

**E4. The five conditions, each with the reason it is needed.** LO 4(m) asks for the model **and the
conditions/assumptions it operates in**, so five bare names are half an answer.

| Condition | Why the model needs it |
|---|---|
| **The population is large** | **changes in gene frequencies of large populations are less likely to occur by chance**; in a small population **the random loss of one or more individual genotypes can eliminate one or more alleles** |
| **Random mating occurs** | **there is no tendency for certain genotypes to mate with other specific genotypes**; if individuals **preferentially choose mates with certain genotypes, including close relatives, random fertilisation of gametes does not occur** |
| **No gene flow**, so individuals do not migrate in or out | **the net movement of individuals between populations must not be extensive enough to change gene frequencies** |
| **No mutations occur** | mutations **modify the gene pool by introducing or removing genes from chromosomes, or by changing one allele into another** |
| **No natural selection occurs** | **all genotypes have equal fitness** and **reproduce equally well**, so **the number of offspring is independent of genotype** |

**E5. What a deviation means.** Since the equilibrium **assumes that no evolutionary forces are acting on
a population, deviations from it suggest that one or more of the five conditions is not occurring**, and
therefore that **the population is evolving**. Say which condition is broken and which process is acting.
That inference makes the model a test rather than a formula.

**E6. Three applications the school names.** **Estimating the frequency of a disease allele and the
number of carriers**; **assessing the genetic health of a small or fragmented population**; and
**detecting selection favouring heterozygotes**, where observed heterozygotes exceed predicted.

## F. The Hardy-Weinberg equation

**F1. The two symbols, and the convention that never varies.** For a gene with **two alleles**, **p is
the frequency of the dominant allele** and **q is the frequency of the recessive allele**. Reversing them
breaks everything after it.

**F2. The allele equation.** These are the only two alleles, so their frequencies account for **the total
genetic complement** and sum to one:

**p + q = 1**, so **p = 1 − q** and **q = 1 − p**

**F3. The genotype equation, and where it comes from.** Each individual carries two alleles drawn at
random from the pool under random mating, so the genotype frequencies are the terms of the square of the
allele equation. The school prints the composite form:

**(p + q)² = p² + 2pq + q² = 1**

| Term | The genotype whose frequency it gives |
|---|---|
| **p²** | **homozygous dominant (AA)** |
| **2pq** | **heterozygous (Aa)**, doubled because **the dominant allele may come from either parent** |
| **q²** | **homozygous recessive (aa)** |

**F4. The route in, and why it is always the same one.** **The homozygous recessive is the only genotype
readable directly from a phenotype**, because a recessive phenotype has one possible genotype while a
dominant phenotype has two. So: **the frequency of the recessive phenotype gives q² → square-root it for
q → p = 1 − q → then p² and 2pq follow.**

**F5. Read what the question asks for.** LO 4(n) names **the frequencies of alleles, genotypes and
phenotypes**, three different outputs. An allele frequency is p or q. A genotype frequency is p², 2pq or
q². A phenotype frequency groups genotypes, so **the frequency of the dominant phenotype is p² + 2pq**.
If a **number** of individuals is wanted, **multiply the frequency by the population size**.

## G. The five conditions and the microevolutionary processes are one list

Each condition is the absence of one process from section C or D, which turns the model into a
diagnostic.

| Condition violated | Process therefore acting |
|---|---|
| Population is not large | genetic drift, including founder and bottleneck effects |
| Mating is not random | inbreeding or other non-random mating |
| Migration occurs | gene flow |
| Mutation occurs | mutation, generating new alleles |
| Genotypes differ in fitness | natural selection |

## H. Biological evolution, and the link from micro to macro

**H1. The definition LO 4(f) asks for is three words.** Biological evolution is **descent with
modification**, elaborated as **the cumulative changes that occur in a population from generation to
generation over time**. **It encompasses both
microevolution and macroevolution.**

**H2. The two scales differ in zoom and in time, not in mechanism.**

| | Micro-evolution | Macro-evolution |
|---|---|---|
| Scale | **small-scale evolutionary change within the species level** | **large-scale evolutionary events over geological time** |
| What changes | **allele or genotype frequencies within a population, over a few generations** | **phenotypic changes significant enough to warrant placement in taxonomic groups at species level and higher** |
| Viewpoint | **seen close up and zoomed in**, a **short-term perspective** | **seen zoomed back, the overall pattern**, the **origin of species and higher taxa** |

**H3. The link is the second half of the outcome, and it is one sentence.** **Macroevolution occurs as a
result of microevolution, and macroevolution cannot take place without microevolution.** The reason is
that **the same mechanisms, natural selection, mutation, genetic drift and gene flow, account for the
genetic variation within populations and for the origin of new species and higher taxa as well**. The
counter-argument, if a question wants both sides, is that **chance events do not care about adaptive
superiority**, so **microevolution may not be the exclusive explanation** for present diversity.
