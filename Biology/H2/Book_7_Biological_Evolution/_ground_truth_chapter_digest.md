> Candidates should be able to:

### 2. Content node map

- **Framing prose** (p.2 to 4)
  - Hardy-Weinberg as the statement that variation stays constant when nothing disrupts it
  - Natural selection as the major driving mechanism, with fitness defined by surviving offspring
  - Populations evolve, individuals do not
  - The environment acts on phenotypes, it does not direct changes in DNA
  - Other mechanisms of genetic change: mutation, genetic drift, sexual selection, artificial selection
  - Sources of evidence: biochemistry, morphology, genetic information, geology, physical science, phylogenetic trees, geographical distribution, fossil record
  - The four interacting factors in evolution: reproductive potential, genetic variation, competition for limited resources, proliferation of those that survive and reproduce better
  - Adaptation as the accumulation of favourable genetic changes; extinction when change is too fast or drastic
- **Official Content list and Learning Outcomes** (p.5)
- **Textbook references and relevant websites** (p.6)
- **Book outline** (p.7)
- **Section 1, Definition of Biological Evolution** (p.8 to 9)
  - Biological evolution: descent with modification, cumulative change in a population over generations, encompassing both microevolution and macroevolution
  - Microevolution: small-scale change within the species level, driven by changes in allele or genotype frequencies over a few generations, the short-term zoomed-in view
  - Macroevolution: large-scale change over geological time, phenotypic change big enough to warrant placement in taxonomic groups at species level and higher, the long-term zoomed-out view

Every other outline reference I spot-checked (sections 2, 3, 4, 5, and subsections 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.4.2, 4.5, 5.1) is correct. `[SCHOOL]`

**5.2 The chapter names the discoverer inconsistently.** Page 2 prints "Godfrey Hardy"; chapter 7.3 page 37 prints "Godfery H. Hardy". The correct name is Godfrey Harold Hardy, so chapter 7.3 carries a typographical error in the surname's first name. `[INFERRED]` Trivial, and neither spelling appears in the syllabus, which never names either scientist outside the compound term.

**5.3 Page 2 says the equilibrium was derived "In the early 20th Century" without a year; chapter 7.3 page 37 gives 1908.** No contradiction, but if a date is wanted, 1908 is the one the Book states.

Beyond these, the chapter is factually sound. The microevolution and macroevolution definitions, the fitness definition, the populations-not-individuals point and the environment-acts-on-phenotypes point are all correct and are all directly examinable.

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 A dropped negative on page 17 reverses the meaning of a bullet, and the reversed version is wrong.** The Modern Synthesis section prints:

> "Selection for the favourable phenotypes over the unfavourable ones **does immediately lead to evolution** but NOT generation of a new species yet."

Compare the parallel statement on page 15, in the Darwin section, which is correct: "Over many generations, the proportion of individuals possessing the advantageous traits increases whereas the proportion of those lacking the characteristics decreases, leading to evolution of the population." And page 18: "over a long period of time, it could lead to evolutionary changes." Selection does not lead **immediately** to evolution; it changes allele frequencies over generations. The most likely reading is that a "not" or a "not always" has been lost from the page 17 sentence. Either way the sentence as printed contradicts pages 15 and 18. `[SCHOOL]` on both statements, `[INFERRED]` on the diagnosis.

**5.2 The marsupial and placental passage on page 34 is spliced from a different example and is ungrammatical as printed.** The text reads:

> "To an astonishing degree, marsupials resemble the placental mammals living today on the other continents. Genetic and fossil evidence indicate that the last common ancestor of these two animals lived 140 million years ago. And in fact, that ancestor for and most of its descendants were not mole-like. **5.3 The first-mammal dates on page 33 and the common-ancestor date on page 34 sit awkwardly together.** Page 33: "The first mammals arose approximately 225 million years ago... **The Hardy-Weinberg equation, exactly as the school prints it.** Figure 3.3 on page 40 is an image and does not extract as text, so it was rendered and read visually. The equation as printed is:

> **(p + q)² = p² + 2pq + q² = 1**

with two labelled columns beneath it:

> **Frequency of allele types:** p = Frequency of allele A; q = Frequency of allele a
> **Frequency of allele combinations:** p² = Frequency of AA (homozygous dominant); 2pq = Frequency of Aa (heterozygous); q² = Frequency of aa (homozygous recessive)

and an italic note:

> "The Hardy-Weinberg equation is applied to populations with a simple genetic situation: dominant and recessive alleles controlling a single trait. The frequency of all of the dominant (A) and recessive alleles (a) equals the total genetic complement, and adds up to 1 or 100% of the alleles present."

Page 41 prints the allele-frequency relation separately, in text:

> "If there are only two alleles for a particular gene locus, and the frequency of only one of them is known, the frequency for the other can be calculated using the formula: **p + q = 1**, then **p = 1 – q** and **q = 1 - p**"

**Two conventions to fix now, because mark schemes here reject near-misses.** First, **p is the dominant allele and q is the recessive allele**, always, in this Book. Second, the school writes the composite form **(p + q)² = p² + 2pq + q² = 1**, which is algebraically the same as the syllabus's form but shows where the genotype equation comes from. The syllabus itself prints only `p2 + 2pq + q2 = 1`, in the Mathematical Requirements on page 25. `[OFFICIAL]` A candidate should be able to write both lines, `p + q = 1` and `p² + 2pq + q² = 1`, from memory, and **9477 Specimen and prelim practice both supply them in the stem**, so memorising them is cheap insurance rather than a requirement.

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**This is the chapter with the real problems, and one of them sits on an examined distinction.**

**5.1 Sickle cell and heterozygote advantage are filed under stabilising selection, and the chapter's own page 67 says that is the wrong category.** This is the most serious conceptual error in the Book.

- Page 53, heading: "NATURAL SELECTION: (B) STABILIZING SELECTION ... **EXAMPLE 2: SICKLE-CELL ANAEMIA (SDL)**"
- Page 42, cross-reference: "it could suggest that natural selection is favouring heterozygotes (**see section 3.4.1 (B) Stabilizing selection** for an example of heterozygote advantage in the context of sickle cell anemia in malaria-prone regions)."
- Page 67, the chapter's own contradicting statement: "the following processes tend to **reduce** genetic variation within populations: (a) genetic drift, (b) **stabilizing selection** (c) directional selection."
- Page 67, the correct category: "Balancing selection occurs when natural selection **maintains stable frequencies** of two or more phenotypic forms in a population, a state called BALANCED POLYMORPHISM. **HETEROZYGOTE ADVANTAGE** and FREQUENCY-DEPENDENT SELECTION are mechanisms that preserve balanced polymorphism and **maintain genetic variation**."

Stabilising selection reduces variance by eliminating both extremes. Heterozygote advantage does the opposite: it maintains two alleles at stable frequencies, which is why it is the standard answer to LO 4(e), "explain how genetic variation (including harmful recessive alleles) may be preserved in a natural population". Filing it under stabilising selection makes it an example of the mechanism that destroys the very thing it preserves. **Learn sickle cell as balancing selection and heterozygote advantage, from pages 67 to 68, not from page 53.** `[SCHOOL]` on both conflicting placements, `[INFERRED]` on the correction.

There is a defensible reading in which the sickle-cell case is loosely called stabilising because it holds allele frequencies steady, and some textbooks do describe balancing selection as a stabilising force in that broad sense. But the school's own page 67 list explicitly puts stabilising selection among the variation-reducing processes and heterozygote advantage among the variation-maintaining ones, so the Book is inconsistent with itself regardless of which convention you prefer.

**5.2 Mutation is called the only source of genetic variation, and the chapter says otherwise two pages later.**

- Page 45: "**beneficial mutations are the only source of genetic variation that makes evolution possible**."
- Page 47: "The combined effects of these three mechanisms ensure that sexual reproduction rearranges existing alleles into fresh combinations each generation, providing **much of the genetic variation that makes evolution possible**."
- Page 44, the chapter's own three-source list: mutations to generate new alleles, crossing over and independent assortment, and sexual reproduction, with the second and third bracketed together as generating "new combinations of existing alleles".

Mutation is the only source of **new alleles**. It is not the only source of genetic variation, and page 45's sentence as printed contradicts pages 44 and 47. **This matters because LO 4(a) names three sources by name: "mutation, meiosis and sexual reproduction".** `[OFFICIAL]` An answer that gives only mutation cannot score three marks on the outcome's own wording. Write "mutation is the only source of new alleles", never "the only source of variation". `[SCHOOL]` on the error, `[OFFICIAL]` on the correction, since LO 4(a) settles it.

**5.3 The peppered moth timeline is wrong by roughly a century.** Page 50 sets out three columns: "Before industrialization (before 1940s)", "During industrialization (1940s to 1950s)", "Post-industrialization (from 1950s onwards)". The Industrial Revolution in Britain ran through the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the melanic form of *Biston betularia* was first recorded in Manchester in 1848, and it reached close to fixation in polluted industrial areas by the 1890s. Kettlewell's release-recapture experiments were carried out in the 1950s and the Clean Air Act was passed in 1956, so only the third column's date is right, and the chapter's own Figure 3.14 is captioned around the Clean Air Act. `[INFERRED]` on the correction. **The nine advantages of molecular methods, page 129, verbatim headline clauses.** This is the single highest-value block in chapter 7.5, because LO 4(l) says "**state the advantages of molecular methods**".

> 1. "Molecular data is genetic... Nucleic acid and amino acid sequence variation has a clear genetic basis that is easy to interpret."
> 2. "Molecular methods can be used with all organisms. All living organisms possess nucleic acids and proteins, and so molecular data can be collected from any organism."
> 3. "Molecular methods can be applied to a huge amount of genetic variation."
> 4. "The molecular approach helps us to understand phylogenetic relationships that cannot be determined by non-molecular methods such as comparative anatomy."
> 5. "Molecular data is quantifiable. Nucleic acid and amino acid sequence data is precise, accurate, and easy to quantify, which facilitates the objective assessment of evolutionary relationships."
> 6. "Molecular data often provides information about the process of evolution."
> 7. "The database of molecular information is large and growing."
> 8. "Molecular methods allow us to reconstruct phylogenies among groups of present-day prokaryotes and other microorganisms for which we have no fossil record at all."

Point 8 carries its own limitation, stated in the same paragraph: "Still, the findings are often inconclusive, as in cases of several taxa diverging at nearly the same time in the distant past. Point 9 carries the rRNA against mtDNA contrast: "the DNA that codes for ribosomal RNA (rRNA) changes relatively slowly, and so comparisons of DNA sequences in these genes are useful for investigating relationships between taxa that diverged hundreds of millions of years ago. In contrast, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) evolves relatively rapidly and can be used to explore recent evolutionary events." **This is the reasoning behind 9477 Specimen P3 Q1(b)(ii).** `[OFFICIAL]`

### 6. Load-bearing versus skippable

**Load-bearing:**

- **The five definitions on page 108**, and in particular the pair **biological classification** against **phylogeny**. LO 4(i) defines classification, LO 4(k) defines phylogeny, and the distinction between them is that classification groups by shared characteristics and **may not** take evolutionary relationship into account, while phylogeny does. That one clause is the whole distinction and both outcomes turn on it. `[OFFICIAL]`
- **The four tree components on page 111, as a table.** Root, branch, node, tip, with what each represents. Every tree-reading question is answered from this table, and tree-reading is the largest single archetype in this chapter's share of the corpus.
- **Finding the most recent common ancestor, page 112.** Trace back until the branches converge. One line, constantly tested.
- **The two things a tree cannot tell you, page 113.** Branching order is not absolute age, and a taxon does not evolve from its neighbour on the tree. These are the two most common tree misreadings and the school states both corrections explicitly.
- **Rotation does not change a tree, page 112.** The classic distractor is the same tree drawn with two nodes swapped, offered as a different phylogeny.
- **Homology and analogy, pages 114 to 117, as a matched pair with their evolution types.** Homologous structures come from a recent common ancestor and reflect evolutionary relationship, giving divergent evolution. Analogous structures are independently acquired from similar environments and **do not** reflect evolutionary relationship, giving convergent evolution. The bat wing against the bird wing on page 116 is the cleanest worked case in the Book and it has the subtlety a good answer needs: the underlying skeletons are homologous, the wings are not.
- **Shared ancestral against shared derived characters, pages 118 to 119**, and the point on page 119 that whether a character counts as ancestral or derived depends on the level you are working at. The backbone is ancestral among vertebrates and derived among animals. That is exactly the kind of relative-frame reasoning a level-5 question rewards.
- **The outgroup method and the five-vertebrate worked example, page 120.** This is how a tree is actually built from a character table, and a character table is a standard data stem.
- **The whole of section 5.7, pages 121 to 129.** This is **LO 4(l)**, the longest outcome in Core Idea 4, and every clause of the outcome has a named block here. The mark-bearing content is:
  - the core inference, that greater sequence similarity means closer relationship, and its converse
  - the molecular clock assumption, that mutation rate has been roughly constant, which is what turns a difference count into a divergence time and which is the assumption a "suggest a limitation" question attacks
  - **multiple sequence alignment**, named in the outcome, including why insertions and deletions break it
  - **the nine advantages**, quoted in section 3 above, plus the one stated limitation
  - the rRNA against mtDNA rate contrast in advantage 9
- **Cytochrome c, pages 126 to 128.** Marked SDL, but it is the Book's named worked example of a molecular homology and the proportionality conclusion on page 127 is the sentence a mark scheme wants. Learn the conclusion and one number, that horse and yeast differ at 48 residues while duck and chicken differ at 2.

**Skippable or read-once:**

- **Section 5.2, page 109.** Darwin's 1837 sketch and the note that he never used the word "evolution". Context, not content.
- **The PhyloCode box on page 113**, which the school itself marks "note that PhyloCode is not in the syllabus".
- **The distance matrix machinery on pages 123 to 125.** The idea that you can count base or amino acid differences and use the tally as a distance is load-bearing. The formalism of a symmetric matrix with zero diagonal, and the "how do we find the best tree given these distance data" framing, is not required by any outcome and returns zero syllabus hits. Know that you count differences; skip the matrix.
- **The influenza A examples on pages 124 and 125.** They are counting exercises on a real dataset. Do one, do not study both.
- **The allele-frequency-distance paragraph on page 124**, which is about relationships among populations within a species. No outcome.
- **Clade, synapomorphy, plesiomorphy and monophyletic as vocabulary.** All four return **zero** syllabus hits, as do "cladistics" and "outgroup". `[OFFICIAL]` The biology behind them is examinable under LO 4(i)'s clause "describe how evolutionary relationship is established", so learn the concepts under the plain English names the school also uses, shared ancestral character and shared derived character, and expect not to be handed the Greek.
