## A. The unlinked dihybrid cross, which every other ratio is a deviation from

**A1. Two characters, two genes, two different chromosome pairs.** The definition to use: **a dihybrid
cross is a genetic cross involving two characters in which the parents possess different forms of each
character**, and **the two characters are controlled by two genes, two pairs of alleles, located on two
gene loci on two different chromosomes**. The last clause is what makes the cross unlinked, and it is
the clause a linkage question removes.

**A2. The method is the same seven steps, with a bigger square.** Each parent now makes **four types of
gamete instead of two**, so step 6 needs a **4 × 4 Punnett square with sixteen cells**. The two
reference numbers are **9 : 3 : 3 : 1 in the F2** and **1 : 1 : 1 : 1 in the dihybrid test cross**. Fix
those two, because every mechanism in this cluster is diagnosed by how the observed numbers depart from
one of them.

**A3. Independent assortment is why four gamete types exist, and its wording is policed.** The content:
**the segregation of one pair of alleles is independent of the segregation of other pairs.** The
full-mark version names the stage and the mechanism, which is **the random arrangement of the alleles of
one gene pair at the metaphase plate during metaphase I, independent of the alleles of the other gene
pair**. A scheme accepts **independent assortment** and explicitly **rejects random assortment**, so the
near-synonym costs the mark.

**A4. It fails for linked genes for one structural reason.** **Independent assortment involves the
arrangement and separation of whole chromosomes, and therefore all the alleles that are on them will be
moved together as one entity.** So **genes located close together on the same chromosome do not assort
independently as they are linked or joined together on the same chromosome**, and consequently they
**fail to produce the expected 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 or 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 ratio**. That sentence is LO 2(z) in one
line, and it is worth being able to write cold.

## B. Test crosses, which is a whole outcome to itself

**B1. What a test cross is for, and who you cross with.** The purpose: **crossing the organism of the
dominant phenotype but unknown genotype with a homozygous recessive individual**, so that **the
phenotype of the offspring indicates the genotype of the organism tested**. In the dihybrid case the
partner is **homozygous recessive for both gene loci**, which the chapter calls the **double homozygous
recessive**.

**B2. Why the recessive partner is the right instrument.** A homozygous recessive parent **contributes
only recessive alleles**, so it **masks nothing**. Every offspring's phenotype is therefore read
directly off the allele the **tested** parent contributed. Written as a rule: **in a test cross, the
number and proportion of offspring phenotypes is a direct read-out of the number and proportion of
gamete types made by the tested parent.** The chapter uses this rule constantly and never states it.

**B3. The two read-outs you must know cold.** Monohybrid: **all offspring showing the dominant
phenotype means the tested parent was homozygous**, and **a 1 : 1 split means it was heterozygous**.
Dihybrid: **1 : 1 : 1 : 1 in four classes means the tested parent was heterozygous at both loci and the
two genes are unlinked**. Anything else is section F's problem.

**B4. Back crosses are cut.** **Back cross** returns **zero syllabus hits** in every spelling. LO 2(y)
says **test crosses**, and that is the only cross type 9477 names apart from the dihybrid.

## C. LO 2(x) frames five mechanisms as things a dihybrid problem may involve

**C1. Expect two genes, and expect one of them to be doing something unusual.** Read the outcome
again: **codominance, multiple alleles, sex linkage, autosomal linkage and epistasis** are all named as
things a **dihybrid** cross may involve. So the shape to prepare for is **one sex-linked gene plus one
autosomal gene**, or **one codominant gene plus one ordinary gene**, not the single-gene versions
Cluster 1 used to introduce them.

**C2. When the question gives you symbols, use those and no others.** 9477 Specimen Paper 2 Question 4
supplied them outright: **A purple, a yellow, B smooth, b shrunken**. The corpus reject lines are
blunt: **reject different symbols used**, **penalised if the correct symbols are not used**, and, from
a two-gene question, **reject if symbol A, B and O is used to represent gene for colour vision**.
**Reusing the same letters for two different genes is an automatic loss**, and it is the commonest
notation error in a two-gene problem.

## D. Linkage and crossing-over, which is LO 2(z) entire

**D1. Linkage is between genes, and the schemes reject the allele version.** The definition: **genes
that control different characters and situated on the same chromosome at different loci**. A scheme
prints **reject: alleles found on the same chromosome**. Alleles of one gene sit at **one** locus on
**homologous** chromosomes, which is a different arrangement entirely.

**D2. Autosomal linkage needs both halves of its definition.** The reject lines are explicit:
**reject just saying that the two loci are linked**, and **reference must be made to the genes being on
the same chromosome, and to that chromosome not being a sex chromosome**. A separate scheme
**rejects sex-linked** where autosomal linkage was wanted. Both halves, every time.

**D3. Linked genotypes are written in the two-line form, and the notation itself carries marks.** The
alleles carried on **one chromosome** are written **above** the alleles carried on **its homologue**, as
in **LG over lg**. The chapters start using this on page 33 without ever defining it. A prelim scheme
makes the cost explicit: **penalise under MP2 and MP3 if linkage notation not used**, so writing LlGg
out of habit strikes **two** marking points at once.

**D4. Complete linkage means no crossing over, so only parental gametes exist.** **In complete linkage,
no crossing over occurs between the linked genes, so all the linked genes will pass into the same
gamete, resulting in only parental gametes.** With **only two gamete types** instead of four, the
double heterozygote behaves like a single gene: the **F2 gives 3 : 1** and the **test cross gives
1 : 1**, both with **parental phenotypes only**.

**D5. Incomplete linkage means crossing over sometimes separates them.** **Genes located some distance
apart on the same chromosome generally show incomplete linkage and thus can be separated when crossing
over occurs during meiosis.** The event: **at a chiasma, the non-sister chromatids break at
corresponding points, exchange genetic material and rejoin to form recombinant chromatids**. The
consequence: **the separated chromatids end up in separate gametes and, after fertilisation, give rise
to new combinations of alleles in the offspring**, and those offspring are the **recombinants**. Four
gamete types now exist, **two parental and two recombinant**, and there is **no fixed ratio**.

**D6. Recombinants are always the minority, and the reasoning has three steps.** **As crossing over is
a random process, offspring show a majority of parental allele combinations and a minority of
recombinant combinations.** Underneath that sit the mechanistic reasons: **crossing over between two
particular loci is a rare event**, so **it occurs in only some of the cells undergoing meiosis**, and
**even in those cells only two of the four chromatids cross over**. Give at least the first two of
those three when the question asks why.

**D7. Distance raises the recombinant fraction, and that is where this stops.** **The probability of
crossing over occurring between two gene loci increases with increasing distance between the two gene
loci**, so a **larger recombinant percentage implies a larger separation**. You can compute that
percentage from a table of counts in one line of arithmetic, and you should be able to, because a
prelim stem supplies map units and expects you to use them. **Everything beyond that line is cut:**
linkage maps, map units, centimorgans, crossover value as a named quantity, and working out the order
of three genes. Nine different search terms return **zero syllabus hits**, and LO 2(z) asks for the
effect on **phenotypic ratios**, which a map distance is not.

**D8. The arrangement of the parental alleles matters, but its two names do not.** A double
heterozygote can carry **the two dominants on one chromosome and the two recessives on its homologue**,
or **one dominant linked with one recessive on each chromosome**, and which arrangement the parents
were in **decides which two offspring classes are the parental majority**. **Coupling** and
**repulsion**, the Latin names for the two, return **zero syllabus hits** and can be dropped. The
arrangement itself cannot: **9477 Specimen Paper 1 Question 18 turns on it**, since its parental cross
was QQrr × qqRR, so the two large classes are the ones a candidate assuming the other arrangement would
read as recombinants.

**D9. The three-way detection rule, applied to test-cross data.** This single table is the answer to the
commonest linkage question in the corpus.

| What the test-cross offspring look like | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Four classes in 1 : 1 : 1 : 1** | the genes are **unlinked**, on different chromosomes |
| **Two classes only, in 1 : 1, both parental** | the genes are **completely linked**, no crossing over |
| **Four classes with two large and two small, no fixed ratio** | the genes are **incompletely linked**, crossing over has occurred |

**D10. The mark is for the consequence, not for narrating the event.** A scheme prints **reject if only
describing crossing over or the exchange of non-sister chromatids**. So an answer that describes
prophase I beautifully and stops has scored nothing. What earns the mark is that **parental classes
outnumber recombinant classes** and therefore **the expected 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 fails**.

## E. Epistasis, which is interaction between loci

**E1. The distinction that makes epistasis make sense is a counting distinction.** **Dihybrid
inheritance studies two characteristics controlled by two genes**, which is essentially **one gene to
one characteristic**. **Gene interaction studies one characteristic controlled by two or more genes**,
which is **many genes to one characteristic**. Everything odd about an epistatic ratio follows from the
fact that both loci are reporting on the **same** character, so their phenotypic classes can collide.

**E2. Epistasis is suppression across loci, and dominance is masking within one locus.** The
definition: **epistasis is inferred when the expression of an allele of one gene suppresses or inhibits
the expression of alleles of a different gene.** The contrast the chapter draws immediately: **epistasis
is different from complete dominance, which entails the masking of alleles at the same gene locus.**
The gene doing the suppressing is the **epistatic** gene and the one suppressed is the **hypostatic**
gene, and **the epistatic gene can be recessive or dominant in its effect**.

**E3. Every epistasis case is a metabolic pathway with one step blocked, and the enzyme is
compulsory.** The genotype-to-phenotype chain from Cluster 1 is run once per locus. Each locus encodes
**an enzyme catalysing one step**; a genotype that fails to encode a **functional enzyme** blocks that
step, so **the product of that step never forms** and every downstream product is absent too. A corpus
scheme states the penalty directly: **penalise once if no reference to enzyme**. An answer that says one
gene masks the other and stops is the answer that gets penalised.

**E4. The three-move procedure, which is what replaces the ratio table.** The outcome's own bracket
says **knowledge of the expected ratio for various types of epistasis is not required; focus of this
section is on problem solving**. The chapter concedes the point itself by heading its last three table
rows **other ratios not covered in lecture, potentially new scenarios**, which is an admission that no
list can be complete. The procedure that produces every one of them:

1. **Identify which genotype at which locus does the suppressing.** Read it off the stem or off the
   pathway diagram. It is either **a homozygous recessive pair** at the epistatic locus or **the
   presence of one dominant allele** there.
2. **Work out which phenotypic classes therefore merge.** Take the sixteen cells of the ordinary
   dihybrid square and ask, for each of the four 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 groups, whether the suppressing genotype
   is present. Every group in which it is present now shows **the same phenotype**, so those groups
   collapse into one class.
3. **Read the ratio off the merged square**, and state it **with its phenotypes attached**.

Where **two recessive alleles at the epistatic locus** suppress, a group of 3 merges with the group of
1. Where **one dominant allele** suppresses, a group of 9 merges with a group of 3. The moves are
identical and neither needed the number in advance.

**E5. Suppression makes classes merge, and that is the signature you diagnose from.** An epistatic
cross gives **fewer phenotypic classes than a plain dihybrid one**, because two or more of the four
groups now look the same. **Linkage does the opposite**: it keeps all four classes present and skews
their sizes. That single contrast is how **epistasis is a distractor rather than the answer on Specimen
Paper 1 Question 18**, where four classes were all present and merely lopsided.

**E6. Do not volunteer a subtype name.** The words **epistatic**, **hypostatic**, **duplicate
interaction**, **duplicate dominant epistasis** and **dominant and recessive epistasis** all return
**zero syllabus hits**, and the corpus punishes wrong ones: **accept dominant epistasis, reject
recessive epistasis** on a 12 : 3 : 1, and **reject recessive or dominant epistasis** on a duplicate
interaction. Naming the wrong subtype scores below naming none. **Describe the interaction**, which is
what LO 2(aa) asks for, and let the pathway carry the answer.

## F. Working backwards from a ratio to a mechanism

**F1. Reverse inference is a separate skill from forward prediction, and both are tested about
equally.** Forward is **given the genotypes, predict the ratio**, at **13.0% of the corpus**. Reverse
is **given the numbers, name the mechanism**, at **12.6%**, and **9477 Specimen Paper 1 Question 18 is
a reverse question end to end**.

**F2. The decision set is short and fixed.** Chapter 6.0's one-page routing map is the answer key, and
these are its branches: **unlinked dihybrid**, **complete autosomal linkage**, **incomplete autosomal
linkage**, **sex linkage**, **codominance**, **multiple alleles**, **epistasis of some kind**,
**incomplete dominance**, **a class that has died**, and, if none of those fit, **mutation or
modification by the environment**.

**F3. Three questions sort the branches quickly.** First, **how many classes are there**, because
fewer than expected points to **merging**, which means epistasis, and more than expected points to a
**changed dominance relationship**. Second, **are the class sizes 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 or 9 : 3 : 3 : 1**,
because a skew with all classes present points to **linkage**. Third, **do the results differ between
the two sexes or between reciprocal crosses**, which points to **sex linkage**. Three branches carry
**no fixed ratio at all**, and knowing which they are is itself a common answer: **multiple alleles**,
**sex-linked crosses** and **incomplete linkage**.
