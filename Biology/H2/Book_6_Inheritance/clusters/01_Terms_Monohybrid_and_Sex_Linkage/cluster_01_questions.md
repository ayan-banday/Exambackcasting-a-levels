**Eleven questions, 83 marks, 36 parts, one mark per minute, so a little under an hour and a half.**
Every question below is an actual exam paper question. The stem is the paper's own wording. Where a
stem has been edited it is only to let it stand without the parts of the original that came before it,
and the edit is recorded in the Sources block at the foot of that question.

**Book 6 is procedural, and that changes what this drill is.** Most of the marks in this Book come
from executing a genetic diagram to a fixed layout, not from explaining a mechanism. So the mark
schemes below pay you **row by row**: the allele key, the parental phenotypes, the parental genotypes,
the circled gametes, the offspring genotypes, the offspring phenotypes, the ratio. Write every row
every time, even when you can see the answer without one. **Two mistakes in a row scores zero for that
row.**

**Four real pedigree figures, three questions figure-led.** Sex linkage and pedigree analysis is the
largest single concept in Book 6 at 246 of the 1000 curated records, and it is **76% figure-led**.
There is no way to prepare for it by reading. Every figure below is the real image lifted from the
paper the question came from, not a redrawing.

**Two MCQs, both real.** The paper awards one mark for the letter. The drill then asks you to justify
every option, which is where the four choices get audited.

**Cumulative over Books 2 and 3.** Q2(b) needs gene mutation from Book 2 as the only source of a new
allele. Q3 needs crossing over, independent assortment at metaphase I and random fertilisation said
cold from Book 3. Q5(b) needs frameshift and nonsense mutation from Book 2 to explain why one ABO
allele is recessive while the other two are codominant. A Book 2 or Book 3 gap surfacing here is worth
more than a clean Cluster 1 answer, so say it out loud when it happens.

**What the marks reward.** Define every term **inside the question's own situation**, because two
examiner comments in this file punish the abstract version. Keep **gene** and **allele** apart in both
directions. An **organism** is homozygous; **alleles** are identical. Circle your gametes. Put every
genotype under its phenotype. State **sex as well as trait** on the phenotype row of any sex-linked
diagram. On a pedigree, give the conclusion **and** the feature that forces it, because a scheme
rejects the conclusion on its own.

**How to run it.** One part at a time, aloud, answers untouched. Rate your confidence out loud before

**1.** **Gene is a heritable factor or unit of inheritance.**

**2.** **Gene is composed of DNA.**

**3.** **Gene controls a specific characteristic, or codes for a polypeptide or protein.** **Maximum 2 marks for the gene half.**

**4.** **Allele is a form of a gene.**

**5.** **Alleles of a gene occupy the same gene locus, that is, the same position on the chromosome.**

**6.** Alleles **occur in pairs in a diploid cell.**

**7.** Alleles **cause slightly different phenotypes for the same characteristic.**

**8.** **Alleles differ from each other by one or a small number of bases or base pairs.** **Maximum 2 marks for the allele half.**

**(d)** *Define the following term: recessive allele.* **[2]**

**1.** A recessive allele is an **allele that is masked in expression, or masked in the phenotype, by a dominant allele in a heterozygous individual.**

**2.** It is an **allele that is only expressed in the phenotype in homozygous recessive individuals.**

**(e)** *Explain the terms locus and dominant allele.* **[2]**

**1.** Locus **refers to the position occupied by the gene on the chromosome.**

**2.** Dominant refers to an **allele whose characteristic is expressed in the phenotype even in the presence of an alternative allele.**

**(f)** *Explain what is meant by 'reciprocal cross'.* **[1]**

**1.** The **same characteristics or traits are used or investigated**, **but the genders or sexes are reversed or switched.**

### Question 2

**(a)** *Explain how genotype is linked to phenotype.* **[8]** One mark each.

**1.** **A genotype of an organism is determined by the alleles of specific genes.** Accept **genetic make-up with respect to the alleles**.

**2.** **The phenotype of an organism is the physical manifestation or trait resulting from a specific genotype.**

**3.** **And its interaction with the environment.**

**4.** Reference to when **different alleles are transcribed to form mRNA**.

**5.** Reference to **mRNA being translated into polypeptide chains or proteins**.

**6.** **Different alleles will result in the production of different polypeptide chains or proteins.**

**7.** **Different gene products or proteins may affect different cellular pathways, different parts of a pathway, or different functions.**

**8.** **Resulting in different phenotypes.**

**9.** **The alleles present for each particular gene may be dominant or recessive.**

**10.** **Dominant alleles mask the effect or influence of recessive alleles.**

**11.** **Hence only the dominant allele will be expressed in the phenotypes of homozygous dominant and heterozygous organisms.**

**12.** **Phenotypes of homozygous recessive individuals will only be expressed when both alleles are recessive.**

**(b)** *Where a phenotype that neither parent carried can come from at all.* **[2]**

**1.** **Gene mutation gives rise to new alleles which code for new phenotype, characteristic or trait.**

**2.** [INFERRED] The three meiotic sources, **crossing over, independent assortment and random fertilisation**, only produce **new combinations of alleles that already exist**, so none of them can be the answer here; **mutation is the only source of a new allele**.

### Question 3

**(a)** *A factor besides crossing over that brings about genetic variation, excluding mutation.* **[1]**

**1.** **Independent assortment of homologous chromosomes during meiosis I results in different combinations of alleles in the gametes**, giving **2^n possible combinations where n is the number of homologous pairs**. **Reject independent assortment without any sufficient elaboration.**

**2.** **Or, random fertilisation, that is, the random fusion of gametes, results in different genotypes or combinations of alleles in the offspring**, because **each gamete carries a different set of chromosomes**.

**(b)** *Explain how the random fusion of gametes leads to the expression of rare, recessive alleles.* **[2]**

**1.** **By chance, both gametes may carry the recessive allele.**

**2.** The **zygote or offspring then has a pair of recessive alleles, that is, it is homozygous for the recessive allele**, so the recessive phenotype is expressed.

**(c)** *Why the probability for the third child is independent of the first two.* **[2]**

**1.** **Gametes are produced from separate meiosis, where the alleles of the G6PD gene undergo random segregation.**

**2.** **Each fertilisation results from random fertilisation.**

**(d)** *How a plant breeder ensures the offspring are only from the desired cross.* **[2]**

**1.** **Anthers are removed before maturity, to produce male sterility.**

**2.** **Pollen is transferred by hand.**

**3.** **Plants are isolated, or flowers are bagged, before and after pollination.** **Any two.**

**Source:** SRJC Prelim 2016 P2 Q4(c), stem and key verbatim including the reject line (a); NYJC Prelim 2023 P2 Q3(c)(ii), stem and key verbatim (b); IJC Prelim 2015 P2 Q4(c)(ii), key verbatim, stem lightly rewritten to name the couple's two existing children so it stands without the pedigree the original part depended on (c); RVHS Prelim 2013 P2 Q5(a), stem, table context and key verbatim (d).
**Archetype:** LO 2(v), alleles occurring in pairs but only one of each pair reaching a gamete, plus the experimental hygiene that makes any cross interpretable. LO 2(v) has no section of its own anywhere in Book 6, which makes it the easiest whole outcome to skip past without noticing.
**Trap:** naming independent assortment without elaborating it, which one scheme rejects outright; giving crossing over when the stem has excluded it; treating successive children as though earlier outcomes changed the odds, which is the gambler's fallacy the (c) key exists to kill; forgetting that a cross with open flowers is not a controlled cross at all.
**LOs:** 2(v) the inheritance of genes through gametes; reach-back to Book 3 for independent assortment at metaphase I and for the meiotic origin of gamete variety.
**Concepts fused:** (1) independent assortment as a source of gamete variety; (2) 2^n gamete types; (3) random fertilisation as a separate source; (4) two recessive gametes meeting by chance; (5) homozygosity as the condition for expressing a recessive; (6) independence of successive fertilisations; (7) emasculation; (8) hand pollination; (9) bagging and isolation as controls against stray pollen.

### Question 4

**(a)** *State the mode of inheritance of CGD.* **[1]**

**1.** **X-linked recessive.**

**(b)** *Explain your answer for (a).* **[2]**

**1.** If the **gene that causes the disease were on the Y chromosome, females would not get the disease as they have no Y chromosome**, but females do have a chance of getting the disease in the question context.

**2.** **Males only have one X chromosome, and there are no corresponding alleles on the Y chromosome to mask the effects of the recessive allele**, that is, there is **no other X chromosome to mask the effect of the recessive allele**.

**3.** **Females have 2 copies of the X chromosome, so if one copy has the recessive allele the other copy of the X chromosome might still be able to mask the effect.**

**4.** If the **disease were caused by a dominant allele, more females would be expected to get the disease as females have two copies of the X chromosome** and would show symptoms once they inherit a dominant allele on an X chromosome.

**(c)** *Using the symbols A/a, construct a genetic cross diagram to show the outcomes.* **[4]**

**1.** **Key.** Let **X^A represent the X chromosome with the allele A for normal NADPH oxidase**; let **X^a represent the X chromosome with the mutated allele a for NADPH oxidase**; let **Y represent the Y chromosome**. The **allele for normal NADPH oxidase (A) is dominant to the allele for mutant NADPH oxidase (a).**

**2.** **Parental phenotypes: mother not affected × father not affected. Parental genotypes: X^A X^a × X^A Y.** The mark is awarded for **parental genotypes matching parental phenotypes and gender, plus parental gametes circled**.

**3.** **Parental gametes, circled: X^A and X^a from the mother; X^A and Y from the father.**

**4.** **F1 genotypes: X^A X^A, X^A X^a, X^A Y, X^a Y.**

**5.** **F1 phenotypes: not affected female, not affected female carrier, not affected male, affected male.** **F1 phenotypic ratio 2 : 1 : 1.** The mark is awarded for **genotypes corresponding to phenotypes with gender, plus the F1 phenotypic ratio**.

**6.** The four marks are: **the key**, **the parental row with circled gametes**, **the F1 genotypes**, and **the F1 phenotypes with the ratio**.

**(d)** *Probability of the couple getting a son with CGD.* **[1]**

**1.** The **probability of getting a son with CGD is 1 out of 2, that is, 0.50 or 50% or one half.**

**2.** [INFERRED] The denominator is **the sons only**, because the stem asks for the probability among sons; a candidate who quotes one quarter has answered a different question, namely the probability among all offspring.

**Source:** SAJC Prelim 2023 P2 Q5(a), (b), (c) and (d) in full, all four parts verbatim from the question paper and its mark scheme, including the mark-by-mark allocation printed in (c). This is the drill's **full-cycle question**: mode of inheritance, then the justification, then the whole seven-row diagram, then a probability read off the candidate's own diagram.

**Concepts fused:** (1) X-linked recessive deduced from a sex bias in the affected; (2) hemizygosity as the reason for that bias; (3) ruling out Y-linkage; (4) ruling out X-linked dominance; (5) the allele key as its own mark; (6) parental genotypes matched to phenotype and sex; (7) circled gametes; (8) the Punnett square labelled by parent and sex; (9) offspring phenotypes carrying sex as well as trait; (10) a conditional probability read off a completed square.

### Question 10

**(a)** *How many different types of gametes from PpqqRrSsTTUu, if all genes assort independently?* **[1]**

**1.** **Key: B, that is, 16.** No reason is given; the NJC Paper 1 answer scheme is a letter key only.

**(b)** *Explain your answer, and what changes if two heterozygous pairs are linked.* **[2]** [INFERRED: no official key beyond the letter]

**1.** **Only heterozygous gene pairs generate two kinds of gamete; homozygous pairs generate one.** In **PpqqRrSsTTUu** the heterozygous pairs are **Pp, Rr, Ss and Uu**, which is **four**, so the number of gamete types is **2^4 = 16**. **qq and TT contribute one allele each and multiply the count by 1.**

**2.** **Option A is 2^3 and would be right if only three pairs were heterozygous; option C is 2^5 and option D is 2^6, which would need five or six heterozygous pairs.** Counting all six gene pairs as though each doubled the total is the error that produces D.

**3.** **If two of the heterozygous pairs sat on the same chromosome with no crossing over between them, they would be inherited as a single unit**, so the two pairs would contribute **2 gamete types between them rather than 4**, and the total would fall from **16 to 8**. This is why **linked genes fail to produce the expected ratios**.

**Source:** NJC Prelim 2014 P1 Q14, reprinted as RI Prelim 2011 P1 Q5, stem, options and key verbatim (a); the option-by-option audit and the linkage extension in (b) are inferred, because the paper publishes the letter only.
**Archetype:** the MCQ that presents a genotype and asks "how many", which is where LO 2(v) is usually tested. The paper awards one mark for the letter; the drill then makes you justify all four.
**Trap:** counting gene pairs instead of heterozygous gene pairs, which turns 2^4 into 2^6; forgetting that a homozygous pair still contributes to the genotype but not to the count; assuming independent assortment when the stem has not granted it, which is exactly what part (b)'s last sentence removes.
**LOs:** 2(v) alleles occurring in pairs with only one of each pair represented in a gamete; forward link to 2(z), where linkage cuts the gamete count.
**Concepts fused:** (1) heterozygous pairs as the only source of gamete variety; (2) the 2^n rule; (3) homozygous pairs contributing a factor of one; (4) each distractor as a different value of n; (5) complete linkage collapsing two pairs into one unit.

### Question 11

**(a)** *Maximum number of coat colours from agouti × Himalayan.* **[1]**

**1.** **Answer: C, that is, 3.** This is the official MJC key.

**(b)** *Explain your answer, taking each option in turn.* **[3]** [INFERRED: reasoning not in the official key]

**1.** The most informative cross is **C c × C^h c**, because **each parent shows the phenotype of its more dominant allele while hiding a recessive one**, and **albino c is the most recessive of the four alleles**, so it can hide behind either.

**2.** The offspring are **C C^h and C c, both agouti; C^h c, himalayan; and c c, albino**, which is **three different coat colours**, so **C is correct**.

**3.** **A, one colour, would require at least one homozygous parent**, for example **CC × C^h C^h, which gives only agouti**. **B, two colours, is what a cross such as C C^h × C^h C^h gives**, so it is achievable but is not the maximum.

**4.** **D, four colours, is impossible from this cross**, because **only two of the four alleles can be present in any one individual**, so **two parents carry at most four alleles between them and here two of those four are the same allele c**; **chinchilla C^c is not carried by either parent and so cannot appear in any offspring.**

**(c)** *Relative dominance of B, b^M and b^S from Table 4.1.* **[1]**

**1.** **B is completely dominant to both b^M and b^S**, since **any heterozygote carrying B is Normal**, while **b^M and b^S are codominant with each other**, because the heterozygote **b^M b^S has the distinct intermediate phenotype Tonkinese**.

**(d)** *Why no fixed phenotypic ratio can be quoted for a multiple-allele cross.* **[2]** [INFERRED]

**1.** **If a gene controlling a characteristic has three or more alleles, the alleles are called multiple alleles**, but **only two of them can be present in any one diploid individual**, so **which two the parents happen to carry decides which phenotypes are even available in the offspring**.

**2.** **There is therefore no fixed ratio attached to multiple-allele crosses**; the ratio has to be derived from the particular pair of alleles each parent carries, which is why the question asks for a **maximum** number of colours rather than a ratio.

**(e)** *What the pigmented F1 rules out, and what it points to.* **[2]** [INFERRED: this record's own key covers the later parts of the question only]

**1.** **Two pure-breeding albino parents are each homozygous, and their F1 is pigmented**, so **the two parents cannot be homozygous for the same gene**; if they were, every offspring would be albino as well.

**2.** **The two albino lines must therefore be homozygous recessive at two different gene loci**, and the pigmented F1 carries **at least one dominant allele of each**, which is a **gene interaction, that is, epistasis, in which one characteristic is controlled by two genes**. The F2 of **48 pigmented : 35 albino** is close to **9 : 7**, which Cluster 2 derives rather than recalls.

**Source:** MJC Prelim 2016 P1 Q24, stem, options and official letter key verbatim (a), with the option audit inferred (b); ACJC Prelim 2011 P2 Q4(a)(i), stem, Table 4.1 and the paper's own printed answer verbatim (c); VJC Prelim 2017 P2 Q4, reprinted as VJC Prelim 2017 P2 Q4(a) and set again by Physa-snail stems in several papers, stem and data verbatim, with the inference in (e) worked here because the record's key answers a different part (d) and (e).
**Archetype:** multiple alleles, tested mostly by reverse inference and by genetic diagram. Part (e) is the hinge into Cluster 2: the same data that looks like a broken monohybrid is a two-gene interaction.
**Trap:** treating a four-allele dominance series as though an individual could carry more than two of them; assuming a dominance series must be strictly linear when two of its members are codominant with each other; quoting a fixed ratio for a multiple-allele cross when none exists; in (e), concluding "the albino allele is dominant" or inventing a mutation, when the answer is that two different loci are involved.
**LOs:** 2(u) codominant and the dominance relationships; 2(x) multiple alleles as a mechanism a cross may involve; forward link to 2(aa) epistasis.
**Concepts fused:** (1) three or more alleles in a population with two per individual; (2) a dominance hierarchy; (3) codominance embedded inside a hierarchy; (4) the most recessive allele as the one that can hide behind any other; (5) each MCQ distractor as a different parental genotype pair; (6) reading relative dominance off a phenotype grid; (7) the absence of a fixed ratio for multiple alleles; (8) two pure-breeding recessives at different loci producing a dominant F1; (9) the 9 : 7 signature of two loci reporting on one character.
