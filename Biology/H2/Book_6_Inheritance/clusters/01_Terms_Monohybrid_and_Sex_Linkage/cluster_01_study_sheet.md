## A. The ten terms of LO 2(u), in the school's own wording

**A1. Gene, locus and allele are three different things and the schemes police the difference.** A
**gene** is **a unit of inheritance located at a particular locus of a chromosome**, and equally **a
specific DNA nucleotide sequence which codes for RNA or a polypeptide**. A **locus** is **the
specific location of a gene on a chromosome**. An **allele** is **an alternative form of a gene at a
particular gene locus**, **responsible for determining contrasting traits of the same character**.
The sentence that ties them together: **all alleles of a gene determine the same character, but each
has a unique DNA nucleotide sequence, which may result in different phenotypes**.

**A2. Genotype and phenotype.** **Genotype** is **the complete genetic makeup or allelic composition
of an organism**, and in practice **the paired alleles carried by an organism that give rise to a
phenotype**. **Phenotype** is **the physical manifestation of a genetic trait that results from a
specific genotype and its interaction with the environment**. That last clause is the whole of LO
2(bb) sitting inside the definition, so never write phenotype as though the genotype alone fixed it.

**A3. Homozygous and heterozygous describe the organism, not the alleles.** **Homozygous** is the
**condition in which the alleles of a gene pair in diploid condition are identical**, and such an
organism is **true or pure breeding**, so **all gametes produced by this organism will carry the same
allele**. **Heterozygous** is the **condition in which the alleles of a gene pair in diploid
condition are different**, and **50% of its gametes will carry one allele while the other 50% will
carry the other allele**. An examiner comment records candidates writing **homozygous alleles**,
which is the error: an organism is homozygous, alleles are identical.

**A4. Dominant and recessive are defined by when the effect shows, not by how common the allele is.**
**Dominant alleles produce their effects in both homozygous and heterozygous condition**, so **one
copy of the allele is sufficient**, and **a dominant allele masks the influence of the recessive
allele**. **Recessive alleles produce their effects only in homozygous condition**. The remaining two
of the ten, **codominant** and **linkage**, are built in sections E and in Cluster 2.

**A5. Define the term inside the question's own situation, never in the abstract.** Two examiner
comments punish exactly this: most students correctly mentioned **homozygous** but **did not define it
in the context of the question**, and students **lost marks for using back the root word autosome from
the question stem**. So **pure-breeding** is not homozygous, it is **homozygous at both gene loci
coding for the two characters named in the stem**. **Autosomal** is not on an autosome, it is **the
genes for those characters are not located on a sex chromosome**.

**A6. Three words you must know and will probably never be asked to define.** **Wild-type** is **the
most common allele or phenotype in nature**. **True-breeding** is **an organism that gives rise to
all offspring of the same phenotype**. A **carrier has inherited a recessive allele but does not
display that trait**. None appears anywhere in 9477, and all appear constantly in stems.

## B. Genotype produces phenotype through a polypeptide, and that chain is LO 2(w)

**B1. The chain has five links and you are expected to run all of them.** An **allele** is
**transcribed** and **translated** into **a different polypeptide**, which folds into **a specific 3D
conformation**, which usually makes it **an enzyme** catalysing **one step of a metabolic pathway**,
which produces **a different trait**. A different allele means a different nucleotide sequence,
therefore a different amino acid sequence, therefore a differently folded protein, therefore a
different or absent product.

**B2. This chain is the reason a gene can affect anything at all, and it is reused whole in Cluster
2.** Every full-mark epistasis answer is this chain run twice, once for each locus, with one of the
two runs broken. A corpus scheme states the consequence directly: **penalise once if no reference to
enzyme**. Over-learn the chain here and epistasis costs you nothing later.

## C. Genes reach the next generation through gametes, and that is LO 2(v)

**C1. The starting fact is a counting fact.** **Alleles occur in pairs in a diploid cell although
only one of the pair is represented in a gamete.** Everything a cross tracks is which allele of each
pair ends up in which gamete.

**C2. Segregation is what puts one of each pair into a gamete.** **During the formation of gametes,
the paired alleles segregate randomly so that each gamete receives one or the other with equal
likelihood.** The physical basis is **the separation of homologous chromosomes at anaphase I of
meiosis**, so the allele pair is separated because the chromosome pair is separated. The syllabus
never names **Mendel** and never uses the word **segregation**, so learn what the law says and drop
the attribution.

**C3. Fertilisation restores the pair, and it is assumed to be random.** Two haploid gametes fuse, so
the diploid pair is reconstituted with **one allele from each parent**. **Random fertilisation** is
written as its own line inside the genetic diagram in section D, and it is one of the three
assumptions behind any predicted ratio, which Cluster 3 develops.

**C4. This outcome has no section of its own anywhere in Book 6.** It is answered by C1 to C3 plus
the gamete row of every genetic diagram, which makes it the easiest whole outcome in the Book to skip
past without noticing.

## D. The seven-step genetic diagram, because the layout itself is what is marked

**D1. Marks are awarded row by row, not for the final ratio.** Four schemes allocate them the same
way: **correct parental phenotypes**, **correct parental genotypes**, **correct offspring genotypes
for all four**, **correct offspring phenotypes for all four**, with the tolerance printed as **all
correct is 1 mark, two or more mistakes is 0**. On 9477 Specimen Paper 2 Question 4(a), **four of
eleven marks were for the diagram alone** and the stem had already given the answer as 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.

**D2. The seven steps, as the school states them on chapter 6.1 page 8.**

| Step | The instruction | The note attached to it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Define the **alleles** | **Not needed if alleles are already defined in the question** |
| 2 | List down the **parental phenotype and genotype** | Each true-breeding parent has **matching alleles**, TT or tt |
| 3 | List down the **parental gametes** | **Gametes should always be circled.** Each gamete contains **only one allele** for that gene |
| 4 | List the **F1 phenotype and genotype**, using a **Punnett square** if necessary | |
| 5 | List the **F1 gametes** | The two alleles **segregate**, half the gametes receiving one and half the other |
| 6 | List the **F2 genotypes** using a **Punnett square** | Each square is **an equally probable product of fertilisation** |
| 7 | List the **F2 genotypic and phenotypic ratio** | **It is important to show correlation between genotype and phenotype** |

**D3. The lines that go down the left of the page, in order.** These are the words you physically
write, and each labelled line is a place a mark can sit.

| Line | What is written on it |
|---|---|
| The allele key | **Let T represent the dominant allele for tall stem and t represent the recessive allele for dwarf stem** |
| **Parental phenotype** | the two parents' traits, side by side |
| **Parental genotype** | the two genotypes, joined by a lower-case **x** |
| **Parental gametes** | one allele from each parent, **circled** |
| **F1 genotype** and **F1 phenotype** | the offspring of the first cross |
| **F1 phenotype** and **F1 genotype**, restated | the two individuals being crossed at the second step |
| **F1 gametes** | both alleles from each parent, **circled** |
| **Random fertilisation** | written as a line of its own, immediately above the square |
| The **Punnett square** | headed **F1 Male gametes** across the top and **F1 Female gametes** down the side |
| **F2 genotypic ratio** | each genotype with its number |
| **F2 phenotypic ratio** | each phenotype with its number |

**D4. Six conventions inside that layout, each of which a scheme enforces.**

1. **Gametes are circled**, and the school states it with an asterisk. A scheme awards the gamete mark
   **for correct gametes and circling of the gametes** as one condition, so an uncircled row can score
   nothing with the right alleles in it.
2. **Phenotype and genotype are separate labelled rows and every genotype sits under its phenotype.**
   Two schemes reject a bare ratio: **compulsory to see phenotypes, not just the numbers**, and
   **reject 1:1:1:1 with no phenotypes**.
3. **The cross symbol is a lower-case x**, with the two parents side by side on the same line.
4. **Random fertilisation is its own line.** It names the assumption the square encodes.
5. **The Punnett square is labelled by parent and by sex.** A scheme rewards **parental genotypes
   matching parental phenotypes and gender**, which makes the axis labels load-bearing in every
   sex-linkage question.
6. **Step 1 is skipped only when the question has already defined the alleles.** Otherwise the
   **Let ... represent ...** line is itself a mark, and invented symbols are rejected: **reject
   different symbols used**, **penalised if the correct symbols are not used**.

**D5. The plain notation and its three variants, which the chapters start using without announcing
them.**

| Case | Notation | Where the mark turns on it |
|---|---|---|
| Plain single gene | **T and t**, capital dominant, small recessive | the allele key line |
| **Codominance and multiple alleles** | **a shared capital with distinguishing superscripts**, as in **C^R** and **C^W**, or **I^A**, **I^B** and **i** | the lower-case **i** for the recessive ABO allele is where candidates slip |
| **Sex linkage** | **alleles as superscripts on the X or Y chromosome symbol**, as in **X^H X^h** | the phenotype row must then state **sex as well as trait** |
| **Linkage** | **the two-line form**, one chromosome's alleles written above its homologue's | a scheme **penalises under MP2 and MP3 if linkage notation not used**, so one habit costs two marks |

## E. Dominance relationships: what changes when the heterozygote is not simply dominant

**E1. All of these still obey segregation.** Chapter 6.1 heads them **non-Mendelian** while summary
sheet 6.0 files the same three under the branch that **obeys Mendel's laws**, and the sheet is right.
What they modify is **the dominance relationship between the alleles**, and therefore the **phenotypic
ratio**, not how alleles segregate into gametes.

**E2. Codominance means both alleles are expressed, separately and visibly.** The definition:
**a phenomenon in which both alleles are equally expressed in the phenotype of the heterozygote**,
so **the heterozygote simultaneously expresses the phenotypes of both types of homozygotes**. The
biochemical reason is the one that earns the mark: **both alleles code for functional products, and
both products appear in the phenotype of the heterozygote**. Because there are now three distinguishable
phenotypes, the heterozygous cross gives **1 : 2 : 1 in phenotype as well as in genotype**.

**E3. Incomplete dominance is the trap next door, and you need it only in order to rule it out.** It
produces **a blended intermediate**, one phenotype that is neither parent's. Codominance produces
**both parental phenotypes distinctly**, side by side, which is why a roan coat is **separate red and
white hairs** and not pink. A corpus scheme rejects incomplete dominance outright on a speckled
chicken cross, and **incomplete dominance is distractor D on 9477 Specimen Paper 1 Question 18**. The
word appears nowhere in the syllabus. Build the one-line contrast, not a node.

**E4. Multiple alleles means the gene has three or more forms in the population, and any individual
still carries two.** **If a gene controlling a characteristic has three or more alleles, the alleles
are called multiple alleles.** The standard vehicle is the **ABO blood group system**: **three
alleles, four phenotypes, six genotypes**, where **I^A and I^B are codominant to each other, while i
is recessive to both**, and **only two of the three alleles can be present in an individual**. Carry
the six-genotype table in memory purely because it saves working time. There is **no fixed ratio**
attached to multiple-allele crosses, which is itself the answer to a common question.

| Relationship | What the heterozygote shows | Number of phenotypes from a heterozygous cross |
|---|---|---|
| **Complete dominance** | **only the dominant phenotype** | **two**, in **3 : 1** |
| **Codominance** | **both parental phenotypes, distinctly** | **three**, in **1 : 2 : 1** |
| **Incomplete dominance** | **a blended intermediate** | **three**, in **1 : 2 : 1** |
| **Multiple alleles** | depends which two of the three are present | **no fixed ratio** |

**E5. Keep the reasoning behind a lethal allele and drop the term.** The word **lethal** returns zero
syllabus hits and the node is 0.4% of the corpus. What transfers to any problem-solving question is
the reasoning: **when one genotypic class dies, that class is missing from the offspring you can
count, so the denominator shrinks and the surviving ratio is 2 : 1 rather than 3 : 1**. That single
move is worth having because it is one of the residual explanations for a ratio that fits nothing.

## F. Sex linkage, the largest single concept heading in the Book

**F1. Sex determination sets up everything that follows.** The **first 22 pairs are autosomes and are
not involved in sex determination**; **the 23rd pair are the sex chromosomes**, identical in the
female and different in the male. So the **female is homogametic**, making **one type of gamete, all
carrying an X**, and the **male is heterogametic**, making **two types, half X and half Y**, which is
why the sperm determines the sex of the offspring.

**F2. Sex linkage is defined by where the gene sits, and in practice that means the X.** **Sex
linkage refers to the carrying of genes on the sex chromosomes**, and **the genes are mostly located
on the X chromosome rather than on the Y**, because the Y is short and carries few loci. The school
notes that **X-linked** is the more accurate name **because they follow the transmission pattern of
the X chromosome**.

**F3. Hemizygosity is the reason X-linked recessive conditions run overwhelmingly in males.** **A
male has only one sex-linked gene locus**, one copy of the X instead of two, so **he is neither
homozygous nor heterozygous for his sex-linked loci; instead he is always hemizygous**. A single
recessive allele is therefore expressed in a male, while a female needs two. The female heterozygote
is a **carrier**: she **does not suffer from the condition but can transmit the recessive allele to
offspring**.

**F4. The phenotype row of a sex-linked diagram must state sex as well as trait.** The offspring line
of a carrier-by-normal cross reads **1 normal female : 1 normal male : 1 normal female carrier : 1
affected male**. A scheme rejects an answer that **does not represent colour blindness as
sex-linkage**, and another rejects **using the same letters for two different genes**. There is **no
fixed genetic ratio** for sex-linked crosses, because the answer depends on the sexes involved.

**F5. Four transmission rules, and one of them is where your notes go wrong.** **Fathers pass
X-linked alleles only to daughters.** **Sons receive X-linked alleles only from mothers.** **An
affected father transmits the recessive allele to all his daughters, making every one a carrier, and
to none of his sons.** **A carrier mother passes the condition to half her sons.** Note what the
third rule requires: it is the **father's** status that makes daughters carriers, not the mother's.

**F6. The reciprocal cross is how you find out whether a gene is X-linked, and it is the only clean
experimental-design node in the Book.** **A reciprocal cross is a pair of crosses in which the traits
of the two parents are reversed.** The diagnostic: **reciprocal crosses involving X-linked traits
yield non-identical results, in contrast to reciprocal crosses involving autosomal traits, which
yield identical results**. That is a Suggest-shaped answer, not a recall one, and Paper 3 Section A
routinely asks how you would find something out.

## G. Reading a pedigree, which is four decision rules rather than four topics

**G1. A pedigree is family data drawn as a diagram.** **A pedigree chart is a diagram of a family tree
over as many generations as possible showing the descendants from particular ancestors, their
relationships and the presence or absence of the trait in all the members.** Human genetics needs it
because you cannot set up a cross in humans. The word returns **zero syllabus hits** and never
appeared on the specimen, yet it is **12.8% of the school corpus**, because deducing genotypes and a
mode of inheritance from family data sits inside LOs 2(u), (v) and (x).

**G2. Two independent decisions, made from different evidence.** Reading a pedigree is deciding
**dominant or recessive** and then **autosomal or X-linked**, and the two use different features.

| Decision | The feature that settles it |
|---|---|
| **Recessive** | **affected offspring born to two unaffected parents**, and the trait **skips generations** |
| **Dominant** | **every affected individual has an affected parent**, the trait **appears in every generation**, and **unaffected parents never have affected children** |
| **Autosomal** | **males and females are affected in roughly equal numbers** |
| **X-linked recessive** | **far more affected males than females**, and **an affected male's daughters are all unaffected carriers while his sons are unaffected** |
| **X-linked dominant** | **all the daughters of an affected male are affected**, and **a heterozygous female passes it to half her sons and half her daughters** |

**G3. The answer has two clauses and a scheme rejects it with one.** The reject line: **individual
must be homozygous recessive is rejected because recessive is not explained**. The expected shape
names the conclusion and then the pedigree feature that forces it: **autosomal, because males and
females are equally affected**, and **recessive, because it requires both alleles to be defective
before the child is phenotypically affected**.

**G4. Then assign genotypes and, often, a probability.** Work outwards from the individuals whose
genotype is forced: every **affected recessive individual is homozygous recessive**, so both its
parents **each carry at least one recessive allele**. Unaffected offspring of a carrier cross are
**1/3 homozygous dominant and 2/3 heterozygous**, because the affected quarter has been removed from
the sample. A probability for an unborn child is that reasoning multiplied along the line, and it is
the same denominator move as in E5.

## H. Open recall prompts (answer these cold, then self-check)

These are memorisation targets, so they stay as prompts. **Attempt each aloud before looking anything
up** and keep them in rotation until automatic.

1. Define **locus**, **allele** and **gene**, and state what makes two alleles of one gene different.
2. Define **homozygous** and **heterozygous**, and state what fraction of each organism's gametes
   carries which allele.
3. Define **dominant** and **recessive** in terms of the condition in which the effect appears.
4. Run the genotype-to-phenotype chain from allele to trait, naming all five links.
5. State the law of segregation, and name the stage of meiosis that produces it.
6. List the seven steps of the genetic diagram in order.
7. Name the six presentation conventions inside the layout and say which mark each protects.
8. Define **codominance**, and give the biochemical reason the heterozygote shows both phenotypes.
9. Distinguish **codominance** from **incomplete dominance** in one sentence each.
10. State the ABO allele relationships, and give the number of alleles, genotypes and phenotypes.
11. Define **hemizygous**, and use it to explain why X-linked recessive conditions are commoner in
    males.
12. State the four X-linked transmission rules.
13. State the reciprocal-cross diagnostic for X-linkage, both branches.
14. Give the pedigree feature that proves recessive, and the one that proves autosomal.

## I. Errors in your notes, do not encode these

**Read the first one before you touch a pedigree question.** It sits on an examined distinction and
the other three do not.

1. **Chapter 6.1 page 31 calls an X-linked dominant pedigree recessive.** Section 8.4 is headed
   **X-LINKED DOMINANT INHERITANCE**, and its second bullet says Fig. 16 illustrates a trait
   demonstrating **sex-linked recessive** inheritance. The caption directly beneath, the section
   heading, the genotypes inside the figure and the characteristics list all say **dominant**. It is a
   copy-paste of the identical sentence frame from section 8.3. **The caption is right and the lead-in
   sentence is wrong.**
2. **Chapter 6.1 page 25's second broad principle is only true when the father is affected.** As
   printed it says a **homozygous dominant mother** produces daughters who are **carriers**. A
   homozygous dominant mother crossed with an unaffected father produces daughters carrying nothing.
   The rule that actually carries the mark is the bullet above it: **an affected father makes all his
   daughters carriers and none of his sons anything.**
3. **Chapter 6.0 gives a lethal-gene cross two ratios that cannot describe the same offspring**, a
   genotypic **1 : 2 : 1** beside a phenotypic **2 : 1**. The 1 : 2 : 1 is the ratio at fertilisation,
   before the lethal class is lost. Chapter 6.1 page 21 gives **2 : 1 with a quarter of offspring
   dying** and offers no genotypic ratio, which is the correct treatment.
4. **Chapter 6.0 labels AA × aa a test cross.** A test cross is by definition against an individual of
   **unknown** genotype, so if you already know the parent is AA there is nothing to test. The row
   describes what you would see **if** the tested parent turned out to be homozygous.
