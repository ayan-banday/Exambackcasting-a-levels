## 6.0 Inheritance I Overview

1. This one page is a **routing map**, and its job is to take any cross you are shown and send it down the right branch before you write anything.
2. The **first question** you ask of every cross is whether the two genes are **unlinked**, in which case Mendel's laws hold, or **linked**, in which case they do not.
3. The **second question** is how many characters are in play, because a **monohybrid** problem follows one gene and a **dihybrid** problem follows two.
4. Once you know the branch, the cross has a **fixed expected ratio** attached to it, and that ratio is what a question asks you to predict, or to work backwards from.
5. Three branches have **no fixed ratio** at all, and knowing which ones they are is itself the answer to a common question: **multiple alleles**, **sex-linked** crosses, and **incomplete linkage**.
6. When a set of results fits none of the branches, the sheet tells you where to look next, and the two suspects are **mutation** and **modification by the environment**.

## 6.1 Inheritance I

1. Everything in this Book is built from ten words, and **LO 2(u)** names them: **locus**, **allele**, **dominant**, **recessive**, **codominant**, **homozygous**, **heterozygous**, **phenotype**, **genotype** and **linkage**.
2. A **genotype** produces a **phenotype** through a chain that runs allele to **polypeptide** to **enzyme** to trait, and that chain is the reason a gene can affect anything at all.
3. Genes reach the next generation through **gametes**, so what a cross is really tracking is which **allele** of each pair ends up in which gamete.
4. The **genetic diagram** is the method the whole Book runs on, and it has a **fixed set of labelled rows** that the mark scheme pays you for line by line.
5. A **monohybrid** cross follows one gene and a **dihybrid** cross follows two, and the syllabus asks for the dihybrid version, so expect two genes at once.
6. A **test cross** is the standard move for finding out an unknown **genotype** behind a dominant **phenotype**, and it works by crossing against a **homozygous recessive** partner.
7. **Codominance** and **multiple alleles** are the two things that change what a heterozygote looks like, and both shift the ratio away from the plain dominant and recessive case.
8. **Sex linkage** means the gene sits on the **X chromosome**, and because a male carries only one X he is **hemizygous** there, which is why X-linked conditions run far more often in males.
9. A **pedigree chart** is family data drawn as a diagram, and reading one means deciding whether a trait is **autosomal** or **X-linked** and whether it is **dominant** or **recessive**.
10. **Linkage** means two genes sit on the same chromosome so they travel together instead of assorting independently, and **crossing-over** partly breaks that, leaving a majority of **parental** and a minority of **recombinant** offspring.
11. The **environment** modifies the phenotype a genotype produces, and the example the syllabus names by itself is the effect of **diet** on the differentiation of **honeybees**.

## 6.2 Inheritance II

1. A **gene interaction** is the case where two or more genes act on **one** character, which is a different situation from a dihybrid cross where each gene has its own character.
2. **Epistasis** is a gene interaction in which an allele at one **locus** suppresses the expression of alleles at a **different** locus, and this is not the same thing as dominance.
3. The gene doing the suppressing is the **epistatic** one and the gene being suppressed is the **hypostatic** one, and the suppression can be caused by either a **recessive** or a **dominant** genotype.
4. Every epistasis case is really a **metabolic pathway** with a step missing, so the answer that earns marks names the **enzyme** that fails and the product that never forms.
5. Suppression makes phenotypic classes **merge**, which is why an epistatic cross gives fewer classes than a plain dihybrid one, and this is what you are asked to explain.
6. **Discontinuous variation** gives distinct countable classes from **one or a few genes**, while **continuous variation** gives a range with intermediates from **many additive genes** whose effects the environment then modifies.
7. The **type of data** decides the **statistical test**, and this pairing is the cleanest link in the Book: discrete counts go to the **chi-squared test** and measured means go to the **t-test**.
8. A predicted ratio is only a prediction, and it rests on three assumptions worth naming: **random fertilisation**, **equal survival** of offspring, and a **large sample**.
9. The **chi-squared test** asks whether the gap between your **observed** and **expected** counts is small enough to be put down to **chance**, and both the **critical value** you judge it against and the wording of your conclusion are fixed.
10. **Degrees of freedom** is counted from the number of **phenotypic classes**, not from the number of offspring, and that one distinction is where marks are lost.
11. New **combinations** of alleles come out of meiosis and fertilisation, but new **alleles** come only from **mutation**, and only a mutation in a **gamete** is inherited.

## Scope corrections: what your notes teach that 9477 does not examine

All checked by literal search of the syllabus PDF, and then re-checked with every hyphen, dash, space and closed-up spelling, so these are safe to act on. Nothing on the cut list turned out to be hiding behind a different dash.

**Cut entirely.** **Chromosome mapping** is the big one: linkage maps, map units, **centimorgans**, crossover value as a computed number, and working out the order of three genes from three measurements. That is five pages of chapter 6.1 with **zero** syllabus hits under nine different search terms and no outcome behind it. Also gone: the **phenotypic variance** equation, the terms **coupling** and **repulsion**, **lethal alleles** as a named topic, **back crosses**, and stating **Mendel's** laws by name. Mendel is not mentioned once in 9477.

**Zero hits as words, but still fully examined.** **Monohybrid**, **pedigree**, **recombinant**, **polygenic**, **null hypothesis**, **incomplete dominance**, **hemizygous** and **carrier** are all absent from the syllabus and every one of them is live. Continuous and discontinuous variation look absent too, until you notice the outcome splits the phrase across a clause. **Learn the biology and expect not to be handed the word.**

**Three specific rulings.**

1. **Epistasis is examinable and its ratios are not.** The outcome says in its own brackets that knowledge of the expected ratio for various types of epistasis is **not required** and that the focus is on **problem solving**. Your notes give you a two-page table of seven ratios to memorise. Do not. Learn the one procedure that generates any of them, including ones you have never seen, which is what the chapter itself admits can happen.
2. **Autosomal linkage is fully examinable and is the more heavily signposted half.** The outcome names sex linkage and autosomal linkage side by side, and **autosomal linkage is the correct answer to the only clear Book 6 question on the official specimen paper**. What is cut is only the arithmetic on top of it: recognising linkage from data stays, converting it to a map distance goes.
3. **The chi-squared formula, the symbol meanings and the table are all given to you in the exam.** The syllabus states outright that you are not expected to remember them. Nothing about this test is a memory test. What is tested is whether you can turn a ratio into expected numbers and read the table on the right row.

**Why your notes overshoot, measured.** In the Book 6 question bank, **5.1%** of questions turn on recalling a modified epistasis ratio rather than deriving one, and **2.5%** on chromosome mapping. Bigger than either: **288 records** in the Biostatistics bank are t-tests, standard deviations and microscopy, which belong to other papers entirely. Filter before you drill.

## Four errors in the notes themselves

1. **Chapter 6.2 page 9, the Fig. 6 caption says genotype aa gives a white mouse.** It does not. The question stem three lines above and the diagram directly above both say **bb** gives white and **aa** with a dominant B gives **black**. The caption was copied from the Labrador figure on the facing page with the letters swapped wrongly.
2. **Chapter 6.1 page 42, the Case 1 map puts one of its two measured distances on the wrong interval**, and the chapter's own next sentence contradicts the diagram by predicting a different value for that same interval. Only matters if you study mapping, which you should not.
3. **Chapter 6.1 page 31 calls a sex-linked dominant pedigree recessive**, while the figure caption on the same page calls it dominant. The caption is right.
4. **Chapter 6.0 gives the lethal-gene cross a genotypic ratio that cannot belong to the same offspring as the phenotypic ratio printed beside it**, because it counts a class that has already died. Chapter 6.1 gets it right.

**And one collision that is not an error but will cost you marks.** Your notes write degrees of freedom as **df = n − 1**, using **n** for the number of classes. The syllabus and the official specimen paper both write it as **v = c − 1**, where **c** is the number of classes and **n** means **sample size**. Same biology, different letter. Write **v = c − 1** and say **classes** out loud, because an examiner report names using the wrong degrees of freedom as a specific, credit-losing error.
