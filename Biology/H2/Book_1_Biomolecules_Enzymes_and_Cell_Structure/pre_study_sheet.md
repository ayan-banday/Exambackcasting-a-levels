# Pre-Study Sheet: Biomolecules, Enzymes and Cell Structure

Layer 1 = main arguments. Layer 2 = sub-arguments. No detail, no examples, anchor points only.

Exam weighting: 15 to 20% of total marks, 15 to 25 in Paper 2 and 5 to 15 in Paper 3, with no quiet years. On the 9477 specimen, both Paper 3 Section B essay choices came from this Book.

---

## PART A: WATER AND MACROMOLECULES

### 1. Water's polarity causes every property that makes it the medium of life

- 1.1 Oxygen is strongly **electronegative**, so charge sits unevenly: **partial negative on O, partial positive on each H**, molecule uncharged overall.
- 1.2 Opposite charges attract between molecules, giving **hydrogen bonds**: individually weak, **collectively decisive**. The second clause carries the mark.
- 1.3 Properties that follow: **universal solvent** (solutes get **hydration spheres**), **high specific heat capacity**, **cohesion and surface tension**, **ice less dense than water**, **transparency**.
- 1.4 Molecules sort by behaviour toward water: **hydrophilic**, **hydrophobic**, **amphipathic**. Amphipathic is load-bearing, because membranes exist because of it.

### 2. Three of the four macromolecule families are polymers, and one reaction builds them all

- 2.1 **Carbon is tetravalent**, so it builds long skeletons; attached **functional groups** set the reactivity.
- 2.2 The polymers are **polysaccharides, proteins and nucleic acids**. **Lipids are not polymers**, and examiners test that exception.
- 2.3 **Condensation** joins monomers, losing one **H₂O**; **hydrolysis** reverses it, adding one **H₂O**.
- 2.4 One bond name per family, one reaction: **glycosidic**, **ester**, **peptide**.

## PART B: LIPIDS

### 1. Lipids are a solubility category, and the hydrocarbon tails define it

- 1.1 Grouped by behaviour only: **insoluble in water, soluble in non-polar solvents**. No shared monomer.
- 1.2 A **triglyceride** is **glycerol plus 3 fatty acids** via **3 ester linkages**, condensation losing **3 H₂O**.
- 1.3 The many **non-polar C-H bonds** in the tails are why fats are hydrophobic.
- 1.4 Melting point runs through **hydrophobic interactions**: **longer chains** raise it, **C=C kinks** block close packing and lower it. Hence solid animal fats, liquid plant oils.

### 2. Triglyceride structure maps one feature onto each storage function

- 2.1 **Highly reduced**, more C and H per unit mass, so oxidation gives roughly **twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate**, plus **more metabolic water**.
- 2.2 **Hydrophobic, so no water of hydration**: no added mass, **no effect on water potential**, and it works as a **thermal insulator**.
- 2.3 Weak hydrophobic interactions let it **slide under pressure** and cushion organs; **lower density than water** aids buoyancy.

### 3. Phospholipids are amphipathic, and that alone generates membrane structure

- 3.1 Glycerol plus **2 fatty acids** plus a charged **phosphate**: **two hydrophobic tails, one hydrophilic head**.
- 3.2 In water they self-assemble to shield the tails, forming **micelles**, **bilayers** and **liposomes**.
- 3.3 The bilayer holds by **many weak hydrophobic interactions**: strong in aggregate, each weak enough to permit **lateral movement**.
- 3.4 Two variants: **glycolipids** swap phosphate for a **carbohydrate chain** and act as recognition markers; **cholesterol** is a four-ring steroid regulating **fluidity**.

## PART C: CARBOHYDRATES

### 1. The anomeric carbon decides whether a polysaccharide stores or builds

- 1.1 Ring and linear forms are in **dynamic equilibrium**, but the **ring predominates** and is the form that polymerises.
- 1.2 The **anomeric carbon** carries a hydroxyl **below the ring in α-glucose, above it in β-glucose**.
- 1.3 A **glycosidic bond** is a **C-O-C** bond made by condensation from that carbon, named by **which carbons** and by **α or β**.
- 1.4 A sugar is **reducing** if it keeps a **free carbonyl group**; **sucrose is non-reducing** because both carbonyls are locked in the bond.

### 2. α-glucose gives the storage polysaccharides, engineered for three demands at once

- 2.1 **α(1,4)** gives unbranched helical **amylose**; adding **α(1,6)** branches gives **amylopectin** in plants and **glycogen** in animals.
- 2.2 **Large and insoluble**, so **no effect on water potential**, and **compact**, so a large store fits a small space.
- 2.3 **α(1,4) hydrolyses easily**, and **many branch ends** let many enzymes act at once, so glucose mobilises fast.

### 3. β-glucose gives the structural polysaccharide, and inversion is the whole mechanism

- 3.1 **β(1,4)** bonding inverts **alternate monomers**, forcing a **long, straight, unbranched chain**.
- 3.2 Parallel straight chains project **hydroxyls outwards**, so **hydrogen bonds cross-link neighbouring chains** into **microfibrils, macrofibrils and fibres**, giving **high tensile strength**.
- 3.3 **β(1,4) bonds resist hydrolysis**: **amylase cannot cut them**, only **cellulase** can.

## PART D: PROTEINS

### 1. A protein is a sequence that folds into one shape, and the R group decides the fold

- 1.1 Every amino acid has an **amine group**, a **carboxyl group**, a hydrogen and a variable **R group**.
- 1.2 They join by **condensation** into a **peptide bond** between carboxyl and amine.
- 1.3 R group properties decide where each residue lands once folded, so **sequence determines shape determines function**.

### 2. There are four levels of structure, each held by its own named bonds

- 2.1 **Primary**: the **sequence**, held by **peptide bonds**, determining every level above it.
- 2.2 **Secondary**: **α-helix** and **β-pleated sheet**, held by **hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N-H**, not R groups.
- 2.3 **Tertiary**: the 3D fold, held by four bonds between **R groups**: **hydrogen, ionic, disulfide, hydrophobic**.
- 2.4 **Quaternary**: two or more polypeptides associating by those same four bond types.

### 3. The two named proteins are one argument run twice, globular against fibrous

- 3.1 **Haemoglobin** is **globular** and transports oxygen: hydrophilic R groups outside make it **soluble**, four subunits each hold a **haem group with Fe²⁺**, and quaternary structure gives **cooperative binding**.
- 3.2 **Collagen** is **fibrous** and structural: a repeating **Gly-X-Y** puts **glycine, the smallest amino acid**, every third residue, so three chains pack into a **tight triple helix**.
- 3.3 **Staggered covalent cross-links** between molecules stop shearing, giving **high tensile strength**.

### 4. Denaturation destroys the shape without touching the sequence

- 4.1 Heat and pH extremes break the **hydrogen, ionic and hydrophobic bonds** holding the fold.
- 4.2 **Peptide bonds and primary structure survive**, so the chain remains but conformation and function are lost.
- 4.3 pH acts by changing **R group ionisation**, disrupting **ionic and hydrogen bonds** specifically.

## PART E: ENZYMES

### 1. Enzymes speed reactions by lowering activation energy and by nothing else

- 1.1 They are **globular proteins** that **lower activation energy**, do not shift the energetics or the equilibrium, and are **not consumed**.
- 1.2 The **active site** is built by folding from residues far apart in the sequence, so losing the fold abolishes catalysis.
- 1.3 Substrate binds as an **enzyme-substrate complex**; the product no longer fits, so it leaves and the enzyme is reused.
- 1.4 Two competing models of the fit:
    - **Lock-and-key**: a rigid site, exactly complementary to the substrate.
    - **Induced fit**: a flexible site that moulds around the substrate on binding.

### 2. Every rate curve has a shape, and each region needs its own explanation

- 2.1 **Temperature**: more kinetic energy gives more **effective collisions** up to the **optimum**; past it **denaturation** destroys the site, **irreversibly**.
- 2.2 **pH**: moving off the optimum alters **R group ionisation** and distorts the site, usually **reversibly**.
- 2.3 **Concentration**: rate climbs with substrate until active sites are **saturated** at **Vmax**, and with enzyme until substrate becomes limiting.
- 2.4 Rate is measured as **product formed** or **substrate used** per unit time, initial rate from the **tangent at time zero**.

### 3. Inhibition is read off Vmax and Km, and the two types move them differently

- 3.1 **Km** is the substrate concentration at **half Vmax** and measures affinity: **low Km means high affinity**.
- 3.2 **Competitive** inhibitors resemble the substrate and bind the **active site**: **Km rises, Vmax unchanged**, and excess substrate overcomes them.
- 3.3 **Non-competitive** inhibitors bind an **allosteric site** and distort the active site: **Vmax falls, Km unchanged**, and excess substrate does not help.
- 3.4 **End-product inhibition** scales this to a pathway: the final product allosterically switches off an earlier enzyme.

## PART F: CELL STRUCTURE

### 1. Cell theory sets the frame, and surface area to volume ratio sets the size limit

- 1.1 Three tenets: the **cell is the basic unit of life**, **all organisms are made of cells**, **all cells arise from pre-existing cells**.
- 1.2 As a cell enlarges, **volume rises faster than surface area**, so exchange cannot keep pace with demand.
- 1.3 Organisms therefore grow by having **more cells, not larger cells**.

### 2. Prokaryote and eukaryote differ on compartmentalisation, and it is examined both ways

- 2.1 **Prokaryotes**: no nucleus, **no membrane-bound organelles**, **peptidoglycan wall**, **circular DNA** in a nucleoid, **70S ribosomes**.
- 2.2 **Eukaryotes**: a true nucleus, membrane-bound organelles, **80S ribosomes**, larger.
- 2.3 **Compartmentalisation** lets incompatible reactions run at once, concentrates enzyme with substrate, isolates hydrolytic enzymes, and **increases membrane surface area**.
- 2.4 **Mitochondria and chloroplasts** are the anomaly: **double membrane**, own **circular DNA** and **70S ribosomes**, inner membrane **impermeable to protons**.

### 3. Organelle abundance reports the cell's job, and the secretory pathway is how that is examined

- 3.1 A secreted protein runs **rough ER ribosome, ER lumen for folding, transport vesicle, Golgi for modification and sorting, secretory vesicle, exocytosis**.
- 3.2 Abundant **rough ER** means secretion, **smooth ER** lipid synthesis and detoxification, **mitochondria** high ATP demand, **lysosomes** digestion.
- 3.3 Organelles are identified from **drawings, photomicrographs and electron micrographs**, and **ribosomes, centrioles and the nucleolus are not membrane-bound**.

### 4. Microscopy is a question about resolution, not magnification

- 4.1 **Magnification** is how much larger the image is; **resolution** is telling two close points apart. The **electron microscope** wins on resolution, because electrons have a **shorter wavelength**.
- 4.2 Calculations use **magnification = image size ÷ actual size**, from a **scale bar** or an **eyepiece graticule calibrated against a stage micrometer**.

## PART G: CELL MEMBRANES AND TRANSPORT

### 1. Membranes do five jobs, and the fluid mosaic model is how they do them

- 1.1 The five: **define boundaries**, **localise function**, **regulate cell contents**, **transduce signals**, **mediate cell-to-cell communication and adhesion**.
- 1.2 The model is a **fluid phospholipid bilayer** with a **mosaic of proteins** in and on it, **asymmetric** between the two faces.
- 1.3 Only weak **hydrophobic interactions** hold the lipids, so they move **laterally with ease** but **flip transversely rarely**, since the head would have to cross the core.

### 2. Fluidity has four determinants, and cholesterol is the one that works in both directions

- 2.1 **Temperature** raises fluidity, **longer chains** lower it, and **more unsaturation** raises it by preventing close packing.
- 2.2 **Cholesterol** buffers both ways: at **high temperature it restrains movement and lowers fluidity**, at **low temperature it prevents packing and raises fluidity**. It also **lowers permeability** to ions and small polar molecules.

### 3. Proteins and carbohydrates carry the functions the lipid cannot

- 3.1 **Integral** proteins are **amphipathic** and sit in the hydrophobic core; **peripheral** proteins are hydrophilic and sit loosely on the surface.
- 3.2 Protein roles: **transport, enzymatic activity, signal transduction, cell recognition, intercellular joining, anchorage**.
- 3.3 **Glycoproteins and glycolipids** carry short sugar chains on the **outer face only**, as **recognition markers** and to maintain orientation.

### 4. Six transport processes answer one question, so sort each by energy, protein and direction

- 4.1 **Simple diffusion**: **no ATP, no protein, down the gradient**, for small or hydrophobic molecules.
- 4.2 **Facilitated diffusion**: **no ATP**, needs a **channel or carrier protein**, still **down the gradient**, for large or hydrophilic solutes.
    - **Carrier** proteins have a **binding site** and **change conformation**.
    - **Channel** proteins have a **hydrophilic pore** and do **not**.
- 4.3 **Active transport**: **ATP required**, **carrier protein only**, **against the gradient**.
- 4.4 **Endocytosis** invaginates, so the membrane **loses** phospholipid; **exocytosis** fuses vesicles, so it **gains** phospholipid. Both need **ATP**.
- 4.5 **Osmosis** is net movement of water from **less negative to more negative water potential** through a partially permeable membrane.

### 5. Water potential predicts where water moves, and the cell wall decides the outcome

- 5.1 **Pure water has Ψ = 0, the maximum**, and adding solute always makes Ψ **more negative**.
- 5.2 **Plant: Ψ = Ψs + Ψp. Animal: Ψ = Ψs**, because with no wall no **pressure potential** develops.
- 5.3 **Hypotonic**: plant cell **turgid**, animal cell **lyses**. **Hypertonic**: plant cell **plasmolyses**, animal cell **crenates**.

## One-line mental map

**Water:** polarity gives hydrogen bonding, which gives solvent power, heat capacity and cohesion, sorts every molecule as hydrophilic, hydrophobic or amphipathic, and condensation and hydrolysis build and break every polymer.

**Lipids:** non-polar tails make triglycerides a dense water-free store and phospholipids amphipathic, so they self-assemble into the bilayer.

**Carbohydrates:** the anomeric carbon splits α from β, so α gives branched, compact, easily hydrolysed storage and β gives inverted straight hydrogen-bonded chains with tensile strength.

**Proteins:** R groups drive four levels of folding with a named bond at each, giving globular haemoglobin and fibrous collagen, and denaturation strips the fold but leaves the sequence.

**Enzymes:** a folded active site lowers activation energy, rate curves are explained by collisions, saturation or denaturation, and inhibitors are identified by which of Vmax and Km moves.

**Cell structure:** surface area to volume ratio caps cell size, compartmentalisation is the eukaryotic answer, and organelle abundance plus the secretory pathway is how it gets tested.

**Membranes:** amphipathic bilayer plus protein mosaic, fluidity set by temperature, chain length, saturation and cholesterol, and six transport processes sorted by ATP, protein and direction.
