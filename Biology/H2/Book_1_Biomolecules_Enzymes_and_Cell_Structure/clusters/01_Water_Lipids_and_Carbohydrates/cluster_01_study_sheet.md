## A. Water is polar, and polarity causes everything else

**A1. The molecule is asymmetric.** Two hydrogens are covalently bonded to one oxygen at **104.5°**.
Oxygen is **strongly electronegative** (attracts electrons); hydrogen is **electropositive**. So there
is an **unequal distribution of electrons**, giving a **partial negative charge (δ-) on the O** and a
**partial positive charge (δ+) on each H**, while **the molecule as a whole is uncharged**. That makes
water a **polar molecule, with the two ends having opposite charges**.

**A2. Hydrogen bonds follow from polarity.** When the δ+ region of one molecule approaches the δ-
region of another, the electrical attraction gives a **weak bond called a hydrogen bond**. Each water
molecule can form **four** of them. Individually they are **much weaker than covalent bonds, but the
combined effect of many weak bonds is highly significant** — that last clause is the reasoning that
earns marks, not the bond count.

**A3. Polarity makes water the universal solvent.** Most molecules in cells are polar or charged, so
they hydrogen-bond or ionically bond with water. Worked through NaCl: the solvent must **overcome the
attraction of the oppositely charged Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions for each other**; the ions enter **electrostatic
interactions with water molecules, forming hydration spheres**, which **neutralises their attraction**,
so the ions **separate and become dissolved**.

**A4. Every molecule sorts into one of three classes by how it behaves toward water.** This is the
classification the whole Book runs on.

| Class | Definition | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Hydrophilic** | **affinity for water, dissolves readily in it** | **polar molecules and ions / charged molecules** |
| **Hydrophobic** | **not very soluble in water** | **non-polar and cannot form hydrogen bonds with water** |
| **Amphipathic** | **both hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions on the same molecule** | named cases are **proteins and phospholipids** |

**Amphipathic is the load-bearing one.** Membranes exist because of it, and Cluster 3 is built on it.

## B. Carbon and the polymer machinery

**B1. Carbon's tetravalency underwrites biological diversity.** A carbon atom has **six electrons, two
inner and four outer**; the outer shell **accommodates a maximum of eight**; carbon acquires four more
**by sharing electrons**, so it **forms four covalent bonds**. Attached **functional groups determine
the characteristics and chemical reactivity of the molecule**.

**B2. Three of the four families are polymers; lipids are not.** A **macromolecule is a giant molecule
made of many repeating units**, therefore a **polymer**; the repeating units are **monomers**. The
three polymer types are **polysaccharides, proteins and nucleic acids**, from **monosaccharides, amino
acids and nucleotides**. **Lipids are NOT polymers** and are generally smaller, but **they associate
with each other into much larger groups of molecules**. Examiners test that exception directly.

**B3. One reaction builds every polymer; its reverse breaks every polymer.**

**Condensation** (also **dehydration**): two molecules are **covalently bonded to each other through
loss of a water molecule**. Enzyme-catalysed, and **a major energy-consuming activity of the cell**.
**Hydrolysis**: the reverse — the bond is **broken by the addition of a water molecule**.

The bond gets a different name per family, but the reaction never changes:

| Family | Bond formed | Between |
|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | **glycosidic** | anomeric carbon of one sugar and a carbon of the other |
| Lipid | **ester (ester linkage)** | –OH of glycerol and –COOH of a fatty acid |
| Protein | **peptide** | –COOH of one amino acid and –NH₂ of the next |

## C. Lipids are a solubility category, not a structural family

**C1. The category is defined by behaviour, not by a shared monomer.** Lipids are grouped **due to
their insolubility in water and high solubility in non-polar solvents**. They split three ways:
**simple lipids** (**fatty acids and an alcohol, usually glycerol**), **compound lipids** (**fatty
acids, an alcohol and other groups** such as phosphate or sugar), and **lipid derivatives**.

**C2. Triglyceride = glycerol + 3 fatty acids via 3 ester linkages, losing 3 H₂O.** Glycerol is a
**three-carbon alcohol** with **three hydroxyl (OH) groups**, **soluble in water because of its polar
–OH group**. A fatty acid has an **acidic carboxyl (COOH) group at one end and an attached hydrocarbon
chain** of **12–20 carbons**; **the abundance of these non-polar C–H bonds is the reason why fats are
hydrophobic**. Fatty acids differ in **chain length** and in **number and position of C=C double bonds**.

**C3. Melting point is set by chain length and saturation, both routed through hydrophobic
interactions.** A **hydrophobic interaction is a weak bond between hydrophobic molecules, a force of
attraction between non-polar molecules**. Two rules, and you must give the middle reasoning step:

- **Longer chain → more extensive hydrophobic interactions → more thermal energy required → higher melting point.**
- **More unsaturation → kinks where the double bonds occur → molecules cannot pack closely → less extensive hydrophobic interactions → less thermal energy required → lower melting point.**

Consequence: **animal fats are saturated and solid at room temperature**; **plant and fish fats are
unsaturated, liquid, and called oils**.

**C4. Triglyceride structure maps onto five distinct functions.** Each is a structure-to-function pair,
which is exactly how the marks are allocated.

| Structural feature | Function it produces |
|---|---|
| High C and H relative to O, **more C per unit mass** | **more energy released on oxidation** (roughly twice that of carbohydrate) → **efficient energy store** |
| **Highly reduced**, twice the H per unit mass of carbohydrate | **more metabolic water on oxidation** → matters to **desert animals** |
| **Non-polar C–H bonds → hydrophobic → no water of hydration** | **no effect on water potential**, no added body mass, and acts as a **thermal insulator** (subcutaneous fat, **blubber**) |
| Non-polar tails held by **weak hydrophobic interactions** | triglycerides **slide under pressure** → adipose tissue **cushions organs** |
| **Lower density than water** per unit volume | aids **buoyancy** |

**C5. Phospholipids are amphipathic, and that generates all membrane behaviour.** Structure: **one
glycerol + two fatty acids** (two **ester linkages**) with the **third OH joined to a negatively
charged phosphate group** by a **phosphoester linkage**, plus a variable polar or charged group. The
result is **two non-polar hydrophobic tails and a polar/charged hydrophilic head**, so phospholipids
show **ambivalent behaviour towards water**.

Three aggregates form in water, and **all of them serve to shield the hydrophobic tails from the
aqueous environment**:

| Aggregate | What it is | What it becomes biologically |
|---|---|---|
| **Micelle** | **spherical droplet, phospholipid monolayer, heads outside, tails in the water-free interior** | fat transport between gut and tissues |
| **Bilayer** | **two monolayers forming a two-dimensional sheet**, heads to the polar exterior, tails excluded from water | the **selectively permeable membrane** |
| **Liposome / vesicle** | **bilayer folded back on itself into a hollow sphere**, avoiding exposed hydrophobic edges | vesicles, lysosomes, drug delivery |

**Fluidity comes from the same bonds.** Many weak hydrophobic interactions hold the membrane together
**collectively**, yet **each is individually weak enough to permit lateral movement**.

**C6. Glycolipids swap phosphate for sugar, and the sugar is the signal.** **Two hydrophobic
hydrocarbon tails plus a polar short carbohydrate chain (fewer than 15 sugar residues), no phosphate**,
joined to glycerol's –OH by a **glycosidic bond**. The carbohydrate faces outward, so it acts as a
**marker for cell-cell recognition and cell-cell adhesion**.

**C7. Cholesterol.** **Three fused six-membered rings and one five-membered ring.** In membranes it
**regulates membrane fluidity**; it is also a **precursor for bile acids, steroid hormones and vitamin D**.

## D. Carbohydrates: the anomeric carbon decides everything

**D1. Classification.** **C, H and O only**; **the most abundant biomolecules on Earth**. Each has
**one carbonyl group (C=O), as either a ketone or an aldehyde, plus multiple hydroxyl (OH) groups**.
Counted by monomer: **mono (1) / di (2) / oligo (3–10) / poly (more than 10)**. Named by carbon count:
**trioses, tetroses, pentoses, hexoses**.

**D2. The ring form and the anomeric carbon.** Linear and ring forms sit in **dynamic equilibrium**,
but **the ring is predominant because it is energetically more stable**, and the ring is the form built
into polysaccharides. The **anomeric carbon is the carbon bonded to TWO oxygen atoms**. The hydroxyl on
it takes two configurations: **below the plane of the ring = α; above the plane = β** (**ABBA — Alpha
is Below, Beta is Above**).

**This single difference is the most consequential fact in the chapter.** α leads to storage
polysaccharides; β leads to structural ones. Being able to number the carbons and call α from β is an
examinable skill, not background.

**D3. The glycosidic bond.** **A covalent C–O–C bond formed between the anomeric carbon of one sugar
unit and another carbon on the other sugar unit**, made by **condensation with loss of one water
molecule**. Broken by **hydrolysis**, either by **dilute acid at 100 °C** or **by an enzyme**. Its name
depends on **which carbons' hydroxyls are involved** and **whether the orientation is α or β**.

**D4. Reducing versus non-reducing turns on a free carbonyl group.** **Reducing sugars can reduce other
compounds and are oxidised in the process.** **All monosaccharides and some disaccharides (maltose,
lactose) are reducing.** **Non-reducing sugars such as sucrose have no free carbonyl group, because
both carbonyl groups are locked in the glycosidic bond.** Benedict's works because **a free carbonyl
group reduces copper from Cu²⁺ to Cu⁺**, so **blue copper(II) sulphate is reduced to insoluble
brick-red copper(I) oxide under alkaline conditions**.

**D5. Storage polysaccharides are α-glucose, engineered for three things at once.** **α(1,4) bonds give
linear unbranched chains; α(1,6) bonds give branches.**

| | Amylose | Amylopectin | Glycogen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bonds | **α(1,4) only** | **α(1,4) + α(1,6)** | **α(1,4) + α(1,6)** |
| Branching | **unbranched** | branch every **12–30 residues** | branch every **8–12 units** |
| Shape | **helical, six glucose units per turn, compact** | **highly compact** from extensive branching | **more branched, therefore more compact than amylopectin** |
| Where | plant, **starch grains in chloroplasts or amyloplasts** | plant, with amylose in starch | animal, **cytoplasmic granules** in **liver** and **skeletal muscle** |
| Iodine | **blue-black** | — | **red-violet** |

The reasoning that earns the marks, shared by all three:

- **Large and insoluble → no effect on water potential** of the cell.
- **Hundreds to thousands of glucose units → a large carbon/energy store in a small space.**
- **α(1,4) bonds are easily hydrolysed → glucose can be mobilised quickly** when needed.
- **Many free branch ends → a larger number of enzymes can act at once → faster hydrolysis** (branched forms only).
- **Anomeric carbons are tied up in glycosidic bonds → unreactive and chemically stable.**

**D6. The structural polysaccharide is β-glucose, and inversion is the whole mechanism.** To form
**β(1,4) glycosidic bonds, alternate monomers are inverted** — every other glucose is upside down
relative to its neighbour. That forces a **long, unbranched, straight chain**. Then the chain
consequences cascade:

**Straight parallel chains → OH groups project outwards → extensive hydrogen bonds between neighbouring chains → rigid cross-links → microfibrils (60–70 chains, up to 25 nm) → macrofibrils → cellulose fibres in the cell wall.**

Properties that follow: **high tensile strength**; **fibres laid in different orientations in different
wall layers, so the wall withstands forces exerted in all directions**; and **full permeability to
water and solutes**. Cellulose is also **stable** because **β(1,4) bonds are not easily hydrolysed** —
**amylase only hydrolyses α(1,4)**, and only organisms with **cellulase** can digest it.

## E. The comparison the exam keeps asking for

Cellulose versus starch has appeared as a **10-mark question in its own right**. The answer is not two
descriptions side by side; it is one causal chain run twice from the same starting point.

**β-glucose → alternate monomers inverted → straight chains → interchain H-bonds → cross-links → tensile strength → structural support.**

**α-glucose → no inversion needed → helical / branched chains → compact, insoluble → no osmotic effect, fast hydrolysis → energy storage.**

If you can produce both chains from memory, you have the question.

## G. Three errors in your notes — do not encode these

Found by checking the chapters against the syllabus and against themselves. Learn the corrected
version.

1. **Macronutrients are misdefined.** The Water chapter says macronutrients "are needed in very small
   quantities" and micronutrients "in minute amounts", which makes the distinction meaningless.
   **Macronutrients are required in relatively large quantities; micronutrients in trace amounts.**
2. **The glycogen table names the wrong enzyme.** It says glycogen is hydrolysed "by amylase" in one
   row while correctly naming **glycogen phosphorylase** in another, and the cellulose table two pages
   later states amylase acts on starch. Treat **glycogen phosphorylase** as the glycogen enzyme.
3. **Amylose chain length contradicts itself.** The body text says "hundreds to thousands" of residues;
   a borrowed figure says "up to 300". Quote **hundreds to thousands** — it is the notes' own prose and
   the safer answer.

**One genuine syllabus gap:** LO (h) requires **formation *and breakage*** of the ester bond, but the
Lipids chapter never covers triglyceride hydrolysis. Know that **a triglyceride is hydrolysed by
lipase into glycerol and three fatty acids, adding three water molecules** — the reverse of its
formation. It is not in your notes and it is examinable.

**Water itself has no learning outcome.** Nothing in LOs (a)–(s) mentions water, polarity or hydrogen
bonding directly. Water is enabling background that shows up *inside* answers about membranes,
proteins and transport. Study it for the reasoning, not as a topic to be examined on its own.
