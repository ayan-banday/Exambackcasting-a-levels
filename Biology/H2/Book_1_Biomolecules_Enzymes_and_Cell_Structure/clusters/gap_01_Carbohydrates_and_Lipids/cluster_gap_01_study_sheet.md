## PART A — The four gaps where you had the biology right and the wording wrong

These cost marks even though you understood the concept. That is the most expensive kind of gap,
because it is invisible until a marker rejects you.

### A1. Describing hydrolysis of a glycosidic bond

You said: *"Causes alpha 1,4 glycosidic bond to break, causing each anomeric carbon of each
monosaccharide to have a hydroxyl group attached to it"* — then immediately: *"I don't like how that's
phrased."* You were right not to like it.

**The mark scheme wants four separate things:**

1. **Breaking of the α(1,4) glycosidic bond** between α-glucose monomers.

2. **Addition of one water molecule.**

3. **H is added to carbon atom 1** of one α-glucose monomer, **and OH is added to carbon atom 4** of
   the other.

4. **An enzyme is required.**

Point 3 is the one you did not have. "Each anomeric carbon gets a hydroxyl" is not the same claim and
does not score. The water splits, and the two halves go to *named, different* carbons.

**And the reverse, condensation, is the mirror image** — the same four points with H₂O *removed*
instead of added. You worked this out later in the session and got it right, so the two now belong
together in your head as one reaction described from both ends.

### A2. Drawing a condensation reaction

Your own realisation, and it is exactly right: *"when they make us draw, label, they show two
alpha-glucose, you have to show this, and you have to show H₂O, and have to label condensation
reaction. So three things that you have to do."*

**Lock it as a four-item checklist, because you missed the fourth:**

1. Both monomers drawn, correctly oriented (for β, remember the **alternate inversion**).

2. The **bond itself drawn** and **labelled with its full name** — "α(1,6) glycosidic bond", not just
   "glycosidic bond".

3. **H₂O shown** as a product, drawn off to the side.

4. The reaction **labelled "condensation"**.

Marks are allocated per item, so an unlabelled correct drawing throws away half of them.

### A3. Why amylose slows hydrolysis

You reached the right conclusion — *"amylose contains α(1,4), so they do not have branch ends to be

*"Oh shit. Yeah, we should have said that."*

### A4. Cellulose versus glycogen — the difference you missed

You gave: different monomers, different bonds, branched versus straight. All correct. You missed the
fourth, and said so: *"Cellulose also contains intermolecular hydrogen bonding. In between the hydroxyl
groups, but glycogen does not."*

| | Cellulose | Glycogen |
|---|---|---|
| Monomer | **β-glucose** | **α-glucose** |
| Bond | **β(1,4)** | **α(1,4) + α(1,6)** |
| Shape | **linear / straight**, unbranched | **helical and branched** |
| **Interchain H-bonding** | **extensive, between the projecting -OH groups of adjacent parallel chains** | **absent** |

That fourth row is the one that does the work in a structure-to-function answer, because it is what
produces tensile strength. It is also the single most-missed line in this comparison.

## PART B — The three gaps where you genuinely did not know

### B1. Why phospholipids cannot provide structural support

You floundered here, and knew it: *"I don't know, bro. I don't know."*

**Three reasons, and you need at least two:**

1. A phospholipid is **not a long linear macromolecule joined by strong covalent bonds**, so there is
   **no high tensile strength**.

2. It is **fluid** — held together only by **weak hydrophobic interactions** — so molecules **move
   laterally** relative to one another.

3. There are **no numerous interchain hydrogen bonds** between the hydroxyl groups of adjacent
   parallel chains, so **no rigid cross-links** can form.

**The general rule to carry:** whenever a question asks about **structural support**, the answer is
always built from *high tensile strength*, *rigidity rather than fluidity*, and *extensive interchain
bonding*. Cellulose has all three; a phospholipid has none. Your own summary was right: *"whenever they
say structural support, remember high tensile strength and being not fluid."*

### B2. The non-reducing sugar test — the acid step

You caught this as a gap in the teaching sheet, not just in yourself: *"Oh, you didn't even mention that
in the cluster sheet, bro."* Correct, and it is now here.

**Reducing sugar test:** Benedict's reagent, heat. A **free carbonyl group** reduces **Cu²⁺ to Cu⁺**,
so blue copper(II) sulfate becomes a **brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide**.

**Non-reducing sugar test — the part that was missing:**

1. A first Benedict's test gives a **negative** result (stays blue).

2. **Add dilute acid and heat** to **hydrolyse the glycosidic bond**, breaking the disaccharide into
   its **monosaccharides**.

3. **Neutralise** with alkali (Benedict's needs alkaline conditions).

4. Repeat Benedict's. Now **positive**, because hydrolysis has **released the free carbonyl groups**
   that were locked in the glycosidic bond.

**Sucrose is the standard case:** both its carbonyl groups are tied up in the glycosidic bond, so there
is no free carbonyl until acid hydrolysis frees them.

### B3. Why a liver cell stores glycogen *and* fat

You got there, but slowly, and by reasoning about the pancreas rather than about the molecules.

**The answer is a division of labour, and the contrast is the whole point:**

| | Glycogen | Triglyceride (fat) |
|---|---|---|
| Speed of mobilisation | **fast** — many free ends, α(1,4) easily hydrolysed | **slow** — must go through many oxidation steps |
| Energy per unit mass | **lower** | **higher** — more C and H relative to O |
| Role | **short-term, rapid glucose release** to maintain blood glucose | **long-term, energy-dense store** |

So the cell keeps both because **neither one does both jobs**. That sentence is the mark.

## PART C — Two things you were uncertain about and got right anyway

Worth locking in so the uncertainty does not come back.

### C1. Osmotic effect: glucose > glycogen > fat

You said *"fat always beats glycogen. Always beats glycogen, interesting"* — you were surprised, so it
had not settled.

**The reasoning, in order:**

- **Glucose** is a **small soluble monomer**, so the same mass gives a **far larger number of dissolved
  particles**, making the water potential much more negative and the cell take in water fastest.
- **Glycogen** is **large and insoluble**, so hundreds of glucose units contribute as **one particle**.
  Minimal osmotic effect.
- **Fat is hydrophobic**, so it carries **no water of hydration** and has **no osmotic effect at all**.

**Fat always beats glycogen** because "insoluble" still means present in the cytoplasm; "hydrophobic"
means osmotically invisible.

### C2. Why branching requires α(1,6) specifically

You worked this out live and it is worth keeping: a branch can only start where **carbon 6 of one
residue is joined to the anomeric carbon (carbon 1) of another**. An α(1,4) bond runs along the chain;
only a bond involving **carbon 6** can leave the chain and start a new one.

## PART D — The reasoning pattern that earned you the most marks

This is the thing you already do well, and the reason to keep doing it. Your storage-polysaccharide
answer was complete and correct on the first attempt:

1. **Large and insoluble** → no effect on water potential.
2. **Hundreds to thousands of monomers** → large energy store in a small space.
3. **α(1,4) bonds easily hydrolysed** → glucose mobilised quickly.
4. **Many free ends from branching** → many enzymes act at once → fast hydrolysis.
5. **Anomeric carbons tied up in glycosidic bonds** → few free hydroxyls → relatively unreactive
   and chemically stable.

Your cellulose chain was equally strong: **β-glucose → alternate inversion → β(1,4) bonds → long
straight unbranched chain → OH groups project outwards → extensive interchain hydrogen bonding → rigid
cross-links → microfibrils → macrofibrils → high tensile strength, full permeability, unreactive.**

**Both of these are complete causal chains, not lists.** That is exactly what a structure-to-function
question rewards, and it is why those parts went quickly. The gaps in Parts A and B are all places
where a chain like this was *missing a link*, not places where you lacked the idea.
