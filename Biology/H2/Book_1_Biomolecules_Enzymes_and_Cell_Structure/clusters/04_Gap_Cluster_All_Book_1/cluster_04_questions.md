### Question 1

**(a)** *Why enlarged nucleoli must precede an increase in glycoprotein secretion.* **[6]**

**1.** The **nucleolus contains the genes coding for rRNA and is the site of rRNA synthesis /
transcription of rRNA**.

**2.** Larger, more darkly stained nucleoli mean **more rRNA is being transcribed**.

**3.** In the nucleolus, **rRNA assembles with ribosomal proteins to form the large and the small
ribosomal subunits**, which are **unequal in size**.

**4.** The **subunits leave the nucleus through the nuclear pores** and **assemble on mRNA in the
cytoplasm to form a functional ribosome**.

**5.** **More ribosomes allow a higher rate of translation / protein synthesis.**

**6.** The glycoprotein is **secreted, so it is translated on ribosomes bound to the rough endoplasmic
reticulum**, and **more ribosomes on the rough ER means more glycoprotein made per unit time**.

*Gap closed: a nucleolus count earns nothing on its own. The chain is more nucleoli → more rRNA → more
ribosomes → higher rate of protein synthesis.*

*Gap closed: the two ribosomal subunits are unequal — one large, one small.*

**(b)** *The one membrane property behind budding, fusion and exocytosis, and the effect of each on cell
surface membrane area.* **[5]**

**1.** The property is **fluidity / lateral movement of phospholipids within their own monolayer**,
because the phospholipids are **held only by weak hydrophobic interactions between their hydrocarbon
tails**, which are **easily broken and re-formed**.

**2.** Fluidity allows the **Golgi membrane to bend and pinch off a vesicle**, and allows a **vesicle
membrane to fuse with a target membrane without the bilayer breaking or leaking**.

**3.** Both vesicles bud from the **trans face of the Golgi apparatus**. **Lysosomes are formed by the
Golgi apparatus, not by the smooth endoplasmic reticulum.**

**4.** Formation of the lysosome is **budding, not exocytosis** — **nothing leaves the cell and no
vesicle fuses with the cell surface membrane** — so the **area of the cell surface membrane is
unchanged**. Membrane is **removed from the Golgi apparatus**.

**5.** Release of the glycoprotein is **exocytosis**: the secretory vesicle **fuses with the cell
surface membrane**, so the **vesicle membrane is added to it and the total area of the cell surface
membrane increases**.

*Gap closed: lysosomes bud from the Golgi, not the smooth ER.*

*Gap closed: budding is not exocytosis. Exocytosis adds membrane to the cell surface membrane; budding
removes membrane from the donor organelle.*

*Gap closed: vesicles fuse because the weak hydrophobic interactions holding the bilayer together make
it fluid, so membranes slide and merge instead of tearing.*

**(c)** *How a bacterium secretes an enzyme without a nucleolus, rough ER or Golgi apparatus.* **[4]**

**1.** The bacterium has **no nucleus**. Its DNA lies **free in the cytoplasm in a region called the
nucleoid** (R! nucleus / nucleolus / "genetic material" / "bacterial chromosome" on its own — the
**structure** must be named).

**2.** The DNA is **circular, not associated with histone proteins**, and the **nucleoid is not
enclosed by any membrane / nuclear envelope**.

**3.** It has **no nucleolus and no membrane-bound organelles**; its **ribosomes are 70S and free in the
cytoplasm**, and **translation can begin on an mRNA that is still being transcribed**.

**4.** The enzyme is **exported directly across the cell surface membrane by transport proteins**, so
**no transport vesicle, Golgi modification or secretory vesicle is required**.

*Gap closed: a prokaryote's DNA sits in a nucleoid. Writing "nucleus" is an outright reject.*

### Question 2

**(a)** *Audit all four suggested causes of the plateau at X.* **[4]**

**1. A is correct.** **All the carrier proteins are saturated / every binding site is occupied**, so
**the rate cannot rise further no matter how much glucose is outside**. The **number of carrier
proteins is now the limiting factor**.

**2. B is incorrect.** Nothing in the experiment **changes temperature or pH**, so the carriers are not
denatured. If they were, the **rate would fall towards zero, not hold at a constant maximum**.

**3. C is incorrect.** Glucose entry down its gradient is **facilitated diffusion, which requires no
ATP**. Even in a case that did use ATP, an **exhausted ATP supply would collapse the rate, not hold it
level**.

**4. D is incorrect.** If the internal and external concentrations were equal there would be **no
concentration gradient and no net entry**, so the line would **fall to the x-axis, not plateau at a
high rate**.

*Gap closed: a transport plateau is carrier saturation, not ATP running out.*

**(b)** *How a contractile vacuole fills, and every transport process involved.* **[5]**

**1.** **Protein pumps / carrier proteins in the contractile vacuole membrane actively transport ions
into the lumen against their concentration gradient**, using **energy from the hydrolysis of ATP**.
(R! channel proteins.) **"Pump" always means active transport.**

**2.** The **solute concentration of the lumen rises, so the water potential of the lumen becomes more
negative than that of the cytoplasm.** (R! "higher" or "lower" water potential.)

**3.** **Water moves from the cytoplasm, of less negative water potential, into the lumen, of more
negative water potential, down the water potential gradient, by osmosis**, passing through
**aquaporins / water channel proteins by facilitated diffusion**.

**4.** The **hydrophobic core of the bilayer is impermeable to ions**, so the **ions accumulate in the
lumen and cannot leak back out**, which is what holds the water there.

**5.** **Membrane fluidity allows small vesicles to fuse with the vacuole so it can enlarge**, and
allows the **full vacuole to fuse with the cell surface membrane so the water is expelled by
exocytosis**.

**Processes named or implied:** **active transport** (ions in), **osmosis** (water in), **facilitated
diffusion** (water through aquaporins), **exocytosis** (water out).

*Gap closed: the word "pump" in a stem is an instruction to write active transport.*

**(c)** *Deduce the route of P and of Q, and judge the cost of losing transmembrane proteins.* **[5]**

**1.** **P crosses by simple diffusion.** The rate is **directly proportional to the concentration
gradient with no upper limit**, because **P passes through the phospholipid bilayer itself**, **no
protein is involved and so there is nothing that can become saturated**.

**2.** **Q crosses by facilitated diffusion or active transport.** The rate **levels off because all the
channel or carrier proteins become saturated**, and **their number in the membrane is finite and now
limiting**.

**3.** **Simple diffusion and osmosis are the only routes that need no membrane protein**; every other
route depends on one.

**4.** **Transmembrane proteins are not a single class** — they include **channel proteins, carrier
proteins for facilitated diffusion, pumps for active transport, co-transporters, and receptors for cell
signalling**.

**5.** Judgement: a cell that loses them **loses facilitated diffusion, active transport, co-transport
and signal reception all at once**, keeping only **simple diffusion of small non-polar molecules and
osmosis**. **No other single membrane component carries that many functions**, which is why its loss
costs most.

*Gap closed: simple diffusion does not plateau. Facilitated diffusion and active transport do, because
both depend on a finite number of proteins.*

*Gap closed: transmembrane proteins include the carriers, which is exactly why they are the costliest
component to lose.*

and key; the option-by-option audit is an [INFERRED — extension, no official key] rewrite of a
one-mark item into four). VJC Prelim P2, contractile vacuole "explain how the structure of the

**Concepts fused:** (1) carrier saturation as the cause of a transport plateau; (2) ATP dependence and
what its absence would actually look like on a graph; (3) the pump → water potential → osmosis →
exocytosis chain of the contractile vacuole; (4) linear versus saturating uptake kinetics as a
diagnostic for the route taken; (5) the functional breadth of transmembrane proteins.

### Question 3

**(a)** *How the cell surface membrane allows a red blood cell to deform and recover.* **[4]**

**1.** The membrane is **fluid**: the **phospholipids and proteins move laterally within their own
monolayer**.

**2.** They are **held only by weak hydrophobic interactions between the hydrocarbon tails**, which are
**easily broken and re-formed**.

**3.** So the **bilayer can change shape and be deformed without breaking**, and **reseals as the cell
resumes its shape**, which is why nothing leaks out.

**4.** **Unsaturated hydrocarbon tails carry kinks that prevent close packing**, keeping the membrane
fluid at body temperature, and **cholesterol adds mechanical stability and flexibility** so the
membrane does not tear under shear.

*Gap closed: any stem about stretching, squeezing or deformation is answered with fluidity and lateral
movement.*

**(b)** *Predict raft behaviour against non-raft behaviour at 5 °C and at 45 °C.* **[4]**

*At 5 °C:*

**1.** The **non-raft region stays relatively fluid**, because its **kinked unsaturated tails already
prevent the phospholipids packing closely**.

**2.** The raft's **straight, less kinked tails would pack closely and the region would tend towards a
semi-solid / gel state**, but the **higher proportion of cholesterol inserts between the tails and
prevents close packing**, so the **raft does not solidify** and stays workably fluid.

*At 45 °C:*

**3.** **Increased kinetic energy makes the phospholipids move faster**, so the **non-raft region
becomes markedly more fluid and more permeable**.

**4.** In the raft, the **cholesterol restrains the movement of the phospholipids and reduces
fluidity**, so the raft stays **more ordered, more stable and less permeable** than the membrane around
it — cholesterol also **acts as a plug, reducing the escape of ions and small polar molecules**.

**5.** The point of the contrast: **cholesterol works in both directions**, raising fluidity at low
temperature and lowering it at high temperature, **whereas unsaturation only ever shifts fluidity one
way**.

*Gap closed: at low temperature cholesterol prevents the chains packing closely, so the membrane does
not become semi-solid. Do not invert this.*

*Gap closed: cholesterol buffers both ways; unsaturation is one-directional. Cholesterol alone also
gives mechanical stability and reduced permeability.*

**(c)** *Two independent explanations for bursting at 55 °C.* **[5]**

*Route one — the bilayer.*

**1.** The **higher temperature raises the kinetic energy of the phospholipids**, so they **move
laterally and flip between layers more**.

**2.** The **weak hydrophobic interactions between the hydrocarbon tails are overcome**, the molecules
**move further apart and gaps open in the bilayer**.

**3.** The membrane becomes **more fluid and more permeable**, so it **can no longer contain the
contents and the cell bursts**.

*Route two — the proteins.*

**4.** Above the optimum, the **increased vibration breaks the hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and
hydrophobic interactions that maintain the 3D conformation** of the **pumps and carrier proteins**, so
they are **denatured and their binding sites lose their complementary shape**.

**5.** **Ions can no longer be pumped out, solutes accumulate, the water potential of the cytoplasm
becomes more negative than that of the surrounding solution, water enters by osmosis** and, with **no
cell wall to resist the pressure**, the cell bursts.

*Gap closed: bursting at high temperature has two independent routes — hydrophobic interactions in the
bilayer being overcome, and denaturation of the pump proteins. "Whenever there's temperature, think
enzymes" is your own rule; write both.*

### Question 4

**(a)** *Rank the four levels of β-keratin by contribution to the stability of the fibre.* **[6]**

**1.** *Polypeptide.* **Antiparallel β-pleated sheets** stabilised by **numerous intrachain hydrogen
bonds between the C=O and N–H groups of neighbouring segments** of the same chain.

**2.** *Dimer.* **Two covalent disulfide bonds between the two β-keratin polypeptides.**

**3.** *Fibril.* **Two covalent disulfide bonds between β-keratin dimers**, holding a row of dimers
together. (R! peptide bonds.)

**4.** *Fibre.* **Numerous / extensive disulfide bonds between neighbouring fibrils.** ("More" on its
own is rejected; the count in one printed segment is ignored, because the figure shows only a fragment
of a long fibre.)

**5.** *The ranking.* **Fibre contributes most**, because it carries **the largest number of covalent
cross-links and they run in every direction**, so a break in one place is not transmitted through the
structure; then **fibril, then dimer** — both covalent but with **far fewer bonds each**; **the
polypeptide contributes least**, because **hydrogen bonds are individually weak** even though there are
many of them.

**6.** The reason the ranking works at all: **disulfide bonds are covalent, therefore strong, and are
not overcome by temperature or pH**, which is why keratin resists degradation. (R! describing hydrogen
bonds as strong bonds.)

*Gap closed: a keratin-stability answer has to be broken down level by level — polypeptide, dimer,
fibril, fibre — with the bond named at each. A general statement about disulfide bonds is worth one
mark, not four.*

**(b)** *Rank a tendon, a plant cell wall and a phospholipid bilayer by the load each bears, and say
what gives way in each.* **[8]**

**Ranking, greatest load first: tendon (collagen) > plant cell wall (cellulose) > phospholipid
bilayer.**

*The plant cell wall — cellulose.*

**1.** Monomer **β-glucose**, joined by **β-1,4-glycosidic bonds with alternate residues inverted**,
giving **long, straight, unbranched chains**.

**2.** The **–OH groups project outwards in all directions from each chain**, so **numerous interchain
hydrogen bonds form between the projecting –OH groups of neighbouring chains**, cross-linking them into
**microfibrils and then macrofibrils**. Under load it is **those interchain hydrogen bonds that give
way**, not the chains themselves — the **covalent backbone survives and the chains slide apart**.

*The tendon — collagen.*

**3.** Monomer **amino acids**; **three left-handed helical polypeptides wind into a tropocollagen
triple helix**. (R! tropocollagen as the monomer.)

**4.** **Almost every third residue is glycine**, whose **R group is a single hydrogen atom**. **Every
third residue passes through the crowded centre of the triple helix, where only an R group as small as
glycine's will fit**, so the three chains **lie close together and coil tightly**.

**5.** **Hydrogen bonds are both intrachain, within each helix, and interchain, between the N–H of
glycine and the C=O of a residue on a neighbouring backbone**, holding the triple helix together.

**6.** **Tropocollagen molecules lie in a staggered arrangement** and are joined by **covalent
cross-links between the C-terminal and N-terminal of adjacent molecules**, building fibrils and fibres.

*Why the tendon outranks the cell wall.*

**7.** **Collagen bears the greater load.** Cellulose's **only cross-links between chains are hydrogen
bonds**; collagen has **covalent cross-links between tropocollagen molecules on top of its hydrogen
bonds**, and a **covalent bond is individually far stronger than a hydrogen bond**. The **staggered
arrangement spreads the load** so no single junction takes all of it. What finally gives way in a
tendon is therefore **a covalent cross-link**, which takes far more force than the hydrogen bonds that
fail first in cellulose.

*The phospholipid bilayer — last, and by a long way.*

**8.** It is **not a long linear macromolecule** — the phospholipids are **separate molecules not joined
to one another by covalent bonds**, so **there is no continuous chain along which a pulling force can be
transmitted**. They are **held only by weak hydrophobic interactions**, which is what makes the bilayer
**fluid**, and **a fluid structure has no tensile strength at all**. There are **no interchain hydrogen
bonds cross-linking neighbours into rigid fibres**. What gives way is **nothing in particular** — the
molecules simply **slide past one another and the layer flows apart**, which is why the bilayer fails
under a load neither of the other two would notice.

*Gap closed: cellulose's tensile strength comes specifically from interchain hydrogen bonding between
the projecting –OH groups. Monomer, bond and branching alone do not reach the mark.*

*Gap closed: collagen beats cellulose because of covalent cross-links between tropocollagen molecules.*

*Gap closed: collagen hydrogen bonding is intrachain AND interchain, N–H of glycine to C=O of a
neighbouring backbone.*

*Gap closed: every third residue passes through the centre of the triple helix, so crowded that only
glycine's R group fits. Say the sentence, do not gesture at "glycine is small".*

*Gap closed: a phospholipid gives no structural support because it is not a covalently joined linear
macromolecule, it is held by weak hydrophobic interactions and so is fluid, and it forms no rigid
interchain cross-links.*

**(c)** *Predict the effect of pH 2 and of 70% methanol on a fibrous protein.* **[4]**

**1.** At **pH 2 the excess H⁺ ions change the ionisation / charge of the R groups** — **–COO⁻ gains H⁺
to become –COOH, and –NH₂ gains H⁺ to become –NH₃⁺**.

**2.** This **disrupts the ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds that maintain the 3D conformation**, so the
**conformation is lost and the protein is denatured**.

**3.** **Methanol is amphipathic.** Its **non-polar portion interacts with the hydrophobic R groups**,
so it **disrupts the hydrophobic interactions** holding the non-polar residues together.

**4.** The difference: **pH acts indirectly, through the charge on the R groups, on ionic and hydrogen
bonds; methanol acts directly on hydrophobic interactions.** **Neither breaks disulfide bonds or
peptide bonds, both of which are covalent**, so a heavily disulfide-cross-linked fibre survives both
treatments far better than one held mainly by hydrogen and ionic bonds.

*Gap closed: the pH sentence is "disrupt the ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds that maintain the 3D
conformation", reached through pH → ionisation of R groups changes → those bonds disrupted →
conformation lost.*

*Gap closed: an amphipathic solvent such as methanol disrupts hydrophobic interactions, not ionic
bonds.*

### Question 5

**(a)** *Describe the structure of haemoglobin.* **[5]**

**1.** *Primary.* The **unique number and sequence of amino acids in each polypeptide, joined by peptide
bonds**. Haemoglobin has **two α-chains and two β-chains**, the α and β sequences differing.

**2.** *Secondary.* **Regular coiling of the polypeptide backbone into α-helices**, held by **hydrogen
bonds between the C=O and the N–H of every fourth peptide bond in the main chain**. Haemoglobin is
**almost entirely α-helical, with no β-pleated sheet**.

**3.** *Tertiary.* Each chain **folds further into a specific 3D conformation**, held by **bonds between
R groups within the same chain — hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions and disulfide
bonds**. **Hydrophilic R groups face outwards into the aqueous surroundings and hydrophobic R groups
are buried in the interior**, making the molecule **soluble**; a **hydrophobic cleft in each chain
holds the haem group**.

**4.** *Quaternary.* The **four polypeptides associate into one compact globular molecule**, held by
the **same R-group interactions, now acting between chains**.

**5.** *Prosthetic group.* Each chain carries **one haem group, a non-protein iron-containing group with
a central Fe²⁺** that **binds one O₂**; **four haem groups, so up to four O₂ per haemoglobin
molecule**. Haemoglobin is therefore a **conjugated protein**.

*Gap closed: "describe the structure" of a quaternary protein means all four levels plus the prosthetic
group. A quaternary summary on its own caps you at about a third of the marks.*

**(b)** *Choose myoglobin or haemoglobin for an artificial blood substitute, from the 20 mm Hg data and
the two structures.* **[5]**

**1.** **Myoglobin consists of one polypeptide chain with one haem group; it has tertiary structure only
and no quaternary structure, so cooperative binding is impossible.**

**2.** Its **dissociation curve is hyperbolic** and it **saturates at very low oxygen concentrations** —
**84% saturated at 20 mm Hg**.

**3.** **Haemoglobin's four subunits interact cooperatively**: **binding of one O₂ molecule to one
subunit raises the affinity of the remaining subunits for oxygen**, which gives a **sigmoidal curve
rising sharply only between about 15 and 35 mm Hg** — **20% saturated at 20 mm Hg**.

**4.** **Higher percentage saturation at the same oxygen concentration means higher affinity**, so
**myoglobin has the higher affinity**. **A curve lying to the left is higher affinity; a curve shifted
to the right is lower affinity.**

**5.** Decision: **choose haemoglobin.** A carrier must **load in the lungs and unload in respiring
tissue**; **myoglobin's affinity is so high that it holds its oxygen until the concentration falls
below about 20 mm Hg**, which makes it an **excellent local store and a poor transporter** — an
artificial blood built on it would pick oxygen up and never let go of it. Haemoglobin's **sigmoidal
curve means a small fall in oxygen concentration in the tissues produces a large fall in saturation**,
so it **releases a large fraction of its oxygen exactly where it is needed** and reloads fully in the
lungs.

*Gap closed: myoglobin — one chain, tertiary only, one haem group, no cooperativity, hyperbolic curve,
high affinity, excellent store, poor transporter.*

*Gap closed: a curve shifted right is lower affinity, shifted left is higher affinity. Say it as a
rule, not as a guess.*

**(c)** *Predict the fate of a haemoglobin folded with its hydrophilic residues inside, and say why it
could not carry oxygen even in solution.* **[5]**

**1.** Structure: hydrophilic R groups in the interior means **hydrophobic R groups are displaced to
the surface**; the **R-group interactions change**, so the chain **folds into a different 3D
conformation**.

**2.** With **hydrophobic residues on the outside the molecule is insoluble in the aqueous cytoplasm of
the red blood cell**. ("Cannot transport" on its own does not earn the structural mark — **insoluble**
is the word.)

**3.** Prediction: **exposed hydrophobic R groups are energetically unfavourable in an aqueous
environment**, so the molecules **cluster with one another and bury their hydrophobic surfaces against
each other**, **shielding them from the water of the cytosol**. They **precipitate as insoluble
aggregates inside the cell**.

**4.** Consequence: the **aggregates distort the red blood cell**, which may then be **destroyed as it
passes through capillaries**, and the **aggregated haemoglobin is no longer free in solution to pick up
oxygen at all**.

**5.** Even in solution it would fail, because the **hydrophobic cleft in each chain that holds the haem
group is built from hydrophobic residues facing inwards**. With the interior now hydrophilic the
**cleft is lost, the haem group cannot be held in place, and there is no Fe²⁺ presented for oxygen to
bind to**. The **altered conformation also disrupts the subunit interactions on which cooperative
binding depends**.

*Gap closed: buried hydrophilic residues earn "insoluble" plus the loss of the hydrophobic cleft for
the haem group.*

*Gap closed: misfolded proteins aggregate because exposed hydrophobic R groups shield themselves from
the aqueous surroundings by clustering, not merely because the shape is wrong.*

**Source:** RI Prelim P2 Q1(b)(i) "describe the structure of PFK" [4] read across to haemoglobin, with
the four-level key verbatim including "most of its hydrophilic amino acid side chains are on the
external surface while hydrophobic side chains are buried in the interior" (a). ACJC Prelim 2023 P2 Q3,
myoglobin model and the C/D dissociation curves, with the verbatim key including the 84% and 20% data
at 20 mm Hg (b, data verbatim; the blood-substitute decision is [INFERRED — extension, no official
key]). School structured question "state the effect on haemoglobin's structure for the following

structure" [2] (c, verbatim key). The aggregation prediction is [INFERRED — extension, no official
key], built from the verbatim collagenase/collagen solubility key and the misfolded-protein key in the

**Concepts fused:** (1) the four-level description as the standard answer to "describe the structure";
(2) cooperativity as the structural cause of a sigmoidal curve; (3) affinity read off saturation at a
fixed oxygen concentration, and the left/right shift rule; (4) store versus transporter as a
functional judgement; (5) the prosthetic group and the hydrophobic cleft that holds it; (6) hydrophobic
burial as the reason globular proteins are soluble, and its failure as the reason they aggregate.

### Question 6

**(a)** *Account for the whole curve, then predict the effect of doubling the enzyme concentration.*
**[5]**

*The curve has two regions and each has a different limiting factor. Say both.*

**1.** *Initial steep rise — substrate concentration is limiting.* There is an **excess of enzyme, so
not all the active sites are occupied**. **Raising the substrate concentration increases the frequency
of successful collisions between substrate molecules and active sites**, so **more enzyme–substrate
complexes form per unit time** and the **rate rises in proportion**.

**2.** *Plateau — enzyme concentration is limiting.* **All the active sites are occupied / the enzyme is
saturated**, so the **number of active sites, and therefore the enzyme concentration, is now the
limiting factor**. **Further substrate has no effect because each molecule must wait for an active site
to become free**; the rate is at **Vmax**.

**3.** The height of the plateau is set by **how fast the enzyme can turn substrate over, not by how
much substrate is present**.

**4.** *Doubling the enzyme concentration.* Twice as many active sites, so **Vmax doubles and the
plateau sits at twice the height**; the **curve rises more steeply** and **levels off at a higher
substrate concentration**.

**5.** **Km is unchanged**, because Km is a **property of the enzyme and its substrate, not of how much
enzyme is present**.

*Gap closed: a rate-against-substrate curve is answered in two regions with two different limiting
factors — substrate in region 1, number of active sites in region 2. One undivided explanation loses
half the marks.*

**(b)** *Choose between hexokinase I and glucokinase for a glucose-poor cell, and explain a 200-fold Km
difference from a handful of amino acids.* **[5]**

**1.** **Km is the substrate concentration at which the reaction proceeds at half of Vmax.** **The
lower the Km, the less substrate is needed to half-saturate the enzyme, so the higher the affinity of
the enzyme for its substrate.**

**2.** **Hexokinase I has the far lower Km (0.05) and therefore the far higher affinity; glucokinase has
the higher Km (10) and the lower affinity.** **The higher the Km, the lower the affinity — the two are
inverse.**

**3.** Decision: **hexokinase I keeps working where glucose is scarce.** It can **still form
enzyme–substrate complexes and run near its maximum rate at low glucose concentration** — it
**phosphorylates glucose even when the concentration in the cell is low** — whereas **glucokinase needs
roughly two hundred times more glucose even to reach half its maximum rate**, which is why the liver
enzyme only becomes active when blood glucose is high.

**4.** **The amino acids that differ are part of the active site.** [Compulsory — without this the chain
does not connect. A substitution anywhere that left the active site's conformation untouched would not
change Km at all.]

**5.** **The R groups of the differing amino acids have different properties**, so the **R-group
interactions differ, the polypeptide folds differently, and the 3D conformation of the active site
differs**. A **less complementary active site binds glucose less readily**, so **fewer enzyme–substrate
complexes form per unit time**, the affinity is lower and Km is higher.

*Gap closed: Km and affinity are inverse. Higher Km, lower affinity — no exceptions.*

*Gap closed: a single amino acid substitution only explains a fall in activity if you state that the
amino acid is part of the active site. That sentence is the whole mark.*

**(c)** *Deduce how M and N act, name each, and choose the one a cell would use as a pathway switch.*
**[4]**

**1.** **N is a competitive inhibitor.** It is **similar in structure to the substrate** and **binds to
and blocks the active site so the substrate cannot bind**; **adding more substrate lets substrate
molecules outcompete it for the active site**, so the **rate is restored**. **Vmax unchanged, Km
increased.**

**2.** **M is a non-competitive inhibitor.** It **does not resemble the substrate in structure** and
**binds at a site other than the active site, causing a change in the 3D conformation of the active
site** so the **substrate can no longer bind**. **More substrate cannot displace it**, so the **rate is
not restored**. **Vmax falls, Km unchanged.**

**3.** Name both types explicitly. Describing the mechanism without writing **"competitive"** and
**"non-competitive"** leaves a mark on the table.

**4.** The switch: a cell uses **the M type, as allosteric inhibition**. The **inhibitor binds to the
allosteric site — a site other than the active site, on a multimeric enzyme — and triggers a change in
the 3D conformation of the active sites of all the subunits, stabilising the enzyme in its inactive
conformation**; an **allosteric activator does the converse and stabilises the active conformation**.
Because the effect **cannot be overridden by substrate accumulating**, it works as a genuine on/off
switch, which is how **end-product inhibition regulates a whole pathway**. (**Do not mix
non-competitive inhibition with allosteric inhibition** — allosteric describes a multimeric enzyme with
a defined allosteric site.)

*Gap closed: name the inhibitor type. Describing the mechanism without writing the words
"non-competitive" leaves a mark on the table every time.*

*Gap closed: the allosteric site and the active site are different sites. Binding at the allosteric
site changes the shape of the active site — activator stabilises the active form, inhibitor stabilises
the inactive form.*

**Source:** JJC Prelim P1 urease item, verbatim marking points on what limits the rate at P and at Q
(a); the doubling prediction is [INFERRED — extension, no official key]. School structured key "enzyme
B has a lower affinity for its substrate / the higher the Km the lower the affinity of the enzyme for
its substrate", the hexokinase I key "ref. to high affinity of hexokinase I for glucose; this allows
hexokinase I to phosphorylate glucose even when glucose concentrations in the cell are low", and the
catalase amino-acid-position key with its compulsory first marking point "amino acid at position 2 is
part of the active site of enzyme" (b, all verbatim; the paired Km values are [INFERRED — extension,
no official key]). Enzymes-bank reference list R2a/R2b/R3a/R3b, verbatim, including "non-competitive
inhibitor does not resemble the substrate in structure", "allosteric inhibitor binds to the allosteric
site to trigger a change in the 3D conformation of the active sites of all subunits / stabilise the
inactive form of the enzyme. Accept the converse for an allosteric activator" and the instruction

**Concepts fused:** (1) two-region limiting-factor analysis; (2) what changes and what does not when
enzyme concentration changes; (3) Km as an inverse measure of affinity and as a basis for a
suitability judgement; (4) active-site residues as the link between primary structure and catalytic
performance; (5) allosteric versus non-competitive inhibition, and the requirement to name the type.

### Question 7

**(a)** *Draw the condensation of two α-glucose molecules and describe the reverse reaction.* **[6]**

*The four things credited in the drawing:*

**1.** **Name the reaction: condensation.**

**2.** **Draw both monomers** — two α-glucose pyranose rings side by side, each with **CH₂OH on
carbon 6** and **–OH groups on carbons 1 to 4**, with the **–OH on carbon 1 below the plane of the
ring** (that is what makes it α), and the **carbons numbered**.

**3.** **Label the bond fully: α-1,4-glycosidic bond**, drawn as an **oxygen bridge between carbon 1 of
the first ring and carbon 4 of the second**, with the **whole bond boxed or circled**. (Writing
"glycosidic bond" without the **α** and the **1,4** loses the label mark; a line pointing at the bond
rather than a box round it is rejected.)

**4.** **Show the water** — **one molecule of H₂O released**, drawn leaving on a curved arrow.

**5.** The product is **maltose, a disaccharide**.

*The reverse reaction:*

**6.** **Hydrolysis.** **Breaking of the α-1,4-glycosidic bond between the α-glucose residues**, in
which **one water molecule is added per bond broken**, where **H is added to carbon atom 1 of one
α-glucose monomer and OH is added to carbon atom 4 of the other**. **An enzyme — maltase, or amylase
for the polymer — is required.**

*Gap closed: the hydrolysis sentence is exact — H to carbon 1, OH to carbon 4, one water per bond, and
an enzyme is required. "Each anomeric carbon gets a hydroxyl" is not the mark scheme's sentence.*

*Gap closed: your own four-item drawing checklist is the mark scheme's own list — name the reaction,
draw both monomers, label the bond fully, show the water.*

**(b)** *Show that a sugar is present after a negative Benedict's result, and say what happens to the
bonds.* **[4]**

**1.** The negative Benedict's result shows there is **no reducing sugar present**, so the sugar being
transported must be a **non-reducing sugar** such as **sucrose**. Testing for the absence of reducing
sugar first is what makes the rest of the test valid.

**2.** **Boil a fresh sample of the extract with dilute hydrochloric acid.** This **hydrolyses the
glycosidic bond in the non-reducing sugar**, **adding one water molecule per bond broken** and
releasing the **monosaccharides, which are reducing sugars**.

**3.** **Neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate** before testing, because **Benedict's solution is
alkaline and will not work in acid**. [This step is the one most often dropped, and without it the
final test fails.]

**4.** **Add Benedict's solution in excess and heat in a water bath.** A **brick-red precipitate**
confirms that a **reducing sugar has now been produced**, so a **non-reducing sugar was present in the
original extract**.

*Gap closed: acid hydrolysis then neutralise. Both steps, in that order, before the second Benedict's
test.*

**(c)** *What must be different about the bond at a branch point, why nothing else would do, and what
branching achieves.* **[3]**

**1.** A **1,4 bond joins carbon 1 of one residue to carbon 4 of the next, so it continues the chain in
the same line**. **Only a bond formed at carbon 6 can leave the line of the chain**, because **carbon 6
sits on the CH₂OH group projecting out of the ring rather than in the ring itself**.

**2.** So the branch point is an **α-1,6-glycosidic bond, formed between the –OH groups on carbon 1 of a
glucose residue in one chain and carbon 6 of a residue in the other, releasing a water molecule in a
condensation reaction catalysed by a branching enzyme**.

**3.** Branching gives **many free ends**, so **many enzyme molecules can act on the same glycogen
molecule at the same time** and **glucose is released rapidly for respiration**; it also makes the
molecule **compact**, so **more glucose is stored per unit volume**.

*Gap closed: a branch requires a bond involving carbon 6 specifically, because carbon 6 is the only
position that leaves the line of the chain.*

can be catalysed by maltase to produce glucose molecules" [3] and the condensation-drawing item with

**Concepts fused:** (1) condensation drawn to the mark scheme's own four-item standard; (2) the exact
hydrolysis sentence, including the enzyme requirement; (3) reducing versus non-reducing sugars and the
acid-then-neutralise sequence as bond chemistry rather than recipe; (4) bond position as the structural

### Question 8

**(a)** *Which store the liver draws on after thirty minutes and after three days.* **[4]**

**1.** *Thirty minutes — glycogen.* It is **highly branched, so there are many free ends** at which
**glycogen phosphorylase can act simultaneously**, and **glucose is released rapidly**.

**2.** The **glucose released enters respiration directly, in few steps**, so **ATP is available
quickly**; the liver also **releases it into the blood to maintain blood glucose concentration**. This
is the store for **immediate demand**.

**3.** *Three days — fat.* The **glycogen store is limited in quantity and would be exhausted**. **Fat
yields roughly twice the energy per gram**, because **fatty acids are far more reduced — more C–H bonds
and proportionately less oxygen per molecule** — so **a given mass lasts far longer**, and there is
**no practical limit on how much can be stored**.

**4.** Fat could not have served the first period, because it is **slow to mobilise** — it must be
**hydrolysed to fatty acids and glycerol**, and the **fatty acids pass through many oxidation steps
before ATP is produced**. The two stores answer **two different demands, speed and density**, which is
why the cell keeps both.

*Gap closed: answer this one in the molecules — mobilisation rate against energy density. Reasoning
through the pancreas and hormones does not reach the marking points.*

**(b)** *Predict the fate of a cell that hydrolyses its glycogen into glucose it cannot export.* **[4]**

**1.** **Glycogen is one very large molecule built from thousands of glucose residues**, so it
contributes **very few solute particles**; **most of its hydroxyl groups project into the interior of
the helices and hydrogen-bond with one another rather than with water**, so it is **insoluble and
osmotically almost inactive** and can be **stored in large quantities without appreciably affecting the
water potential of the cell**.

**2.** Hydrolysing it releases **a very large number of small, soluble glucose molecules**, so the
**solute concentration of the cytoplasm rises sharply**.

**3.** The **water potential of the cytoplasm becomes much more negative than that of the surrounding
tissue fluid**, so **water enters by osmosis, moving down the water potential gradient from the less
negative to the more negative water potential**. Prediction: the **cell swells and, having no cell wall
to resist the pressure, may burst / lyse**.

**4.** **Fat would have caused no such problem.** Triglycerides are **non-polar and hydrophobic**, so
they are **not in solution at all and carry no water of hydration**; stored as droplets they contribute
**essentially nothing to the solute concentration**, and fat carries **about twice the energy per
gram**, so **fewer molecules are needed for the same energy in the first place**. For the same energy
content the osmotic effect runs **free glucose > glycogen > fat**.

**5.** The rule underneath all of it: **osmotic effect follows the number of dissolved solute
particles, not the mass and not the energy content**. **Polymerising cuts the particle count; being
hydrophobic takes the molecule out of the count altogether.**

*Gap closed: fat beats glycogen on osmotic effect because it is hydrophobic and carries no water of
hydration, not merely because it is a large molecule.*

**(c)** *Why a high-amylose rice raises blood glucose more slowly.* **[4]**

**1.** **Amylose is unbranched and joined only by α-1,4-glycosidic bonds; amylopectin is branched, with
α-1,6-glycosidic bonds at the branch points.**

**2.** **A higher amylose content means a lower amylopectin content in the same total mass**, so there
are **fewer branch points and therefore fewer free ends**. (**Fewer**, not none — an amylose chain
still has ends.)

**3.** **Each free end is a point at which amylase can bind and act**, so **fewer ends means fewer
enzyme–substrate complexes forming at any one time** and a **smaller effective surface for the
enzyme**. **Amylose also coils into tight helices whose hydroxyl groups hydrogen-bond internally**,
making the chain **less accessible to the enzyme**.

**4.** So the starch is **hydrolysed to maltose and then glucose more slowly**, **glucose is absorbed
into the blood more slowly**, and **blood glucose rises more gradually**.

*Gap closed: the missing link is "more amylose means less amylopectin", and only then fewer branch
points → fewer free ends → fewer simultaneous points of attack. And it is fewer free ends, never no
free ends.*

**Source:** School structured keys on storage polysaccharides, verbatim marking points including "most
of the hydrophilic OH groups of the glucose residues project into the interior of the helices and form
hydrogen bonds with one another", "they can be stored in large quantities without affecting the osmotic
potential of cells" and "branched structure allows many enzymes / large surface area for enzymes to act
on it at any one time to release glucose quickly" (a, b, c). The three timeframes in (a), the
hydrolysed-glycogen scenario in (b) and the blood-glucose framing in (c) are all [INFERRED — extension,
no official key] settings placed on top of verbatim keys; the ranking against fat rests on the verbatim
lipid energy-density and hydrophobicity marking points. The amylose/amylopectin structural contrast is
verbatim from the school key "the amylose component is unbranched whilst the amylopectin component is
branched; the glucose residues in the amylose component are linked by (1→4) glycosidic bonds, whilst
the glucose residues in the amylopectin component are linked by both (1→4) and (1→6) glycosidic bonds".

**Concepts fused:** (1) mobilisation rate against energy density as the reason for two stores; (2)
particle count as the determinant of osmotic effect; (3) hydrophobicity removing a molecule from the
osmotic ledger entirely; (4) branch density → free ends → simultaneous enzyme attack; (5) reading a
composition percentage as a statement about what else is present.
