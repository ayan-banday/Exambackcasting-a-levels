### Biology H2 (9477) · Book 1 · attempt on Loom/Wispr Flow, reasoning aloud, BEFORE reading answers

**9 questions, 138 marks, 25 parts.** Rebuilt to the standard you set after Cluster 2: short stem,
long answer. Every stem is one or two sentences. No stem lists the headings to compare on, and no stem
tells you which options are wrong. The concepts live in the answer, not in a longer chain of small
parts, so most questions are three broad parts and two are only two.

**Cumulative over Clusters 1, 2 and 3.** Five parts reach backwards and make you say the earlier
material cold: Q5(a) the bond built on the ribosome and the folding that follows it, Q6(c) what a
carbohydrate chain is joined to on a glycolipid, Q7(b) fluidity from the fatty acid chemistry, Q8(c) a
carrier protein set against an enzyme active site, Q9(c) a transport protein denatured by heat. A
Cluster 1 or 2 gap surfacing here is worth more than a clean Cluster 3 answer, so say it out loud.

**Three MCQs, all with the picture doing the work** — the centriole cross-section in Q4(a), the
four-line oxygen graph in Q7(a), the scale-drawn membrane in Q9(a). The marks are for the
option-by-option reasoning, so the letter on its own is worth almost nothing.

**How to run it.** One part at a time, aloud, mark scheme untouched. Say the reasoning chain, not the
conclusion. When you hit a blank, say the blank in plain words, because the transcript is the gap list.

**What the marks reward.** A structural feature stated without the function it produces earns nothing.
Water potential is **more negative** or **less negative**, never higher or lower. Every compare and

### Question 1

**(a)** *Compare HPV, T4 bacteriophage and HIV, then judge which shared feature best proves none is a
cell.* **[6]**

**1.** The HPV genome is **DNA, double stranded and circular**; T4 bacteriophage contains
**double-stranded DNA**; HIV contains **positive-sense single-stranded RNA, 2 strands**.

**2.** HPV **DNA is associated with histone protein**, whereas **T4 DNA is not**.

**3.** In all three, the **genome is enclosed by a protein capsid built from individual protein subunits
(capsomeres)**.

**4.** **HPV has a spherical capsid, T4 an icosahedral capsid head, HIV a conical capsid.**

**5.** **HPV has 2 capsid protein types (L1 and L2); T4 has only 1.**

**6.** **HIV is enveloped; T4 is a naked virus**, and HPV shows no envelope in Fig. 1.1.

**7.** **T4 has a sheath, collar, tail and tail fibres; HPV has none.** **HIV has 2 surface glycoproteins
(gp120 and gp41) for host recognition and entry; T4 attaches by tail fibres instead.**

**8.** **HIV carries reverse transcriptase / integrase / HIV protease; T4 carries lysozyme.**

*The judgement.* [INFERRED — extension, no official key]

**9.** The strongest shared evidence is that **none of the three contains ribosomes, cytoplasm or any
membrane-bound organelle** — each is **only nucleic acid plus protein, with or without a host-derived
envelope** — so **none contains the enzymes needed for metabolism, replication, transcription or
translation**.

**10.** Small size fails, because **a mycoplasma is smaller than a pandoravirus and is still a cell**.
Carrying one type of nucleic acid is a **genome accounting fact**, not proof the particle cannot run
itself. Obligate parasitism is a **consequence of the missing machinery, not the cause**.

**(b)** *How membrane structure permits budding, and whether HIV entry is that process reversed.* **[5]**

**1.** The **phospholipid bilayer gives the membrane fluidity / lateral movement within each monolayer**.

**2.** The phospholipids are **held only by weak hydrophobic interactions between their hydrocarbon
tails**.

**3.** So the **cell surface membrane can change shape / be pushed outwards — budding** — and **viral
glycoproteins can be embedded in it as exit points**.

*Contrast, any 2:*

**4.** Budding is **evagination of the host cell surface membrane**; HIV entry is **fusion of the viral
envelope with the host cell surface membrane**.

**5.** Budding **removes membrane from the cell**; HIV entry **adds membrane to it**.

**6.** Budding uses **viral glycoproteins already in the cell surface membrane as exit points**; HIV entry
uses **gp120 on the viral envelope binding the CD4 receptor on the cell surface membrane**.

**7.** The judgement: it is **not simply the same process reversed**. Both change the membrane's surface
area in opposite directions, but **budding is driven from inside by the nucleocapsid pushing outward,
whereas entry is driven by a receptor–ligand recognition event on the outside**, and only entry requires
complementary binding before the membranes merge.

R: "budding vs no budding", "fusion vs no fusion", "loss vs no loss of membrane" — where an equivalent
process exists, describe the difference **in that feature**.

ATP**, again saturating and far slower per molecule; and **osmosis would be measured on water rather than
on a solute**.

**(c)** *Carrier against enzyme, the channel exception, and region Y.* [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 2 §G.
INFERRED — extension, no official key. Region Y and the ratio are verbatim keys.] **[6]**

**1.** Shared: **both are specific because the site that does the binding has a shape and charge
distribution complementary to one particular solute or substrate**, and in both cases that site is **built
from R groups brought together by the folding of the tertiary structure**, which is why **losing the
tertiary structure destroys both**.

**2.** Shared, any: **both change conformation when the ligand binds** — the enzyme by **induced fit, the
active site moulding around the substrate**, the carrier by rotating between an outward-facing and an
inward-facing shape; **both are globular proteins**; **both are unchanged at the end and are reused**;
**both saturate, because the number of sites is fixed**, which is why the drug B column plateaus at 13
exactly as an enzyme's rate plateaus at Vmax; **both can be inhibited competitively by a molecule
resembling the ligand or non-competitively by one that distorts the conformation**.

**3.** Different: the carrier **moves its solute from one side of the membrane to the other and releases it
chemically unchanged — only its location has changed**. The enzyme **lowers the activation energy of a
reaction and converts the substrate into a chemically different product**, making and breaking covalent
bonds within it. A carrier makes and breaks no bonds in what it carries.

**4.** A channel is specific by **geometry and charge rather than by binding**: it is a **hydrophilic pore
through the bilayer, lined with polar and charged R groups**, so **only solutes small enough for the pore
diameter and carrying the compatible charge pass**. **Nothing binds and no conformational change occurs**,
which is why a channel moves solute **far faster than a carrier**. Named example: **aquaporin, specific for
water**. Gated channels do open and close, but the gate answers a signal such as voltage or a ligand at a
separate site, **not an induced fit by the transported solute**.

**5.** Region Y is level because **the active transport carrier proteins are all occupied and cycling at
their maximum rate**, so adding more external X changes nothing. This is the same saturation argument as
point 2, arriving on a different graph.

**6.** The maximum intracellular concentration is **0.04 mol dm⁻³**, reached at an external concentration
of **0.002 mol dm⁻³**, so the cell holds X at **0.04 ÷ 0.002 = 20 times** the external concentration.
That ratio is itself the second piece of evidence for active transport: **a passive process cannot hold
the inside twenty times more concentrated than the outside.**

**7.** R! an inhibitor as the cause of the plateau. A respiratory or non-competitive inhibitor would
**depress the whole curve** — the steep initial rise would not have happened, since active transport needs
ATP from the outset — and would **lower or abolish the plateau** rather than produce the normal maximum
shown.

### Question 9

**(a)** *Audit the four accounts of movement across the membrane in Fig. 9.1.* Key: **D**. [INFERRED — no
official key; the option audit is a further extension] **[4]**

**1.** **D is correct.** Solution 1 holds **roughly 21 solute particles against roughly 10 in solution 2**,
so solution 1 has the **more negative water potential** and water moves **from solution 2 to solution 1**
by osmosis. Oxygen is drawn **smaller than the pores** and is absent from solution 2, so it **diffuses down
its concentration gradient from solution 1 to solution 2**. The sodium and chloride ions are drawn **larger
than the pores**, so **neither crosses**.

**2.** **A is wrong on the ions.** It has them crossing, when the diagram draws them **larger than the
membrane pores**; the correct movement is that **neither ion crosses at all**. Its account of water and
oxygen is defensible.

**3.** **B is wrong on the water.** It sends water from solution 1 to solution 2, that is from the more
negative to the less negative water potential, which is backwards; water moves **from solution 2 to
solution 1**. It gets the ions right.

**4.** **C is wrong on the oxygen.** It claims every solute is too large, when the **oxygen molecules are
drawn smaller than the gaps** and are present only in solution 1; oxygen **diffuses from solution 1 to
solution 2**. It gets the water right. Each distractor is the key with **exactly one of the three movements
corrupted**, which is why reading an option whole and asking "does it feel right" fails here.

**(b)** *Water potential arithmetic for a protoplast.* [INFERRED — extension, no official key. There is no
numerical water-potential question anywhere in the corpus; this is built on the real protoplast stem from
School Prelim 2023 P2 Q1a, which asks the qualitative version.] **[4]**

**1.** A protoplast has had its **cell wall removed**, so it **cannot develop a pressure potential** and
**ψp = 0**. Therefore **ψcell = ψs + ψp = −750 + 0 = −750 kPa**.

**2.** Against **−450 kPa** the bathing solution is the **less negative**, so water moves **into the
protoplast**, which **swells and bursts (lyses)**, since there is **no wall to generate an opposing
pressure potential**.

**3.** Against **−750 kPa** the water potentials are **equal**, so there is **no net movement of water** and
the protoplast keeps its volume — which is why protoplasts are kept in such a solution. Against **−1100
kPa** the solution is **more negative**, so water moves **out** and the protoplast **shrinks / shrivels**.
Reject "turgid" and "plasmolysed" throughout, because both terms require a cell wall.

**4.** With the wall intact and **ψp = +300 kPa**, **ψcell = −750 + 300 = −450 kPa**, so the walled cell is
in equilibrium with the **−450 kPa** solution. The same cell contents give **two different water potentials
depending on whether a wall is present to push back**, and the wall is what converts lysis into turgor.

**(c)** *The contractile vacuole membrane, and what 45 °C does to it.* Any answer not linked to a structure
is rejected. **[6]**

**1.** **Protein pumps / carriers for ions are embedded in the contractile vacuole membrane** (channels and
"simple proteins" are rejected), which **actively transport ions into the lumen**, making **the lumen's
water potential more negative than the cytoplasm's** ("positive or higher / lower water potential" is
rejected), **so excess water enters the vacuole by osmosis and is expelled by exocytosis**.

**2.** The **hydrophobic core of the phospholipid bilayer** makes the membrane **impermeable to ions**,
which **allows ions to accumulate in the lumen and prevents them leaving**, keeping the lumen's solute
potential high.

**3.** The membrane is **built from phospholipids in a bilayer held by weak hydrophobic interactions
between the fatty acid tails**, which **gives it fluidity**, and that **allows the vacuole to fuse with the
cell surface membrane for exocytosis** and allows **many small vesicles to fuse with the vacuole so it can
expand**.

**4.** **Aquaporins / water channels in the vacuole membrane allow facilitated diffusion of water into the
lumen.**

Examiner comment: use the stimulus to contextualise that ions are pumped into the lumen and change the
solute potential so that water enters. Channels also let water *leave* the vacuole, so it is the **high
concentration of actively pumped ions that keeps the water there, not impermeability of the membrane to
water** — the impermeability is to ions. And **water is not pumped in or out; the cell moves solutes and
the water follows**.

*Heat at 45 °C.* [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 2 §E and §B, Cluster 1 §C3. INFERRED — extension, no official
key]

**5.** Heat **increases the vibration of the atoms in the pump protein**, which **disrupts the hydrogen
bonds, ionic bonds and hydrophobic interactions between the R groups** holding it in shape. What is lost is
the **tertiary conformation** (and the quaternary, if the pump is multi-subunit); the **primary structure
survives, because peptide bonds and disulfide bonds are covalent and are not broken by heat**. Saying heat
breaks the peptide bonds scores zero.

**6.** The failure chain: the **binding site is no longer complementary to the ion**, so **ions are no
longer actively transported into the lumen**, so the **solute potential of the lumen is not made
sufficiently negative**, so the **water potential of the lumen is no longer more negative than the
cytoplasm's**, so **water no longer enters by osmosis** and the vacuole does not fill. Water therefore
**accumulates in the cytoplasm**, and since an amoeba has **no cell wall to generate an opposing pressure
potential**, the cell **swells and bursts (lyses)** — the same argument as the protoplast in (b).

**7.** The bilayer itself is not denatured, because **denaturation is the loss of a 3D conformation and a
phospholipid has no tertiary structure to lose**. What heat does to it is **raise the kinetic energy of the
fatty acid tails and weaken the hydrophobic interactions between them**, so the membrane becomes **more
fluid and more permeable**, which lets pumped ions **leak back out of the lumen** and makes the failure
worse. That change is **reversible on cooling**; the denaturation of the pump is not.

**Concepts fused:** (1) pore size versus particle size as what decides whether a solute crosses at all;
