**(b)** *Why arctic foxes have more cholesterol than giraffes, why the value is a percentage, and how
unsaturation makes a membrane fluid.* **[6]**

**1.** **Arctic foxes live in cooler environments.**

**2.** **A higher percentage of cholesterol in their membranes will prevent the close packing of
phospholipids at lower temperatures, increasing membrane fluidity / preventing the membrane from
solidifying.**

**3.** **The cell membrane may be of different length / width / size / mass in different animals.**

**4.** Expressing it as a percentage **allows valid comparison of the values in different animals.**

*Fluidity from the fatty acid chemistry [2].* [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 1 §C3 and §C5. INFERRED —
extension, no official key]

**5.** Neighbouring tails are held to each other by **weak hydrophobic interactions — forces of
attraction between non-polar molecules** — and it is the **sum of many individually weak interactions**
that holds the bilayer together while still letting each phospholipid **move laterally within its own
monolayer**.

**6.** Each **C=C double bond puts a kink in the hydrocarbon tail**, so **the tails cannot pack closely
together**, so **the hydrophobic interactions between them are less extensive**, so **less thermal energy
is needed to keep them moving** and the membrane **stays fluid at a lower temperature**. Run the chain in
that order — a kink stated without the packing step and the interaction step earns one mark at most.

**7.** They are not the same mechanism, and the difference is direction. **Unsaturation acts in one
direction only**: the kinks are built into the phospholipid itself, they always reduce packing, so they
**raise fluidity at every temperature**. **Cholesterol acts in both directions**: at **low temperature it
sits between the tails and prevents them packing into a regular solid array, so it stops the membrane
solidifying**, while at **high temperature it restrains the movement of the tails, so it reduces
fluidity**. Cholesterol therefore **narrows the range over which fluidity varies (a buffer)**, whereas
unsaturation **shifts the whole range one way**. This is also what Q7(a) is showing: cholesterol lowers
oxygen permeability most at the cold end.

**(b)** *Rate-to-concentration ratios for substance W, the process involved, and the counterfactual
tables.* The process is **passive simple diffusion**. [INFERRED — extension, no official key] **[4]**

**1.** The ratios of rate to concentration are **48/4 = 12.0**, **109/8 = 13.6**, **239/16 = 14.9**,

**2.** The ratio is **roughly constant and slightly rising, with no sign at all of falling towards a
plateau**: doubling the concentration roughly doubles the rate every time, right up to the highest
concentration tested.

**3.** That is the signature of **passive simple diffusion**, in which the rate is **directly proportional
to the concentration gradient and is not limited by any protein**, so **no transport protein is required**.

**4.** For the transport to have been **facilitated diffusion**, the table would have had to **flatten into
a plateau at high concentration**, because a fixed number of transport proteins **saturates**.

**5.** For it to have been **active transport**, the same plateau would have to appear, and in addition the
**rate would have to be largely independent of external concentration** and would collapse if a
respiratory inhibitor were added. (For bulk uptake by endocytosis the uptake would be in **discrete
packets requiring ATP**, again showing saturation and, being far slower per molecule, nothing like this
smooth proportionality.)

**(c)** *Carrier protein against enzyme active site, the channel exception, and why region Y is level.*
**[6]**

*Carrier, enzyme and channel [4].* [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 2 §G. INFERRED — extension, no official key]

**1.** Shared feature, and it must be one of the two: **both are specific because the site that does the
binding has a shape and charge distribution complementary to one particular solute or substrate**. In
both cases that site is **built from the R groups of amino acids brought together by the folding of the
tertiary structure**, which is why **losing the tertiary structure destroys both**.

**2.** Second shared feature, any one: **both change conformation when the ligand binds** — the enzyme by
**induced fit, the active site moulding around the substrate**, the carrier by rotating between an
outward-facing and an inward-facing shape; **both are globular proteins**; **both are unchanged at the
end and are reused**; **both saturate, because the number of sites is fixed**, which is why the drug B
column plateaus at 13 exactly as an enzyme's rate plateaus at Vmax; and **both can be inhibited
competitively by a molecule resembling the ligand or non-competitively by a molecule that distorts the
conformation**.

**3.** What the carrier does that the enzyme does not: **it moves its solute from one side of the
membrane to the other. The solute is released chemically unchanged — only its location has changed.**

**4.** What the enzyme does that the carrier does not: **it lowers the activation energy of a reaction
and converts the substrate into a chemically different product**, making and breaking covalent bonds
within it. A carrier makes and breaks no bonds in what it carries.

**5.** The channel is specific by **geometry and charge rather than by binding**. It is a **hydrophilic
pore through the bilayer, lined with polar and charged R groups**, so **only solutes small enough to fit
the pore diameter and carrying the compatible charge can pass**; **nothing binds and no conformational
change occurs**, which is why a channel moves solute **far faster than a carrier**. Named example:
**aquaporin, a channel specific for water**. (Gated channels do open and close, but the gate responds to
a signal such as voltage or a ligand at a separate site; **it is not the transported solute inducing a
fit**.)

*Region Y and the accumulation ratio [2].* Keys: **region Y is level because the active transport carriers
are operating at their maximum rate**, and the maximum intracellular concentration is **20 times greater**
than the external.

**6.** The plateau is the point at which **every carrier protein is occupied and cycling as fast as it
can**, so adding more external X changes nothing. This is the same saturation argument as point 2 above,
arriving on a different graph — which is the point of putting the two halves in one part.

**7.** It is not a respiratory inhibitor or a non-competitive inhibitor, because either would **depress the
whole curve, not just region Y** — the steep initial rise would not have happened at all, since active
transport needs ATP from the outset, and an inhibitor inactivating the carriers would **lower or abolish
the plateau**, not produce the normal maximum shown.

**8.** It is not an increasing diffusion gradient either, because the stem states X is **actively
transported**, and in any case a diffusion gradient would drive X **outwards**, since the inside is far
more concentrated than the outside.

**9.** The maximum intracellular concentration is **0.04 mol dm⁻³** and it is reached when the external
concentration is **0.002 mol dm⁻³**, so the ratio is **0.04 ÷ 0.002 = 20 times greater**.

**10.** That accumulation ratio is itself the second piece of evidence for active transport: **a passive
process cannot hold the inside twenty times more concentrated than the outside.**

### What was cut from the 13-question set, and why

- **Nine multiple-choice items are gone.** Three survive, and only because the figure carries the
  question: the end-on centriole micrograph in Q4(a), the four-line oxygen graph in Q7(a), and the
  scale-drawn membrane in Q9(a). Every isolated recall MCQ was cut, and four of the cut ones were kept as
  *content* by rewriting them as written audits inside a larger question — the centriole trap into Q4(c),
  the endocytosis/exocytosis trap into Q5(c), the fluid-mosaic evidence set into Q6(a), and the substance
  W table into Q8(b).
- **The cell-theory MCQ, the non-living-evidence MCQ and the dengue-domains part were all cut as
  duplicates** of the 14-mark essay, which already forces the three tenets, the acellularity argument and
  the living-characteristics argument out of you in one go.
- **The membrane-status MCQ** (which structures are bounded by one membrane, which by two, which by none)
  was cut as a duplicate of the four-row organelle audit now in Q4(c).
- **Three of the four microscopy calculations were cut.** The magnification-from-a-stated-actual-size MCQ,
  the chloroplast-arrow magnification and the prokaryote-versus-eukaryote size ratio all test the same
  arithmetic as the surviving scale-bar calculation. One scale bar plus one graticule calibration covers
  the skill without asking for the same unit conversion four times.
- **The cell-fractionation MCQ was cut** because differential centrifugation is not a 9477 learning
  outcome in its own right and the question tests organelle size ranking, which Q4 already reaches.
- **The three-way "compare HIV and a typical bacterium" part was cut** as a duplicate of the HPV/T4/HIV
  comparison in Q1(a) and the prokaryote work in Q3.
- **The "which visible features suggest a prokaryote" MCQ was cut** because Q3(a) and Q3(c) already make
  you name the visible prokaryotic features and contrast them with the eukaryotic equivalents in prose.
- **The stomatal-opening part was cut.** Its mechanism — ions pumped in, water potential made more
  negative, water follows by osmosis — is the same chain as the contractile vacuole in Q9(c), which is the
  richer of the two because it also asks about the membrane structure that makes the pumping possible.
- **The 20-mark specimen essay on "different ways in which molecules can enter cells" was cut** as a
  wholesale repeat of Q3(b), Q5(c), Q8 and Q9 stacked end to end. Its one piece of unique content, the
  prokaryotic entry routes and why a walled cell cannot do endocytosis, is now Q3(b). Keep the full essay
  for a Book 1 synthesis paper, where it can be answered once against everything.
