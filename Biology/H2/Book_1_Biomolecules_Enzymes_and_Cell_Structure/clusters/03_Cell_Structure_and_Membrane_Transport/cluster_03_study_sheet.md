# PART 1 — CELL STRUCTURE

## A. The cell theory, and why cells stay small

**A1. The theory.** The **cell is the fundamental unit of structure, function and organisation**, and
**all new cells come from pre-existing cells**. Built by **Schleiden (1838, plants) → Schwann (1839,
plants and animals) → Braun (1845, cells as basic units of life) → Virchow (1855, all cells from
pre-existing cells)**. Microscopy was the enabling technology: the theory could not exist before the
instrument.

**A2. Five functions define a fundamental unit of life:** **take in raw materials**; **extract energy
and synthesise its own molecules**; **grow in an organised manner**; **reproduce after its own kind**;
**respond and adapt**.

**A3. Cells are size-bounded at both ends.** The **lower limit** is the minimum space to contain the
essential machinery (DNA, enzymes). The **upper limit is set by the surface area to volume ratio** —
as a cell enlarges, volume grows faster than surface area, so exchange with the environment cannot keep
up. **Consequence: organisms get bigger by having more cells, not bigger cells.** That reasoning chain
is examined directly and is worth more than the fact.

---

## B. Prokaryote versus eukaryote

**B1. The defining difference:** prokaryotes have **neither a true nucleus nor membrane-bound
organelles**; eukaryotes have both. Size: **prokaryote 0.2–2.0 µm, eukaryote 10–100 µm**.

**B2. The bacterial cell as LO (d) requires it:** **small and unicellular**, **peptidoglycan cell
wall**, **circular DNA lacking histones**, **70S ribosomes**, and **no membrane-bound organelles**.
Learn those five as a set — the LO names them explicitly, so they are the mark scheme.

**B3. Downstream consequences** worth naming: **binary fission rather than mitosis**; **no cytoskeleton
and no cytoplasmic streaming**; flagella built from **two protein building blocks** rather than
multiple microtubules.

> **Trap.** Generalising a eukaryote fact to bacteria ("bacteria have 80S ribosomes", "bacterial DNA is
> in a nucleus") is a standing MCQ distractor. Ribosome size is the usual bait: **eukaryotic 80S,
> prokaryotic 70S — and mitochondria and chloroplasts also have 70S**, which is itself a question.

## C. Compartmentalisation is the organising principle

**C1. Why membranes inside the cell at all — two advantages, both examinable:**

1. They **maintain characteristic differences between organelle contents and the cytosol**, allowing
   **incompatible processes to run simultaneously** in one cell.
2. They **increase membrane surface area for embedding enzymes and proteins**, raising reaction
   efficiency.

**C2. The organelles.** Learn structure and function as a pair, and learn the third column, because
"which cell would have many of these, and why" is how this gets examined rather than as recall.

| Organelle | Structure | Function | Abundant in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nucleus** | **envelope of two membranes perforated by nuclear pores**; contains **chromatin** and a **nucleolus** | encloses and **protects DNA from the metabolically active cytoplasm**; pores **allow mRNA and rRNA out, proteins in** | all nucleated cells (**red blood cells are the notable exception**) |
| **Nucleolus** | **dense mass**, composed of **DNA carrying rRNA genes, RNA and protein** | **site of manufacture of ribosomal subunits**; **disperses before nuclear division, reassembles at the end of telophase** | cells with heavy protein-synthesis demand |
| **Rough ER** | **sheet-like cisternae, rough because ribosomes stud the cytosolic face** | **bound ribosomes are the site of protein synthesis**; the **ER lumen is the site of protein folding**; products leave enclosed in **transport vesicles** | **cells active in protein secretion** |
| **Smooth ER** | **network of tubules lacking ribosomes** | **synthesis of lipids** (including steroids), **metabolism of carbohydrates**, **detoxification of drugs and poisons**, **storage of calcium ions** | **cells active in hormone secretion**; liver (detoxification); skeletal muscle (Ca²⁺) |
| **Golgi apparatus** | **stack of flattened membrane-bound cisternae** with a **cis (forming) face and a trans (maturing) face** | **modification and packaging of ER products** by **glycosylation and trimming**; buds **secretory vesicles** and **lysosomes** from the trans face | **cells active in any form of secretion** |
| **Lysosome** | **spherical sac bounded by a single membrane**, **electron-dense**, interior **pH 5**, contains **hydrolytic enzymes** | **digestion of ingested material**, **autophagy of worn-out organelles**, **autolysis**; compartmentation **provides optimal pH and protects cellular contents from hydrolysis** | phagocytic and defensive cells |
| **Mitochondrion** | **double membrane**; **outer smooth, inner highly convoluted into cristae**; matrix houses **enzymes, circular DNA, RNA and 70S ribosomes** | **site of cellular respiration, generates ATP**; **cristae increase surface area for enzyme systems**; **matrix is the site of the Krebs cycle**; the **inner membrane is impermeable to protons**, which is what allows the proton gradient | **number corresponds to level of metabolic activity** |
| **Chloroplast** | **double-membrane envelope**; **stroma** with circular DNA and starch grains; **thylakoids stacked into grana** | **site of photosynthesis**; **thylakoids are the site of the light-dependent reactions** and **increase surface area for pigment attachment**; **stroma is the site of the Calvin cycle**; **thylakoid membrane impermeable to protons** | **photosynthetic cells only** |
| **Ribosome** | **two subunits of protein and rRNA**; **80S eukaryotic, 70S prokaryotic** | **site of protein synthesis**. **Bound** ribosomes make proteins **for export or for organelles**; **free** ribosomes make proteins **that stay in the cytosol or nucleus** | protein-synthesising cells |
| **Centriole** | **pairs at right angles**, each of **nine triplets of microtubules in a ring** | replicate before division and **act as the microtubule organising centre for spindle fibres** | **animal cells; absent in plant cells** |
| **Plant cell wall** | **cellulose microfibrils in a matrix of other polysaccharides** | **mechanical support and protection**; **allows a pressure potential to develop**; **prevents osmotic bursting**; pathway for water and mineral salts | plant cells (and fungi) |
| **Plant vacuole** | large sac bounded by the **tonoplast**, containing **cell sap** | **storage**, **disposal of toxic by-products**, **pigments**, **protection**, and **cell growth and elongation as water accumulates** | mature plant cells |

> **The 28% trap lives here.** A **centriole is NOT enclosed by a membrane** — it is nine microtubule
> triplets. Not every organelle is membrane-bound. Ribosomes, the cytoskeleton and centrioles are not.

**C3. The secretory pathway is the integrating story.** This one sequence connects five organelles and
is the standard way (b) and (c) get examined in application form:

**Nucleus (gene) → mRNA → ribosome bound to rER → polypeptide enters the ER lumen and folds → transport vesicle → Golgi cis face → progressively modified moving cis to trans → secretory vesicle buds from the trans face → fuses with the plasma membrane → exocytosis.**

Two details that earn extra marks: **different Golgi cisternae contain different enzymes**, which is
why modification is progressive rather than one-step; and **the plasma membrane is not part of the
endomembrane system**, though it continuously interacts with it by receiving and producing vesicles.

## D. Microscopy

**D1. Magnification and resolution are different ideas.** **Magnification is how much larger the image
is than the object. Resolution is the ability to distinguish two adjacent points as separate.** A
higher magnification with no gain in resolution just gives a bigger blur. Treating the two words as
synonyms is a standing trap.

**D2. Light versus electron.** The electron microscope's advantage is **resolution**, because
**electrons have a much shorter wavelength than light**. **TEM shows internal detail; SEM shows the
3D exterior.**

**D3. Magnification calculation.** **Magnification = image size ÷ actual size.** Rearrange for whichever
is asked, and watch the units — this is a **quantitative** node, so drill variants until mechanical.
For practical work, LO (b) names the **eyepiece graticule** and **stage micrometer**, used together to
calibrate the graticule against a known scale.

**D4. Cell fractionation:** **homogenisation** then **differential centrifugation**, done in an
**isotonic, buffered solution at 4 °C** — isotonic to stop osmotic damage, buffered to prevent pH
denaturation, cold to slow enzyme activity.

# PART 2 — MEMBRANES AND TRANSPORT

## E. What membranes are for

Five functions, and the exam asks for them by name:

1. **Definition of the cell's boundaries** — a **selectively permeable phospholipid bilayer**.
2. **Organisation and localisation of function** — electron carriers in thylakoid and inner mitochondrial membranes; hydrolytic enzymes confined to lysosomes.
3. **Regulation of the cell's contents** — uptake and accumulation, waste removal, confinement of chemicals.
4. **Signal transduction** — receptors on the outer surface detect signals and trigger internal responses.
5. **Cell-to-cell communication** — proteins binding the extracellular matrix or other cells' surfaces to mediate adhesion.

---

## F. The fluid mosaic model

**F1. Two named components:** the **fluid phospholipid bilayer** and the **mosaic of proteins** embedded
in it. Four characteristics: **asymmetry**, **lateral fluidity**, **dynamic protein movement** (some
anchored by microfilaments), and **amphipathic organisation with a hydrophobic core**. Evidence comes
from **freeze-fracture electron microscopy** showing a cobblestoned interior.

**F2. Where fluidity comes from.** Phospholipids are **held together primarily by hydrophobic
interactions between the fatty acid tails**. Because these are **weak interactions, phospholipids move
freely laterally**. **Transverse flipping is rare, because the hydrophilic head would have to cross the
hydrophobic core.** That asymmetry between easy lateral movement and rare flipping is the whole
mechanism.

**F3. Four factors affecting fluidity** — each needs the middle reasoning step, not just the direction:

| Factor | Effect | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | **fluidity rises with temperature** | **low:** kinetic energy of the chains falls, chains **pack tightly**, **hydrophobic interactions increase**, motion restricted, bilayer **semisolid**. **high:** kinetic energy rises, **lateral movement, chain flexing and transverse flipping increase**, hydrophobic interactions are overcome, **more space between phospholipids** |
| **Fatty acid chain length** | **longer chains → less fluid** | **longer chains → increased hydrophobic interactions → higher phase transition temperature** |
| **Degree of saturation** | **more saturated → less fluid** | **saturated chains are long and straight → close packing → solidification**. **Unsaturated chains have kinks → prevent close packing → more fluid** |
| **Cholesterol** | **buffers fluidity in both directions** | see below |

**F4. Cholesterol has three effects, and the fluidity one is dual.** **Steroids wedged between
phospholipids in animal cell membranes.**

- **Stability:** its **rigid steroid ring interferes with the motions of the hydrocarbon chains**, **enhancing mechanical stability**.
- **Fluidity, dual role:** **at high temperature it restrains phospholipid movement, decreasing fluidity**; **at low temperature it prevents the chains packing closely, decreasing the tendency to freeze, increasing fluidity**. In short it **resists changes in fluidity caused by temperature, acting as a "temperature buffer"**.
- **Permeability:** it **decreases permeability to ions and small polar molecules** by **filling spaces between hydrocarbon chains and plugging transient gaps**.

The dual role is the single most misremembered fact in the chapter, because the direction reverses.
Anchor it on the mechanism: cholesterol always **gets in the way of whatever the phospholipids are
trying to do**. When they want to move, it restrains; when they want to pack, it separates.

**F5. Proteins: integral versus peripheral.**

| | **Integral (intrinsic)** | **Peripheral (extrinsic)** |
|---|---|---|
| Location | **deeply embedded in the hydrophobic interior**; **unilateral** or **transmembrane** | **not embedded, loosely bound to the surface**, often to exposed parts of integral proteins |
| Structure | **amphipathic** — hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions; held by **extensive hydrophobic interactions** with the phospholipid tails | **rich in hydrophilic amino acids** |
| Solubility | **usually insoluble** in aqueous media | **usually soluble** |
| Release | **only by detergents or non-polar solvents** | **easily, by mild change of ionic strength or pH** |

**Six functions of membrane proteins:** **anchorage**, **transport**, **enzymatic activity**, **signal
transduction**, **cell-to-cell recognition** (usually **glycoproteins**), **intercellular joining**.

**F6. Carbohydrates.** **Short branched chains of fewer than 15 sugar units**, bonded to lipids
(**glycolipids**) or proteins (**glycoproteins**), always on the **outer** face. Two roles: they
**maintain orientation** (being highly hydrophilic, they stay in contact with the external aqueous
environment and so cannot rotate inward), and they are the **recognition components** for cell sorting,
antibody-antigen binding, adhesion, and immune identification of foreign cells. **The ABO blood types
are variations in the carbohydrate part of red-cell surface glycoproteins.**

## G. Transport: six processes, one question

Every process answers the same question differently: how do you cross a barrier built to stop you?

| Process | Energy? | Protein? | Direction | What crosses this way |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple diffusion** | **No ATP** | **No** | **down the gradient** | **small and/or hydrophobic** — O₂, CO₂, N₂; small uncharged polar: H₂O, glycerol, ethanol |
| **Osmosis** | **No** | No (aquaporins assist) | **less negative Ψ → more negative Ψ** | water |
| **Facilitated diffusion** | **No ATP** | **Yes — carrier or channel** | **down the gradient** | **large, hydrophilic** — glucose, amino acids, ions |
| **Active transport** | **Yes — ATP** | **Yes, and specifically carrier proteins, never channel** | **against the gradient** | **ions and small hydrophilic molecules** |
| **Endocytosis** | **Yes — ATP** | membrane itself (clathrin in receptor-mediated) | into the cell by **invagination** | **macromolecules** |
| **Exocytosis** | **Yes — ATP** | membrane fusion machinery | out of the cell by **vesicle fusion** | **macromolecules** |

**Definitions to get exactly right:**

- **Simple diffusion:** the **net movement of a substance from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration**. Note the qualification the notes make: ATP is not needed, but it is **wrong to say no energy is involved** — the molecules have **intrinsic kinetic energy**.
- **Osmosis:** the **net movement of freely moving water molecules from a region of less negative water potential to a region of more negative water potential through a selectively permeable membrane**. Say "less negative to more negative", not "high to low concentration".
- **Facilitated diffusion** is called facilitated because **a transport protein enhances the rate**, and **the protein is specific to the substance transported**.

**Carrier versus channel** is a recurring compare question:

| **Carrier protein** | **Channel protein** |
|---|---|
| **possesses a binding site for solute molecules** | **possesses a central hydrophilic pore** |
| **undergoes a conformational change** to transport | **does NOT undergo a conformational change** |

Channels come as **leak channels** (open at all times, e.g. **aquaporins**) and **gated channels**
(open or close to regulate passage, e.g. **voltage-gated Na⁺ channels**).

**Six factors affecting rate of diffusion:** **steepness of the concentration gradient**, **distance**
(thinner membrane, faster), **surface area**, **structure** (number and type of transport proteins),
**size of the diffusing molecule**, and **temperature**.

**The sodium-potassium pump** is the worked example of active transport: a **carrier protein acting as
an antiport**, moving **3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in**, powered by ATP. Transport by carriers is
classified **uniport** (one substance), **symport** (two, same direction) or **antiport** (two, opposite
directions).

> **The 45% trap.** **Endocytosis takes membrane INTO the cell**, so the plasma membrane **loses**
> phospholipid. **Exocytosis fuses vesicle membrane WITH the plasma membrane**, so it **gains**
> phospholipid. Reason it from where the vesicle goes and you cannot get it backwards.

## H. Water potential

**H1. The concept.** **Water potential (Ψ) is the tendency of water to move.** **Pure water has Ψ = 0,
which is the maximum.** **Adding solutes always makes Ψ more negative.** Water moves from **less
negative to more negative** Ψ.

**H2. The two equations, and why they differ.**

- **Plant cell: Ψ_cell = Ψ_s + Ψ_p**
- **Animal cell: Ψ_cell = Ψ_s** — **because an animal cell has no cell wall**, so no pressure potential develops.

**Solute potential (Ψ_s) is always negative** and depends on **the number of solute particles**.
**Pressure potential (Ψ_p) is positive** and **rises as water enters** and pushes the protoplast against
the wall.

**H3. The cell states.**

| | Hypotonic surroundings (water enters) | Isotonic | Hypertonic surroundings (water leaves) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Plant** | **turgid** — protoplast pressed against wall, wall prevents bursting | no net change | **plasmolysed** — protoplast pulls away from the wall (**incipient plasmolysis** is the point where it just begins) |
| **Animal** | **lysed** (bursts) | **normal** | **shrivelled / crenated** |

**The plant/animal difference is entirely the cell wall.** It is why the same external solution kills an
animal cell and merely firms a plant cell, and that comparison is a standard question.

## J. Gaps and errors — read before drilling

**Two syllabus items are missing from these chapters entirely.**

1. **Viruses.** LOs (e) and (f) require virus structure (**including enveloped viruses and
   bacteriophages**) and the `discuss` question of **how viruses challenge the cell theory and concepts
   of what is considered living**. The word "virus" appears exactly **once** in these two chapters,
   inside a scale-figure caption. Source this from **Book 4**. The `discuss` question is a live
   free-response candidate: viruses have genetic material and evolve, but **are not made of cells,
   cannot reproduce independently, and have no metabolism**, so they sit outside all three tenets.
2. **No numerical water-potential calculation, no rate-versus-concentration saturation graph** (the
   standard facilitated-diffusion and active-transport plateau), and **no graticule calibration**
   despite LO (b) naming it. All three are examinable and all three come from the drill file.

**Four internal errors — learn the corrected version.**

1. **Wavelength figures contradict themselves three ways.** One page gives light = 200 nm and electrons
   = 0.5 nm (those are *resolution* values, mislabelled as wavelengths); another gives light =
   400–700 nm; the summary table gives electrons = 0.005 nm. **Trust the summary table**, and keep
   resolution and wavelength as separate quantities.
2. **The cell theory is dated to the "mid-17th century"** on one page and then correctly narrated as
   **1838–1855** on the next. Two centuries out. Use the dates.
3. **Lysosomes in plant cells.** The overview map says "absent in plant cells"; the summary table says
   the plant vacuole "sometimes functions as a lysosome". Safe answer: **lysosomes as distinct
   organelles are characteristic of animal cells, while the plant vacuole can carry out lysosomal
   functions**.
4. **Bulk transport is filed under "occurs against a concentration gradient".** The **ATP requirement is
   correct; the gradient claim is not** — endocytosis and exocytosis move bulk material regardless of
   any concentration gradient. Say **ATP-requiring**, not "against the gradient", for bulk transport.

**One terminology oddity:** the notes use **"endosome"** non-standardly for a fused food vacuole plus
lysosome, then call the same product a **phagocytic vacuole** in the next paragraph. Use **phagocytic
vacuole**, which matches the mark schemes.
