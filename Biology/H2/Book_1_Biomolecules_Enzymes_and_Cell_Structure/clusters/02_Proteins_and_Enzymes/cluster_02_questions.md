### Question 1

**(a)** *State the property the R groups in Fig. 1.1 share, say where such residues sit in a globular
protein in aqueous solution and where a –CH₂–OH residue would sit instead, explain how these amino
acids cause misfolded proteins to aggregate, then name the interaction involved and say what it does
in a triglyceride.*

[INFERRED — extension for the first half and for the closing cross-cluster point; the aggregation half
is verbatim]

1. Every R group in Fig. 1.1 is a **hydrocarbon or hydrocarbon-dominated side chain with no charged
group and no –OH group, so all eight are non-polar and hydrophobic**.

2. Non-polar R groups **cannot form hydrogen bonds with water**, so in a correctly folded globular
protein in aqueous solution they are **buried in the interior of the molecule**, where they form
**hydrophobic interactions with one another** and are shielded from the aqueous surroundings.

3. An R group of **–CH₂–OH is polar and hydrophilic**, because the **–OH group can form hydrogen bonds
with the surrounding water**, so that residue is found **on the exterior surface of the globular
protein**.

Now the verbatim mark scheme, any two of the following three points.

4. Misfolded proteins have their **hydrophobic amino acid R groups / side chains exposed**.

5. There is a **need to shield themselves away from the aqueous ER lumen**.

6. Hence **hydrophobic R groups from different misfolded proteins associate with one another via
hydrophobic interactions**.

1. **Monomer and the bond along a chain.** Collagen is a **protein**: its monomers are **amino acids**,

are **supertwisted about one another**, so tension is resisted by the geometry of the rope itself and
**the twist cannot be pulled out**, whereas parallel cellulose chains resist being pulled apart only
through the bonds between them. **Accept the converse if it is argued properly**: cellulose's strength

**(b)** *Explain how amylase plays its role in the hydrolysis of starch to glucose, naming the ways the
active site lowers the activation energy.*

1. **Amylase binds to starch** by either **the lock-and-key hypothesis: the shape of the substrate is
complementary to the shape of the active site of the enzyme**, or **the induced-fit hypothesis: the
initial binding of substrate to enzyme causes a conformational change in the shape of the enzyme
active site which leads to more effective binding**.

2. There is **formation of an enzyme–substrate complex, which lowers the activation energy**.

3. The active site does this by **serving as a template to position substrate molecules in the correct
orientation for catalysis**; by **inducing stress in / straining critical bonds of the substrate,
allowing the substrate to attain the unstable transition state**; and by **providing a
microenvironment that favours the reaction**, in which covalent bonds are made or broken in a
controlled way.

4. Finally there is **release of glucose from the active site of the amylase, as the shape of glucose
is not complementary to the active site**, and the enzyme is free to bind another substrate molecule.

**(c)** *Which hypothesis cannot account for group specificity, and which better explains the lowering
of activation energy?*

[INFERRED — extension, no official key; every claim is keyed to marking points M10 to M13 of the
15-mark mode-of-action essay]

1. **Lock-and-key cannot account for group specificity.** In that model **the enzyme has a rigid
structure, so only substrates that are exactly complementary are able to bind to the active site**; a
set of **structurally or chemically similar but non-identical substrates** would therefore all fail to
fit.

2. **Induced fit explains group specificity**, because the **active site is not in a precise
complementary conformation to the substrate before binding**, and **a change in the 3D conformation of
the active site is induced on binding, moulding the active site into a precise conformation** around
whichever of the similar substrates has arrived.

3. On lowering the activation energy, **induced fit again gives the better account**. Because the
active site **closes around the substrate**, it can **strain the bonds in the substrate**, **hold the
substrate in the correct orientation** and **provide a microenvironment in which covalent bonds are
made or broken in a controlled way**.

4. Lock-and-key explains **why the right substrate binds**, but it offers **no mechanism by which
binding itself destabilises the substrate**, since in that model nothing moves. **[4]**

eventually be working** and the same maximum rate is reached, just at a higher substrate concentration.

move; but the inhibited molecules are **permanently removed from the working pool**, so the ceiling on
the rate falls.

**at the active site itself**, so it is **in direct competition with the substrate for one site and can
be displaced by raising the substrate concentration**. The non-competitive inhibitor binds **at a site
other than the active site** and **alters the conformation of the active site so that it is no longer
complementary to the substrate**; because the two molecules are **not competing for the same place**,
flooding the enzyme with substrate **does not displace it at all**. **[4]**

**(c)** *Explain the shape of the graph in Fig. 9.2; draw the high-ATP curve and explain it.*

Shape of the original curve, 2 marks.

1. **PFK is an allosteric enzyme with 2 or more subunits held together by weak intermolecular bonds,

2. **The enzyme exists in two conformational states, and binding of substrate at the active site
stabilises the active conformation.**

3. **Binding of a substrate molecule to the first subunit induces a conformational change in the other
subunits such that their affinity for substrate increases.**

Drawing, 1 mark.

4. **A second sigmoidal curve shifted to the right of the original curve, reaching the same maximum PFK
activity (the same plateau) but only at a higher substrate concentration.**

Explanation, 2 marks.

5. **ATP is an allosteric inhibitor which binds to the allosteric site, stabilising PFK in its inactive
conformation, which slows down / inhibits PFK activity.**

6. **The active site of PFK has a lower affinity for its substrate.**

7. **The same maximum rate of reaction can be reached when substrate concentration is high.** **[5]**

**(d)** *Which statements would be true if enzyme Z was inhibited by the end-product instead of enzyme
W?* Key: **C** (1, 3 and 5 only) **[1]**

[INFERRED — extension, no official key beyond the answer letter]

1. **Statement 1 is true.** With **enzyme W no longer inhibited, substrate continues to be
converted**, so **intermediate 1 accumulates and increases**.

2. **Statement 2 is false** for the same reason — it is the direct contradiction of statement 1.

3. **Statement 3 is true**: the **feedback loop still regulates the end-product to the same set
level**, because the end-product still shuts down a step in its own pathway as soon as it rises above
that level; what changes is **where in the pathway the material piles up**, not how much end-product
the cell settles at.

4. **Statement 4 is false**: with the block now at enzyme Z, **intermediate 3 is being made but not
consumed**, so it **accumulates and increases**, not decreases.

5. **Statement 5 is true**: the cell **continues to spend substrate and energy converting material into
intermediates that cannot be turned into end-product**, which is exactly the waste that inhibiting the
**first** enzyme of a pathway is designed to prevent. **[2]**
