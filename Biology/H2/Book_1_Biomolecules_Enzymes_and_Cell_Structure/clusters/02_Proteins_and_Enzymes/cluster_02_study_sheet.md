# PART 1 — PROTEINS

## A. A protein is a sequence that folds into one specific shape

**A1. Composition and the marker element.** Proteins contain **C, H, O, N**, and in some cases **S**.
**Nitrogen is what distinguishes proteins from carbohydrates and lipids** — a standard identification
question. There are **20 different amino acids**, and the sequence is **encoded by DNA**.

**A2. Function comes from two things.** To explain any protein's function you examine **the nature of
the amino acids present, based on their R groups**, and **the 3D conformation of the molecule**.
Diversity of function follows from diversity of structure, which follows from variation in **length and
sequence** of the polypeptide.

**A3. The monomer has four parts on one central carbon:** a **basic amine group (–NH₂)**, an **acidic
carboxyl group (–COOH)**, a **hydrogen atom**, and a **variable R group**. Three properties follow:

- **Zwitterion:** both groups ionise, giving a **net-neutral dipolar ion at pH 7**.
- **Amphoteric, therefore a buffer:** accepts H⁺ onto –COO⁻ when acid is added, releases H⁺ from –NH₃⁺ when alkali is added.
- **R groups fall into three classes:** **non-polar** (hydrophobic, **buried in the interior**), **polar** (hydrophilic, no net charge), **charged** (hydrophilic, acidic or basic).

That third property is the one that does the work. Where an R group ends up in the folded protein is
decided by whether it likes water.

**A4. Peptide bond.** Formed by **condensation** between the **–COOH of one amino acid and the –NH₂ of
the next**, losing water; broken by **hydrolysis**. The chain has an **N terminus** and a **C terminus**,
with **R groups projecting from the backbone**.

## B. Four levels of structure, and the exact bond at each

This table is the single highest-yield item in the cluster. Getting the bond wrong at a level is the
most common way to lose marks in the whole Book.

| Level | What it is | Bonds | Between what, exactly |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | **linear sequence of amino acids** | **peptide** (covalent) | **–NH₂ of one amino acid and –COOH of another** |
| **Secondary** | **coiling or folding into α-helix or β-pleated sheet** | **hydrogen bonds ONLY** | **N–H group of one amino acid and C=O group of another, along the polypeptide backbone**. The notes stress: **these hydrogen bonds DO NOT involve R groups** |
| **Tertiary** | **folding of a single polypeptide chain into an overall specific 3D conformation** | **hydrogen, ionic, hydrophobic interactions, disulfide** | **R groups of different amino acids within the same chain**, often residues far apart in the sequence |
| **Quaternary** | **association of multiple polypeptides into a functional protein** | the **same four** | **R groups of amino acids in different chains** |

**Every higher level is a consequence of the primary structure.** That sentence is worth a mark on its
own and is the reason a single amino-acid substitution can destroy a protein.

**α-helix:** stabilised by **intrachain hydrogen bonds** between the **C=O of residue n and the N–H of
residue n+4**; **3.6 amino acids per turn**; **R groups project outside the helix**, preventing steric
interference. **Proline inserts a kink and disrupts the α-helix.** Example: **keratin**.

**β-pleated sheet:** **extended zigzag, sheet-like conformation**; the same C=O/N–H hydrogen bonds, but
they may be **intrachain or interchain**; **parallel or antiparallel**; residues **usually have small R
groups** because bulky ones cause steric hindrance. Example: **silk fibroin**.

## C. Fibrous versus globular

The classification that matters, because it predicts solubility and role.

| Fibrous | Globular |
|---|---|
| chains **elongated and wound around each other into a rope-like structure** | chains **folded, bent and twisted into a compact spheroidal structure** |
| **repetitive** amino acid sequence | **specific and non-repetitive** sequence |
| **small, specific variety** of amino acids | **wide variety** of amino acids |
| sequence **may vary slightly** between samples; length may vary | sequence **never varies**; length always identical |
| **stable**, from numerous **hydrogen and covalent** bonds | **relatively unstable**, from **non-covalent** bonds |
| **generally insoluble** in water | **more soluble** in water |
| **structural** functions | **metabolic** functions |
| collagen, keratin, myosin, actin, elastin, silk fibroin | enzymes, hormones, antibodies, haemoglobin |

## D. Structure to function, run twice

### D1. Haemoglobin — globular, transport

**Function:** to **transport oxygen in the blood from the lungs to other tissues**, supplying cells with
the O₂ required for aerobic respiration. A **tetramer (α₂β₂) made of two identical αβ dimers**.

Answer this the way the notes lay it out, level by level, each **Structure** paired with its
**Relating structure to function**:

| Level | Structure | Function it produces |
|---|---|---|
| Secondary | **eight α-helices per chain**, hydrogen-bond stabilised | holds the chain in shape |
| Tertiary (a) | **hydrophilic residues at the surface, hydrophobic residues buried in the interior** | **makes haemoglobin soluble in an aqueous medium**, hence a good transport protein |
| Tertiary (b) | folding forms a **hydrophobic cleft lined with hydrophobic residues** | allows the **haem prosthetic group to bind**; **each haem binds 1 O₂**, so **each haemoglobin binds 4 O₂** |
| Haem group | **an iron ion (Fe²⁺) held in a porphyrin ring** | **Fe²⁺ combines reversibly with O₂**, which **enhances release of O₂ in metabolically active tissues** |
| Quaternary | four subunits held **primarily by hydrophobic interactions**, plus ionic and hydrogen bonds | enables **cooperativity** |

**Cooperativity — the mark-bearing chain, in order:**

**First Fe²⁺ binds O₂ → the F helix is pulled closer to the haem group → this creates a strain on the other subunits → their previously obscured haem groups are revealed → the remaining subunits change conformation slightly → their affinity for O₂ increases.**

Hence haemoglobin is an **allosteric protein** and this is **cooperative binding**. Deoxyhaemoglobin is
**taut**; oxyhaemoglobin is **relaxed**.

**Sickle cell** is the primary-structure demonstration: **non-polar valine substitutes charged glutamic
acid at position six of the β chain**, altering the 3D conformation and changing the cell's shape.

### D2. Collagen — fibrous, structural

**Function:** its **strong, insoluble fibres are the major stress-bearing components of connective
tissues** — bone, teeth, cartilage, tendon, blood vessel walls — because it has **great tensile strength**.

- **Primary:** a **repeating tripeptide sequence Glycine–X–Y**, where **X is often proline** and **Y is often hydroxyproline or hydroxylysine**; each chain about **1000 residues**. **Proline residues prevent it from forming an α-helix.**
- **Secondary:** each chain takes a **left-handed collagen helix with about three residues per turn**.
- **Tertiary: absent.** The notes state plainly that **collagen does not have a tertiary structure**. Examiners test this.
- **Quaternary:** **three parallel α-chains wind around each other with a right-handed, rope-like twist** to form **tropocollagen**.

**Tensile-strength argument, in order:**

**Every third residue passes through the crowded centre of the triple helix → only the small R group of glycine can fit → hence the absolute requirement for Gly at every third residue → the three chains pack tightly → high tensile strength.** The **twist cannot be pulled out under tension because the chains are supertwisted about each other**. Add **extensive interchain hydrogen bonds** plus **covalent cross-links**.

**Assembly:** **tropocollagen molecules lie side by side in a staggered arrangement, linked by covalent cross-links → collagen fibril → collagen fibre.**

> **Precision warning.** The mark scheme accepts **"tight triple helix"** and rejects "compact triple
> helix". It accepts **"glycine is the smallest amino acid"** and rejects "one of the smallest". It
> requires **"procollagen"** where the precursor is meant. These are verified rejections, not caution.

## E. Denaturation

**Denaturation is the loss of the specific 3D conformation, while the primary structure survives.** It
disrupts **secondary, tertiary and quaternary** structure.

| Denaturant | Mechanism (what it actually breaks) |
|---|---|
| **Heat** | **increases vibrations of the atoms, disrupting hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and hydrophobic interactions** |
| **pH change** | **changes the charges on acidic and basic R groups, disrupting ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds** |
| **Organic solvents** | **disrupt the hydrophobic interactions that make up the stable core**; the protein turns inside out |
| **Urea / detergents** | **disrupt ionic and hydrogen bonds** |

**What does NOT break: peptide bonds and disulfide bonds.** Both are covalent, and neither heat nor pH
appears in any breakage list. Saying "heat breaks the peptide bonds" is the classic over-claim and
scores zero.

# PART 2 — ENZYMES

## F. Enzymes lower activation energy, and nothing else

**F1. The core claim.** **Activation energy is the minimum energy required to start a reaction.** The
enzyme **lowers the activation energy so more reactant molecules can surmount the barrier**. It does
**not** change the **ΔG between reactants and products**, and it does not make an unfavourable reaction
favourable. Stating that an enzyme "provides energy" is wrong.

**F2. Three named mechanisms of lowering Eₐ:** **orientating substrates in close proximity in the
correct orientation**; **straining critical bonds**; **providing a favourable microenvironment**.

**F3. Six properties:** **effective in small amounts and reusable**; **speeds reactions 10³–10⁸ times**;
**specific**; **heat-denaturable with an optimum temperature**; **pH-sensitive with an optimum pH**;
**regulated by activators and inhibitors**.

---

## G. The active site is built by folding

**G1. Four classes of residue** make the enzyme work: **catalytic** residues do the chemistry;
**binding** residues determine **specificity**; **structural** residues hold the shape;
**non-essential** residues do neither.

**G2. The active site is formed by residues far apart in the primary structure, brought together by
folding.** This is the direct link back to Part 1, and it is why denaturation destroys catalysis: lose
the tertiary structure and the pocket stops existing.

**G3. Cofactors.** **Inorganic metal ions**; **coenzymes** (transient, mostly vitamin-derived);
**prosthetic groups** (permanently bound). Enzyme plus cofactor is a **holoenzyme**; the protein alone
is an **apoenzyme**.

**G4. Mode of action.** **Effective collision → enzyme-substrate complex → product release → enzyme
reused.** The substrate is held **by non-covalent bonds (hydrogen, ionic) between the R groups of
binding residues and the substrate**, and **the product is released because it is no longer
complementary to the active site**.

**G5. Two competing hypotheses, and they explain different things.**

| | Lock-and-key (Fischer, 1894) | Induced fit (Koshland, 1959) |
|---|---|---|
| Active site | **rigid**, exact complementary fit | **flexible**, **moulds around the substrate** |
| Explains | **substrate specificity** | substrate specificity **and group specificity** |

Induced fit is not simply "the better one" — it is the one that accounts for enzymes that accept a
family of related substrates.

---

## H. Rate: measure it properly, then vary one thing

**H1. Measuring.** Rate is **the amount of substrate converted to product per unit time**, measured
either as **product formation** (catalase, volume of O₂) or **substrate disappearance** (amylase, starch
and iodine).

**H2. Only the initial rate is meaningful.** **Initial rate = the initial gradient** of the time-course
curve. The curve **flattens later** as substrate runs out, so a rate taken late understates the enzyme.
Initial rates are then re-plotted against whatever factor you varied.

**H3. The four factors, and the explanation for each region.**

| Factor | Shape | Why it rises | Why it stops rising |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[S]** | rise then **plateau** | more substrate, more effective collisions, more ES complexes per unit time | **active sites become saturated**; **[E] is now the limiting factor** |
| **[E]** | rise then **plateau** | more active sites available | **substrate becomes limiting**. **If substrate is in excess the rise continues linearly** |
| **Temperature** | rise to an **optimum**, then a **drastic fall** | see chain below | see chain below |
| **pH** | peak at an **optimum**, falling either side | intra-molecular bonds intact, active site ideal | see three-residue split below |

**H4. Temperature — the dual effect.** Below the optimum: **increasing temperature increases the kinetic
energy of substrate and enzyme molecules → increases the frequency of effective collisions between
substrates and active sites → increases formation of enzyme-substrate complexes per unit time → more
product per unit time → rate rises.**

Above the optimum, and this is the chain to memorise: **the rate falls despite the increasing frequency
of collisions → thermal agitation disrupts the hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and other non-covalent
interactions that stabilise the 3D conformation → the enzyme and its active site lose their 3D
conformation → the active site is no longer complementary to the substrate → the enzyme is denatured
and loses catalytic function → fewer effective collisions, fewer ES complexes, less product.**

The graph is really **three superimposed curves**: kinetic energy rising, enzyme stability falling, and
the **resultant actual rate**. The optimum is where the two opposing effects cross. Draw it that way.

**Temperature denaturation is irreversible.**

**H5. pH — different mechanism, reversible.** **Changing pH alters the ionic charge on acidic and basic
R groups**, which **disrupts the ionic and hydrogen bonds that maintain the 3D conformation**.

The three-residue split is the highest-value part of this section, because it lets you write three
distinct marks instead of one:

- **Structural residues** affected → **the conformation of the active site is no longer complementary to the substrate** → **no ES complex can form**.
- **Binding residues** affected → **the substrate cannot be held in the correct orientation for catalysis**.
- **Catalytic residues** affected → **the R groups no longer possess the correct ionisation or charge to catalyse the reaction**.

**Unlike heat, the effects of pH are normally reversible within limits** — restoring optimum pH usually
restores the rate. That contrast is itself a question.

## I. Kinetics and inhibition

**I1. The two constants.** **Vmax is the maximum rate at a given enzyme concentration with excess
substrate.** **Km is the substrate concentration at ½Vmax**, and it measures affinity: **a low Km means
a high affinity** for the substrate. Km is a property of the enzyme-substrate pair and does not change
with [E].

**I2. The two inhibition types.** Learn the mechanism and derive the table, never the reverse.

| | **Competitive** | **Non-competitive** |
|---|---|---|
| Inhibitor shape | **resembles the substrate** | **does not resemble the substrate** |
| Binds to | **the active site** | **a site elsewhere on the enzyme** |
| Mechanism | **competes with substrate for the active site**, so fewer active sites are free at any moment | **binding distorts the enzyme's 3D conformation, so the active site is no longer complementary** |
| **Vmax** | **unchanged** | **lowered** |
| **Km** | **increased** | **unchanged** |
| Raising [S] | **reverses the inhibition** | **does not reverse it** |

**Why Vmax is unchanged under competitive inhibition:** enough substrate always out-competes the
inhibitor, so the enzyme still reaches its maximum. **Why Km rises:** you now need more substrate to
reach half of that maximum. **Why non-competitive lowers Vmax:** a fraction of enzyme molecules are
effectively taken out of service no matter how much substrate you add, so the ceiling drops. **Why Km
is unchanged:** the enzymes still working are unaltered, so their affinity is the same.

Derive it that way and the table cannot be scrambled. Memorise the table alone and it will be.

**I3. Allosteric regulation.** Regulators bind an **allosteric site on a multi-subunit enzyme**.
**Activators stabilise the active form and raise affinity; inhibitors stabilise the inactive form and
lower affinity.** The curve is **sigmoidal**. **Cooperativity in haemoglobin uses the same mechanism** —
which is why Part 1 and Part 2 close a loop.

**I4. Reversible versus irreversible.** **Reversible inhibition is non-covalent and temporary;
irreversible inhibition is covalent and permanent.**

**I5. Metabolic pathway control.** **End-product inhibition:** the end product binds an earlier enzyme,
**either altering the active site's conformation to lower affinity, or blocking substrate entry**.
Pathways are advantageous because **no intermediate accumulates**, **steps are small and controlled**,
**each step is a control point**, and **multi-enzyme complexes order the sequence and raise local
substrate concentration**.
