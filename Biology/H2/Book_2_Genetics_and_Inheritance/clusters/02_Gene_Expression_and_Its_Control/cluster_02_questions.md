# Mark Schemes

### Question 1

**(a)** *Why the transcript is continuous while one new DNA strand is not, and what each polymerase needs to start.* **[6]**

**1.** **DNA and RNA polymerases synthesise the new strands in the 5' to 3' direction**, because a polymerase can only add a nucleotide to a **free 3'-OH group**.

**2.** The **template for DNA replication is double-stranded and antiparallel, while the template for mRNA synthesis is single-stranded** — only one of the two DNA strands, the template strand, is transcribed.

**3.** **The direction of unwinding of the DNA template is opposite to the direction of synthesis for the lagging strand**, so the lagging strand has to be built as a series of **Okazaki fragments, 100–200 nucleotides long in eukaryotes, each synthesised away from the replication fork**, while the **leading strand is synthesised continuously towards the fork from a single primer**.

*What each polymerase needs to start.* [INFERRED — extension, no official key]

**4.** **DNA polymerase III cannot initiate synthesis de novo**, so **primase must first join ribonucleotides into an RNA primer of about 10 nucleotides, providing the free 3'-OH end that DNA polymerase can extend**. On the lagging strand **every fragment needs its own primer**, later removed by **DNA polymerase I** using **5' to 3' exonuclease activity** and sealed by **DNA ligase**.

**5.** **RNA polymerase needs no primer.** Once the general transcription factors and RNA polymerase have assembled into the **transcription initiation complex** at the promoter, the polymerase **catalyses the first phosphodiester bond itself**, so **one initiation event serves the whole transcript**.

**6.** In replication **both parental strands are copied at the same fork**, which is what forces one of them to run against the direction of unwinding. In transcription **only the template strand is copied**, so no second strand has to be built in the opposite direction and no discontinuity arises.

**(b)** *How the structure of RNA polymerase allows it to transcribe a tRNA gene.* **[5]**

**1.** **The active site has a specific / complementary 3D conformation, and it binds to the promoter / template strand of the tRNA gene.**

**2.** **The specific 3D conformation of the active site is also complementary to the ribonucleotides and to the DNA template.**

**3.** **Catalytic residues in the active site catalyse the formation of the phosphodiester bond between the 3'-OH group of one nucleotide and the 5' phosphate group of the other.**

**4.** **The active site contains catalytic residues that break the hydrogen bonds between the strands of the DNA molecule to expose the template strand.**

**5.** Reference to the action of enzymes: the polymerase **lowers the activation energy** and **is not used up**, so **many RNA polymerase molecules can transcribe the same gene simultaneously**; it also carries **binding sites for general transcription factors**.

*Book 1 link.* [CROSS-BOOK — Book 1 Cluster 2 §G. INFERRED — extension, no official key]

**6.** The active site exists only because of folding: it is **formed by the R groups of residues that are far apart in the primary structure and are brought together by the tertiary structure**, with **binding residues determining specificity and catalytic residues doing the chemistry**. Denature RNA polymerase and the pocket stops existing, which is why the enzyme is a protein and the template is not.

**Source:** Topical 2017/GE-STQ/Q7a · TJC Prelim 2017 P2 Q4(a) (a, first three marking points verbatim; the primer half and the one-fork argument are an extension); Topical 2018/STQ/Q1d · ACJC Prelim 2018 P2 Q3d (b, verbatim key, with "tRNA molecules" restated as "an RNA molecule from a tRNA gene"; the Book 1 folding link is an extension).
**Archetype:** mechanism explain-chain, ~28% of the Book, with a compare-and-distinguish spine.
**Trap:** direction reversal — a polymerase reading 5' to 3', or the lagging strand described as continuous. Also the quiet one: assuming RNA polymerase needs a primer because DNA polymerase does.
**LOs:** 2(c); reaches back to 2(b) (Cluster 1) and to Core Idea 1 enzymes (Book 1).
**Concepts fused:** (1) 5' to 3' synthesis as a chemical constraint, not a convention; (2) free 3'-OH as the thing every polymerase needs; (3) double-stranded antiparallel template against single-stranded template; (4) fork direction forcing discontinuity; (5) Okazaki fragments and their size; (6) primase, DNA polymerase I and ligase as the price of discontinuity; (7) RNA polymerase self-priming from the transcription initiation complex; (8) active-site complementarity to two different substrates at once; (9) catalytic residues breaking hydrogen bonds; (10) activation energy and enzyme reuse explaining simultaneous transcription; (11) tertiary structure building the active site.

### Question 2

**(a)** *Peptide bonds formed in cytoplasm and in mitochondria.* MCQ key: **B**. **[5]**

**1.** Transcribe first. The DNA triplet code given, **TATTCTTCCACT**, is the template, so the transcript is its **complement with uracil replacing thymine**, read as **5' AUA AGA AGG UGA 3'**. That is four codons, not four amino acids.

**2.** **B is correct.** In the **cytoplasm** the codons read **AUA = isoleucine, AGA = arginine, AGG = arginine, UGA = stop**, giving **three amino acids and therefore two peptide bonds**. In the **mitochondrion**, Table 2.1 makes **AUA = methionine** and **AGA = stop**, so translation stops after the first codon: **one amino acid and therefore no peptide bonds**.

**3.** **A is wrong** on its second figure only. It gets the cytoplasmic count right but claims one peptide bond in the mitochondrion, which would require **two amino acids to be joined** — impossible when **AGA is read as a stop codon and there is no tRNA with an anticodon complementary to a stop codon**.

**4.** **C is wrong** on its first figure. Three peptide bonds would need **four amino acids**, which is what you get if you **count all four codons and forget that UGA terminates in the cytoplasm**.

**5.** **D is wrong on both figures**, and it is the answer of a candidate who **counts codons rather than bonds** and **ignores Table 2.1 altogether**. The general rule: **n amino acids are joined by n − 1 peptide bonds**.

**(b)** *The feature contradicted, and where a substitution is tolerated.* **[5]**

**1.** The feature contradicted is that **the genetic code is (almost) universal — the same code is used by almost all organisms.** Mammalian mitochondria are one of the recognised exceptions, which is why the word in the notes is "almost".

**2.** The position most often tolerated is the **third base of the codon**.

**3.** Reading Fig. 2.1, **most amino acids are encoded by degenerate codons that differ in the 3rd position** — the four codons in any one box, such as CUU, CUC, CUA and CUG for leucine, or GGU, GGC, GGA and GGG for glycine, **differ only at the third base and still specify the same amino acid**.

**4.** So a substitution at the third base often gives a **silent mutation**: the codon changes, the amino acid does not, and the **primary structure, the 3D conformation and the function of the protein are unchanged**.

**5.** A substitution at the **first or second base moves the codon into a different box of Fig. 2.1 and normally changes the amino acid**, or creates a stop codon, so it is far more likely to change the protein. The code is **degenerate but unambiguous**: one amino acid may have several codons, but **every codon codes for just one amino acid**.

**6.** The mechanism behind the third-base tolerance is the **wobble base phenomenon** — **the usual rules of base pairing are violated at the 3rd nucleotide of a codon**, so **a single tRNA can read more than one degenerate codon**. Only **methionine (AUG) and tryptophan (UGG) have a single codon each**, and both sit alone in Fig. 2.1.

**Archetype:** data / figure interpretation feeding an apply-to-novel-context deduction.
**Trap:** counting codons instead of peptide bonds, and transcribing the DNA as though it were already mRNA. Second trap: treating the code as fully universal because the mnemonic says U.
**LOs:** 2(c); reaches back to 2(a) (Cluster 1) for complementary base pairing and the U-for-T substitution.
**Concepts fused:** (1) transcribing a template strand into mRNA with U for T; (2) reading a real 64-codon table; (3) reading frame set from the first codon; (4) stop codons and the absence of a complementary tRNA; (5) n amino acids give n − 1 peptide bonds; (6) universality and its exceptions; (7) degeneracy concentrated at the third base; (8) silent mutations as the consequence; (9) unambiguity as the separate half of the same feature; (10) wobble as the mechanism; (11) AUG and UGG as the two single-codon amino acids.

### Question 3

**(a)** *Which variants can be translated, and which modification explains two proteins.* MCQ key: **C**. Examiner's report: **correct in only 33.12% of scripts, and the most common error was A**. **[5]**

**1.** Work out the legal variants first. The pre-mRNA is **P exon, T intron, Q exon, U intron, R exon, V intron, S exon**. Splicing **removes introns only and never changes the sequential order of the exons**, and **the first exon cannot be spliced out**. Variant 1 (P Q R S), variant 2 (P R S) and variant 4 (P Q S) are all legal; **variant 3 is illegal because exon P has been removed** and **variant 5 is illegal because exon S has been placed before exon R**.

**2.** **C is correct.** Its first column, 1 and 4, are both legal variants, and its second column is a true statement: **a longer poly(A) tail increases the half-life and hence the stability of the mRNA, so more polypeptide is translated from it — differing tail lengths therefore give different expression levels of the two proteins.**

**3.** **B is not eliminated by its first column** — 2 and 4 are both legal variants — so this option has to be killed on its second column, and that is the whole discrimination in the question. It is wrong because **all mature mRNAs must have a 5' cap and a poly(A) tail** before they can be exported and translated; an mRNA lacking either is degraded, not translated into the second protein.

**4.** **A is wrong** because **variant 3 has lost exon P, the first exon, which cannot be spliced out**. Its second column is also loose: alternative splicing changes **which exons are present**, and it is the removal of an **exon**, not of introns, that changes the coding sequence between the two mature mRNAs.

**5.** **D is wrong** on both columns. **Variant 5 puts exon S before exon R**, and splicing cannot reorder exons. Its second column is false as well: **the 5' UTR is not removed during processing** — it is the leader sequence that precedes the start codon in the mature mRNA and is where the small ribosomal subunit begins scanning.

**(b)** *How a substitution inside an intron destroys both proteins.* **[5]**

**1.** The **5' and 3' splice sites are the sequences that flank the ends of an intron**.

**2.** They **allow the recognition and binding of snRNPs to the splice sites**, and the recognition is by **complementary base pairing / hydrogen bond formation between the snRNA and the splice site sequence**.

**3.** This **recruits other proteins, forming the spliceosome**, which then **excises the intron and splices the flanking exons together**.

*The consequence.* [INFERRED — extension, no official key]

**4.** A substitution that destroys a splice site means the **spliceosome cannot bind, the intron is not excised, and it is retained in the mature mRNA**. Because **intron lengths are not multiples of three**, the retained intron **shifts the reading frame of every codon downstream**, and an intron read in frame will normally supply a **premature stop codon**, so **translation terminates early and a truncated, non-functional polypeptide is produced** from every variant that uses that splice site.

**5.** [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 1 non-coding DNA] This is why **"non-coding" does not mean "without function"**. LO 2(h) lists **introns, centromeres, telomeres, promoters, enhancers and silencers** as non-coding DNA with structure and function; an intron's function is **to be recognised and removed**, so the sequence at its ends is under selection even though it codes for no amino acid.

**(c)** *What the cap and the tail each contribute on export, and which the ribosome reads.* **[3]**

**1.** The **5' cap** is a **methylated guanine nucleotide / methylguanosine triphosphate**. It **protects the mRNA from degradation by hydrolytic enzymes such as nucleases**, it **distinguishes mRNA from the other types of RNA molecule**, and it **defines the 5' end of the mRNA, which serves to recruit the small subunit of the ribosome for translation initiation**.

**2.** The **3' poly(A) tail** of about **200 adenine nucleotides**, added by **poly(A) polymerase**, **protects the mRNA from degradation by nucleases, making it a more stable template for translation in the cytoplasm**, and is **required to facilitate export of the mRNA out of the nucleus via the nuclear pores**.

**3.** The one the ribosome itself reads is the **5' cap**: the small subunit **binds the mRNA by recognition of the 5' cap** and then **scans downstream, 5' to 3', in search of the start codon AUG**. The tail is read by the export machinery and by the degradation machinery, never by the ribosome.

**Source:** School Prelim 2025 P1 Q9 (a, verbatim stem, figure, options, key **C** and examiner report including the 33.12% figure and the exon-P and exon-order explanations, converted from a row-select into a written audit) [FIG: q3a_splice_variants.png]; Topical 2017/GE-STQ/Q4c · PJC Prelim 2017 P2 Q3(c) (b, first three marking points verbatim; the intron-retention consequence and the non-coding-DNA link are an extension); NYJC Prelim 2025 P2 Q3c and HCI Book 2 chapter 2.2 pp. 18–19 (c, verbatim functions of the cap and the tail; the "which does the ribosome read" judgement is an extension).
**Archetype:** data / figure interpretation, then mechanism explain-chain, then structure to function.
**Trap:** intron and exon inverted, and the belief that alternative splicing can reorder or drop the first exon. Also splicing placed after export or after translation.
**LOs:** 2(c) and 2(i) iii; reaches back to 2(h) (Cluster 1).
**Concepts fused:** (1) the two rules of alternative splicing — introns only, order preserved; (2) the first exon cannot be removed; (3) every mature mRNA carries both a cap and a tail; (4) poly(A) length as an expression-level control; (5) the 5' UTR survives processing and is where scanning begins; (6) splice sites as snRNP recognition sequences; (7) snRNA base pairing as the recognition mechanism; (8) spliceosome assembly from snRNPs; (9) intron retention producing a frameshift and a premature stop; (10) non-coding DNA with function, straight out of Cluster 1; (11) the three functions of the cap and the two of the tail, kept apart; (12) cap read by the ribosome, tail read by the export and decay machinery.

### Question 4

**(a)** *How every UAC tRNA came to carry arginine, and what it does to the embryo.* **[7]**

**1.** Reference to a **possible mutation in the gene sequence for the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase**.

**2.** **Resulting in an altered 3D conformation of the active site**, **which is now complementary in shape to the amino acid arginine and to the corresponding tRNA with anticodon UAC**.

**3.** [CROSS-BOOK — Book 1 Cluster 2 §B. INFERRED — extension] The mutation changes the **primary structure of the synthetase**, and because **every higher level of protein structure is a consequence of the primary structure**, the **hydrogen, ionic, hydrophobic and disulfide interactions between R groups fold the chain differently**, moving the residues that form the active site. A change at one position in a sequence is enough to remodel a pocket built from residues far apart in that sequence.

**4.** Reference to an **altered primary sequence of polypeptides — all methionine replaced by arginine — so that the folding of polypeptides into their tertiary structure / 3D conformation is affected**.

**5.** Reference to **non-functional proteins being made in the cells**.

**6.** Reference to **possible disruption of metabolic processes in the cell / cells might die easily, so the embryo cannot develop further into a foetus**.

**7.** [INFERRED — extension] The damage is not confined to one protein. The synthetase acts **upstream of every translation event in the cell**, so **every polypeptide is affected simultaneously**. And because **UAC is the anticodon of the initiator tRNA that pairs with the start codon AUG**, **arginine rather than methionine would be placed at the N-terminus of every polypeptide**, so even initiation is corrupted.

**(b)** *The ribosome's only check, and 20 synthetases against 45 tRNAs.* **[6]**

**1.** The only check the ribosome makes is **complementary base pairing between the anticodon of the incoming aminoacyl-tRNA and the codon exposed at the A site**, and that pairing is **held in place by hydrogen bonds**. Nothing in the ribosome inspects the amino acid.

**2.** Fidelity of the genetic code therefore rests entirely on the **aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase**, which **recognises both the specific amino acid and the specific anticodon** before it makes the attachment. This is the point part (a) exploits.

**3.** **Cells produce 20 different synthetase enzymes, one for each of the 20 distinct amino acids**, and each **covalently attaches its amino acid to the 3' CCA stem via an ester linkage, forming an aminoacyl-tRNA, in a reaction requiring the hydrolysis of ATP**.

**4.** For the 45 tRNAs, any one of: the idea of the **induced fit model, with a change in conformation to fit the anticodon of the tRNA more snugly**; or **the active site of an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase is able to bind to slightly different anticodons**; or **there are a few different aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases having an active site complementary to the same amino acid but complementary to different anticodons**.

Marker's note: **degeneracy and the wobble base phenomenon were the common wrong answers** — they explain why several codons specify one amino acid, not why several tRNAs carry it.

**5.** [CROSS-BOOK — Book 1 Cluster 2 §G] Specificity is the standard enzyme argument: **the active site of each aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase must be complementary to the 3D conformation of the specific amino acid and the specific anticodon sequence of the tRNA in order for them to bind**, with **binding residues determining specificity**. Induced fit, rather than lock-and-key, is what allows one active site to accept a family of related anticodons.

**6.** The ATP here is the only ATP in translation. Everything inside the ribosome — **delivery of aminoacyl-tRNA by elongation factors, dissociation of the initiation factors, and translocation** — runs on **GTP**.

**(c)** *A ribosome that cannot translocate.* **[5]** [INFERRED — extension, no official key]

**1.** It would still complete **codon recognition**: the aminoacyl-tRNA whose **anticodon is complementary to the codon already exposed at the A site** binds there by **complementary base pairing held in place by hydrogen bonds**, delivered by **elongation factors with hydrolysis of GTP**.

**2.** It would still complete **one peptide bond**. **Peptidyl transferase in the large ribosomal subunit catalyses the formation of a peptide bond between the carboxyl end of the growing chain held by the P-site tRNA and the amino group of the amino acid in the A site**, and the **ester bond between the P-site tRNA and the chain is broken**, leaving a **deacylated tRNA in the P site and a new peptidyl-tRNA in the A site**.

**3.** It would then fail at **translocation**. The ribosome cannot move **one codon, three nucleotides, in the 5' to 3' direction**, so the **deacylated tRNA cannot move from the P site to the E site and diffuse out**, the **peptidyl-tRNA cannot move from the A site to the P site**, and the **next codon is never exposed at the A site**.

**4.** With the A site permanently occupied by the peptidyl-tRNA, **no further aminoacyl-tRNA can bind and elongation halts after exactly one more peptide bond**. Protein output falls to zero almost immediately, rather than tailing off.

**5.** Termination never happens either. **A stop codon can never reach the A site**, so **no release factor can bind to it**, **no water molecule is added by hydrolysis to free the carboxyl end of the polypeptide from the P-site tRNA**, and **the polypeptide is never released through the exit tunnel of the large subunit**. The ribosome, the mRNA and the tRNAs are all locked up rather than recycled, so the pool of free subunits falls too.

### Question 5

**(a)** *Acetylation raising a rate, methylation closing a switch.* **[8]**

*Start from the packing.* [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 1 DNA packing]

**1.** DNA is wound around a **histone octamer of two each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4, 146 bp per nucleosome core**, giving the **10 nm "beads-on-a-string" nucleosome fibre**; this coils with **histone H1 and linker DNA** into the **30 nm chromatin fibre**, which **non-histone scaffold proteins** fold into **looped domains** and finally the **condensed chromosome**. **The chromatin fibre, when complexed with histones and folded into various levels of compaction, makes the DNA molecule inaccessible to proteins.**

**2.** The chromatin in a nucleus is therefore either **euchromatin, which is diffused, or heterochromatin, which is highly condensed. Genes found in euchromatin are available for transcription, whereas those in heterochromatin are transcriptionally inactive.** **Condensation prevents transcription factors and RNA polymerases from gaining access to the promoter of a specific gene, thus inactivating transcription of that gene.**

*Acetylation.*

**3.** **The N-terminus of each histone protrudes outwards from the nucleosome as a histone tail**, and **histone tails are rich in lysine residues, which are positively charged. They interact strongly with the negatively charged phosphate groups of the DNA backbone and increase the affinity of DNA for the nucleosome surface.**

**4.** **Histone acetyltransferases (HATs) acetylate the lysine residues**, so **their positive charges are neutralised** and **the affinity of the histone complex for DNA is reduced**.

**5.** **Consequently the chromatin becomes more diffused / less compact, and the control regions of genes are exposed to transcription factors and RNA polymerase**, so the **transcription rate increases and hence the quantity of RNA increases**. **Histone deacetylases (HDACs)** run the same chain backwards: lysines **regain their positive charges, affinity increases, the chromatin becomes more compact**, access is prevented and the **rate falls**.

*Methylation.*

**6.** **Methyl groups are added to specific nucleotides after DNA replication, catalysed by DNA methyltransferases. In vertebrate DNA this is restricted to cytosine nucleotides in the sequence 5'–CG–3', known as CpG dinucleotides**, and **CpG dinucleotides are clustered into CpG islands, which are usually found in the promoter regions of many genes.**

**7.** First mechanism: **methylation changes the 3D conformation of DNA and thus prevents binding of transcription factors to the promoter, so transcription initiation is prevented.**

**8.** Second mechanism: **methylated DNA serves as a recognition signal for methyl-CpG-binding proteins (MeCPs), which in turn recruit other proteins such as histone deacetylases. HDACs modify the chromatin in the region of the CpG island so that it becomes more condensed, preventing binding of transcription factors and RNA polymerase, and transcription initiation is prevented.**

**9.** The two outcomes differ in kind, not degree, and the outcome column is where the marks sit. Acetylation and deacetylation give **an increased or decreased transcription rate, hence an increased or decreased quantity of RNA**. Methylation gives **transcription prevented, hence no RNA formed**, which is why it is the mechanism used for **long-term inactivation of genes during cell differentiation**.

**(b)** *Reading the methylation data, and whether the protein differs.* **[6]**

*Describing the pattern.*

**1.** **There is more methylation in the induced pluripotent stem cells than in the normal cells** overall.

**2.** Specific difference: **the induced pluripotent stem cells have some or more methylation at markers 32, 96 and 154, where the normal cells have none.** Accept instead: **at gene marker 154, T1 and T2 have 8 and 10 methylated sites respectively, whereas N1 and N2 have none.**

**3.** Specific difference: **the induced pluripotent stem cells have 3 samples with no methylation, which is fewer than the 6 in the normal cells.** Accept instead: **the difference between the two T samples is larger than the difference between the two N samples — every T1 row carries methylation, while 3 of the T2 rows carry none, whereas each N block has 3 rows with none.**

Reject: **quoting no exact numbers; comparing a T block against a non-corresponding N block; naming gene markers without counting how many are methylated.**

*Whether the protein changes.*

**4.** **No change to the type of protein**, and accept **no change to the amino acid sequence**.

**5.** **Since there is no change to the nucleotide sequence encoding the protein**, and accept **no change to the codon sequence in the mRNA**. Methylation adds a methyl group to a cytosine that is already there; it removes no base and inserts none.

**6.** [INFERRED — extension] What changes is **how much of the protein is made, and in which cells** — the quantity and the spatial pattern, not the identity. That is the whole point of the word "epigenetic": **heritable changes in gene expression without a change in the DNA sequence**, which is also why this level can be reversed by demethylation without any repair of the gene.

**Archetype:** mechanism explain-chain feeding a data / figure interpretation.
**Trap:** histone acetylation direction inverted — saying acetylation condenses chromatin and silences the gene. Second trap: claiming methylation changes the protein, which confuses an epigenetic mark with a mutation.
**LOs:** 2(i) i; reaches back to 2(d) and 2(h) (Cluster 1) for the packing hierarchy and for euchromatin against heterochromatin.
**Concepts fused:** (1) the three levels of condensation, named with their proteins and dimensions; (2) euchromatin and heterochromatin as accessibility states; (3) histone tails and the lysine positive charge; (4) charge to affinity to compaction to access to rate, as five separate links; (5) HATs and HDACs named as enzymes; (6) CpG dinucleotides, CpG islands and promoters; (7) DNA methyltransferases; (8) the conformational mechanism of methylation; (9) the MeCP-to-HDAC recruitment mechanism; (10) the outcome column distinguishing a lowered rate from no RNA at all; (11) reading lollipop methylation data with counted markers and corresponding comparisons; (12) methylation changing quantity, never type of protein.

### Question 6

**(a)** *What each stretch of the promoter region contains, and how it acts.* **[7]**

**1.** Region **A** contains a **negative control element, a silencer**. Deleting it **raises expression from 100 to 320, more than threefold**, so whatever binds there normally **decreases transcription**. Silencers are **distal control elements** bound by **repressors, which are proteins**, and the repressor **destabilises the transcription initiation complex**, giving a **decreased rate of transcription**.

**2.** Region **B** contains **no control element that is active in these cells**: deleting it **leaves expression unchanged at 100**. Note the limit of the inference — the data show nothing bound there in this cell type, not that the sequence is inert everywhere.

**3.** Region **C** contains a **positive control element, an enhancer**. Deleting it **lowers expression from 100 to 15**, so it normally **increases transcription**. Enhancers are bound by **activators, which are proteins**.

**4.** The enhancer mechanism, in order: **activators bind their enhancer**; a **DNA-bending protein loops the DNA, bringing the bound activators close to the promoter**; the activators **interact with mediator proteins, which act as adaptor molecules facilitating interaction between the activator, the general transcription factors and RNA polymerase**; this gives **improved recruitment and a stable transcription initiation complex, correctly positioned on the promoter**, and the **rate of transcription rises above basal**.

**5.** Region **D** contains the **promoter, including the TATA box**. Deleting it **abolishes expression, to less than 1**.

**6.** The promoter mechanism: **TFIID binds the TATA box, roughly 25 to 30 bp upstream of the transcription start site; general transcription factors assemble and mediate the binding of RNA polymerase, forming the transcription initiation complex**, and the promoter also **determines which of the two DNA strands is used as the template**.

**7.** The discriminator between C and D is what remains when each is removed. Losing an enhancer drops the rate to a **basal** level, because the transcription initiation complex can still form. Losing the promoter removes **even the basal rate**, because there is nowhere for the complex to assemble. That is why D goes to almost zero and C only to 15.

**(b)** *A mutation inside an intron of MCM6 switching on LCT.* **[6]**

**1.** Compulsory: the **mutation results in specific DNA sequences within the introns acting as enhancers, which allow activator proteins to bind**.

**2.** Then, any one route. **A DNA-bending protein causes DNA bending, which brings the bound activators closer to the promoter**, and **mediator proteins, RNA polymerase and other transcription factors are also recruited to the promoter.** Or: **the activator recruits a histone acetyltransferase, which adds acetyl groups to the histone tails, loosening the packing of the nucleosomes / chromatin / DNA.** Or: **the activator recruits a chromatin remodelling complex, which alters the structure of the nucleosomes / chromatin around the promoter, rendering it accessible to transcription factors and RNA polymerase.**

**3.** In each case the result is **an increased rate of formation of the transcription initiation complex at the LCT gene, and hence an increased rate of transcription of LCT**.

**4.** [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 1 non-coding DNA] The reason distance and gene identity do not block this: **distal control elements exert their effects when located hundreds or even thousands of bp upstream or downstream of the transcription start site, may also be located within an intron, and function in an orientation-independent fashion.** Cluster 1 lists **introns, promoters, enhancers and silencers together as non-coding DNA**, and here two of those categories occupy the same stretch of sequence.

**5.** [INFERRED — extension] The mutation is **not in the LCT coding sequence**, so **lactase itself is unchanged in amino acid sequence, 3D conformation and catalytic function**. What the mutation changes is **how much lactase is transcribed and for how long in the individual's life** — a change in **temporal specificity**, since the wild-type pattern is expression that declines after infancy.

**6.** [INFERRED — extension] It also explains why the effect is inherited as a property of one chromosome: the enhancer acts on the **LCT promoter on the same DNA molecule**, which is what "upstream on the same chromosome" buys, and looping is what makes that physical proximity possible despite the distance.

**Source:** SAJC Prelim 2023 P1 Q14 (a, verbatim stem and data table, converted from a four-option conclusion into a written deduction; region B's null result and the basal-rate discriminator are extensions) plus HCI Book 2 chapter 2.4 pp. 10–12 verbatim for the mechanisms; ACJC Prelim 2024 P3 Q1(d)(i) (b, verbatim key including the compulsory first marking point and all three alternative routes; the non-coding-DNA link, the unchanged-lactase point and the same-chromosome point are extensions).

**Trap:** assuming an enhancer must be adjacent to the promoter, or must lie outside the gene. Second trap: swapping protein and DNA — calling an enhancer a protein or a repressor a sequence.
**LOs:** 2(i) ii; reaches back to 2(h) (Cluster 1) for non-coding DNA.
**Concepts fused:** (1) reading a deletion series as a control-element map; (2) a deletion that raises expression implies a negative element; (3) a null result and the limit of what it licenses; (4) enhancer and silencer as DNA, activator and repressor as protein; (5) the promoter, its TATA box position and TFIID; (6) the four-step looping and mediator chain; (7) basal rate against increased rate as the promoter–enhancer discriminator; (8) orientation independence and action at thousands of base pairs; (9) enhancers inside introns; (10) the alternative activator routes through HATs and chromatin remodelling complexes; (11) a regulatory mutation leaving the protein sequence untouched; (12) temporal specificity as the thing that changed.

### Question 7

**(a)** *Does half-life set the amount of protein?* **[6]**

**1.** Supporting the claim, at the extreme: the **uncapped mRNA has a half-life of only 1.41 h and produces almost no protein, 0.011, against 16.10 h and 1.000 for the normal cap**. **Stability of mRNA determines the duration for which translation can occur — the more rapidly an mRNA is degraded, the less time is available for it to be translated.**

**2.** Supporting the claim: **modified cap II has both the longest half-life, 27.00 h, and the largest protein yield, 13.094**, so the two do move together across most of the table.

**3.** Against the claim, and this is the decisive row: **modified cap I has a slightly shorter half-life than the normal cap, 15.50 h against 16.10 h, yet produces 4.777 times as much protein**. **The hypothesis that the mRNA with modified cap I is translated more frequently than the mRNA with the normal cap is therefore valid**, because **the half-lives are similar while the protein amounts are not.**

**4.** Against the claim: the relationship is **not proportional**. **Modified cap II has 1.7 times the half-life of modified cap I but nearly 3 times the protein**, so half-life cannot be the only variable.

**5.** The mechanism behind the split: the **5' cap does two separate jobs**. It **protects the mRNA from degradation by hydrolytic enzymes such as nucleases**, which is what sets the half-life, and it **defines the 5' end of the mRNA, which serves to recruit the small subunit of the ribosome for translation initiation**, which is what sets the **frequency** of initiation. A modification can improve one without improving the other.

**6.** Judgement: **stability sets how long translation can occur and initiation sets how often it occurs, and the total protein is the product of the two.** The claim is half right, and the table contains its own counter-example.

**(b)** *A 3' UTR-binding protein against a fall in eukaryotic initiation factor.* **[7]**

**1.** Both changes act at the **translational level**, the level LO 2(i) iv defines as **half-life of RNA and initiation of translation** — the first change works through half-life, the second through initiation.

**2.** The **3' UTR carries stabilising or destabilising sequences containing binding sites for specific proteins that increase or decrease the rate of poly(A) tail shortening.** A stabilising protein bound there **increases the mRNA's stability and half-life and prevents degradative enzymes from digesting the mRNA**, so **the mRNA is translated more frequently and more polypeptide is synthesised** from the same transcript.

**3.** The decay it is holding off: **gradual shortening of the poly(A) tail by an exonuclease begins as soon as the mRNA reaches the cytosol and acts as a timer counting down the mRNA's lifetime.** Once the tail falls below about **25 nucleotides in humans**, either the **5' cap is removed by decapping and the mRNA is rapidly degraded from its 5' end**, or the mRNA **continues to be degraded from the 3' end, through the tail and into the coding sequence**. Some mRNAs are instead **cut internally by an endonuclease recognising sequences in the 3' UTR**.

**4.** The effect is **gene-specific**: only the mRNA carrying that particular 3' UTR sequence is stabilised, so the cell can raise the output of one product **without transcribing the gene again**.

**5.** The **eukaryotic initiation factors** are **involved in scanning the mRNA for the start codon AUG, locating the binding site of the initiator tRNA to the AUG codon, and forming the translation initiation complex at the 5' mRNA region.** Lowering their abundance or activity **lowers the rate of translational initiation**.

**6.** But, and this is the discriminating point, **this has a global effect on overall translational activity, rather than on the rate of translation of specific mRNAs.** Every message in the cell slows down together.

**7.** Comparison and judgement: both changes alter **how much polypeptide is made and neither alters which polypeptide**, but they differ in **scope and in what they are for**. A protein on a 3' UTR, like a **translational repressor binding the 5' or 3' UTR and blocking the attachment of ribosomes or initiation factors**, is how a cell tunes one product against the others. Initiation-factor abundance is how a cell turns its whole protein output up or down at once, which is what a stress response needs.

**Source:** NJC Prelim 2024 P3 Q3(b)(ii) (a, verbatim data table and verbatim key for the modified-cap-I evaluation; the full evaluate-both-ways framing and the two-jobs-of-the-cap mechanism are extensions); CJC Prelim 2023 P2 Q4b(ii) (b, verbatim key for the 3' UTR-binding-protein chain, with the iron-response context stripped out so the mechanism stands alone) plus HCI Book 2 chapter 2.4 pp. 18–20 verbatim for the decay routes, the eIF role and the global-effect sentence.
**Archetype:** data interpretation with an explicit evaluate command, then compare and distinguish.
**Trap:** collapsing the whole translational level into "longer tail, more protein", which the table itself refutes. Second trap: treating an initiation-factor change as gene-specific.
**LOs:** 2(i) iv.
**Concepts fused:** (1) evaluating a claim by finding the row that breaks it; (2) half-life as time available for translation; (3) the cap's protective job separated from its recruitment job; (4) frequency of initiation as a second, independent variable; (5) 3' UTR stabilising and destabilising sequences; (6) proteins acting on the rate of poly(A) shortening; (7) exonuclease shortening as a timer, with the 25-nucleotide threshold; (8) the two decay routes, decapping and 3' to 5'; (9) endonuclease cleavage as a third route; (10) eIFs scanning for AUG and building the initiation complex; (11) global against gene-specific as the discriminator; (12) translational repressors on the UTRs; (13) raising output without a second round of transcription.

### Question 8

**(a)** *Why nine hormones from one polypeptide is neither splicing nor translational control.* MCQ-derived key: **specific chemical signals are required for the formation of unique peptide hormones in different tissues**. **[5]**

**1.** Every tissue makes **one mRNA and one polypeptide of the same 285 amino acids**, so **the primary structure is identical in all four tissues**. Nothing that could differ between tissues has been decided by the time translation finishes.

**2.** It cannot be **alternative splicing**. Alternative splicing **joins exons in different combinations to give different mature mRNAs from the same pre-mRNA, which are translated into different amino acid sequences** — it would have to show up as **different polypeptides**, and the stem states there is only one.

**3.** It cannot be **translational control**. **Half-life of RNA and initiation of translation** change **how much** polypeptide is made, not **which** peptides result from it; here the number and identity of the products differ, not their amount.

**4.** What it does show is **post-translational control**: the polypeptide undergoes **extensive proteolytic cleavage at sites containing basic amino acid residues**, and because **the proteases that recognise these sites are tissue-specific**, the set of hormones produced depends on **which proteases that tissue expresses**.

**5.** Therefore **specific chemical signals are required for the formation of unique peptide hormones in different tissues** — the tissue-specific expression of the proteases is itself the regulated step, one level further up. A further wrong option worth killing: the amino acid sequence **cannot be rearranged**, because cleavage can only cut a chain, never reorder it.

**(b)** *The covalent bonds from gene to functional protein, and which one is reversible.* **[7]**

**1.** **Phosphodiester bonds form between the 3' hydroxyl group of one ribonucleotide and the 5' phosphate group of the next during transcription.** **Catalysed by RNA polymerase, these covalent bonds link ribonucleotides to form the sugar–phosphate backbone of the mRNA**, and they are **essential for mRNA stability and integrity, enabling accurate transfer of genetic information to the ribosome.**

**2.** **Peptide bonds are formed between amino acids during translation in ribosomes via condensation reactions**, **catalysed by peptidyl transferase, the rRNA catalytic activity of the large ribosomal subunit**. The bond is a **covalent linkage between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another, determining the primary structure of the protein.**

**3.** **Disulfide bonds form between cysteine residues, stabilising the tertiary structure and the quaternary structure where applicable**, and they **form in the lumen of the rough ER or the Golgi**. Example: **insulin and antibodies carry inter-chain and intra-chain disulfide bonds that stabilise the functional conformation**, and in insulin a **connecting peptide is removed from the middle only after those bonds have formed**.

**4.** **Glycosylation — covalent attachment of oligosaccharides to amino acid residues**, giving **glycoproteins that require it for stability and activity**, and, in membrane proteins, for **signal recognition and immune activation**.

**5.** **Phosphorylation — covalent addition of phosphate groups to threonine, serine or tyrosine residues by kinases**, which **regulates enzyme activity**.

**6.** **Ubiquitination — covalent attachment of ubiquitin to tag a protein for degradation by the proteasome.** **At least four ubiquitins are required on the substrate before a proteasome can bind to it, and the polyubiquitin chain is recognised by a specific receptor in the proteasome.**

**7.** The judgement. Only **phosphorylation is built to be undone**: it is **reversible, with phosphatases removing the phosphate group**, so it is the switch a cell uses when it will want the protein back. **Proteolytic cleavage and ubiquitin-directed degradation are one-way** — a hydrolysed peptide bond cannot be re-formed by the cell in situ — so switching a protein off by degradation costs the cell everything already spent on making it, and a new round of transcription and translation to reverse.

**8.** [CROSS-BOOK — Book 1 Cluster 2 §E] The same distinction explains denaturation. **Peptide bonds and disulfide bonds are the two covalent bonds that survive heat and pH change**, which is why **denaturation destroys the secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure while leaving the primary structure intact**. A cell therefore cannot dispose of a protein by denaturing it; it needs a **protease or a proteasome** to hydrolyse the peptide bonds.

**Source:** YIJC Prelim 2023 P1 Q13, reset as NYJC Prelim 2023 P1 Q10 with the same four statements reordered (a, verbatim stem facts, the four statements and the key, converted from multiple choice into a written argument); VJC Prelim 2025 P3 Q5b (b, verbatim essay key across all five sections, trimmed of lipidation and prosthetic groups; the reversible-against-one-way judgement and the Book 1 denaturation link are extensions).
**Archetype:** compare and distinguish forced to a judgement, on top of a discuss-scale free-response key.
**Trap:** naming any difference between tissues as alternative splicing, because it is the mechanism most recently learned that produces "different proteins from one gene". Second trap: calling ubiquitination reversible because it is an addition.
**LOs:** 2(i) v and 2(c); reaches back to Book 1 Core Idea 1 for protein structure and denaturation.
**Concepts fused:** (1) identical primary structure ruling out every mechanism upstream of translation; (2) alternative splicing changing which protein, not how many products of one polypeptide; (3) translational control changing amount, not identity; (4) proteolytic cleavage at basic residues; (5) tissue-specific proteases as the regulated variable; (6) cleavage cutting but never reordering; (7) phosphodiester bonds and the polarity of their formation; (8) peptide bonds by condensation, catalysed by an rRNA; (9) disulfide bonds and where in the cell they form; (10) glycosylation and what glycoproteins are for; (11) reversible phosphorylation by kinases and phosphatases; (12) ubiquitin, the four-ubiquitin threshold and the proteasome receptor; (13) reversible against one-way as the basis for a ranking; (14) which bonds survive denaturation, and why that forces the cell to use a protease.

### Question 9

**(a)** *The advantage of five levels rather than one.* **[9]**

**1. Chromatin level.** **Longer-term switching of genes on and off, to restrict the expression of genes.**

**2.** **Allows for specialisation and differentiation of cells** — a liver cell can keep a lens gene silenced permanently rather than repressing it afresh every cell cycle.

**3.** **More efficient and less wasteful of resources, as only the genes required for that cell's functions are expressed.**

**4. Transcriptional level.** **The rate of transcription can be regulated to meet the shorter-term requirements of the cell.**

**5.** **Combinatorial control allows flexibility in the regulation of transcription in response to changes in signals or stimuli, spatially and temporally, when the appropriate combination of specific transcription factors is present.**

**6.** **Coordinate control allows the simultaneous transcription of genes with related functions, or involved in the same metabolic pathway, in the presence of the activators.**

**7. Post-transcriptional level.** **Alternative splicing allows the production of different proteins from a single gene**, and **degradation of unprocessed or incompletely processed mRNAs prevents wastage of resources, such as ribonucleotides, on translating them.**

**8. Translational level.** **The half-life of an mRNA affects how long translation of that mRNA can occur and hence the amount of protein produced, preventing continuous translation and the production of proteins that may not be needed.**

**9. Post-translational level.** **Allows rapid production of functional protein from a stored precursor, by phosphorylation or cleavage, for immediate responsiveness to cell conditions or signals**, or **the ability to convert between active and inactive forms quickly in response to signals**; **allows activation of a protein where it is needed, so the inactive form can be transported and stored safely**; and **allows recycling of amino acids from proteins that are no longer required for cellular functions.**

**(b)** *Two rankings that run opposite ways.* **[5]** [INFERRED — extension, no official key]

**1.** Most economical to least: **chromatin, then transcriptional, then post-transcriptional, then translational, then post-translational.** Blocking at the chromatin level means **transcription is prevented, hence no RNA is formed**, so the cell spends **no ribonucleotides, no ATP on splicing, nothing on export and no amino acids**. Blocking at the post-translational level means the cell has already paid for all of it and then degrades the product.

**2.** Fastest-acting to slowest: **post-translational, then translational, then post-transcriptional, then transcriptional, then chromatin.** Post-translational control acts on **protein that is already present in the cytoplasm**, so **phosphorylation or cleavage produces a functional protein immediately**. A chromatin change produces nothing until transcription, processing, export and translation have all been run.

**3.** The two rankings are inverses of each other because **they measure the same distance along the pipeline from opposite ends**. The earlier a level sits, the more downstream expenditure it prevents, and the more downstream steps still have to run before its effect can be seen.

**4.** The trade explains what each level is used for. **DNA methylation, the most economical and the slowest, is used for long-term inactivation of genes during differentiation.** **Post-translational modification and degradation, the least economical and the fastest, are used for immediate responsiveness to signals.** **Initiation of transcription, which the notes call the most important level, sits in the middle where both properties are acceptable**, which is why it carries most of the day-to-day regulation.

**5.** It also explains why the five are **not alternatives**. A cell with only chromatin-level control could never respond to a signal within a cell cycle. A cell with only post-translational control would have to **synthesise every protein it might ever need, continuously, and then destroy most of them**, and would still be unable to make a cell type. Only spatial and temporal specificity across all five levels lets **cells with exactly the same genome differ morphologically and functionally**.

**Source:** NJC Prelim 2024 P3 Q4(b) (a, verbatim key for the advantages half of the question, all five levels; the prokaryote half of the original is not set here because operon structure is taught in Book 4 and has not been studied); the ranking in (b) is an extension built on the outcome column of the master control table, HCI Book 2 chapter 2.4 p. 3, and on the notes' own statements that methylation is used for long-term inactivation during differentiation and that initiation of transcription is the most important level.
**Archetype:** discuss / evaluate, the standing free-response candidate for LO 2(i).
**Trap:** listing the five levels with no advantage attached to any of them, which is the way this essay most often caps. Second trap: treating the levels as alternatives rather than as a stack, and so having nothing to say when asked why a cell keeps all five.
**LOs:** 2(i), all five sub-levels.
**Concepts fused:** (1) long-term silencing as what chromatin control buys; (2) differentiation as the outcome of that silencing; (3) resource economy stated as a benefit, not assumed; (4) short-term rate control at transcription; (5) combinatorial control and the spatial and temporal specificity it produces; (6) coordinate control of a pathway's genes; (7) alternative splicing as protein diversity from one gene; (8) quality control by degrading incompletely processed mRNA; (9) half-life limiting how long translation runs; (10) stored inactive precursor as the basis of an immediate response; (11) safe transport and storage of an inactive form; (12) amino acid recycling; (13) economy and speed as inverse rankings along one pipeline; (14) matching each level to the job its position suits it for.
