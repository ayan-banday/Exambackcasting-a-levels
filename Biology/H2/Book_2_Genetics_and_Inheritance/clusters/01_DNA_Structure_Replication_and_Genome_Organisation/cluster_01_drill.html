Question 1 [16 marks]

(a) A student argues that no single molecule can be both a chemically stable store of information and a source of variation, because the two demands pull against each other. Decide whether DNA defeats that argument. (answer in terms of the molecule's own chemistry, not how it is packaged, and not its two strands running in opposite directions) [12]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (a)

(a) Decide whether DNA defeats the claim that stability and variation cannot coexist in one molecule. [12]

1. The claim fails, because stability is a property of the backbone and the helix, whereas variation is a property of the base sequence carried on that backbone, so the two demands are met by different parts of the same molecule.

Stability, max 8:

2. The 2' carbon of deoxyribose has an attached hydrogen atom (compulsory point), which makes DNA less susceptible to chemical / enzyme degradation than a sugar carrying an OH there.

3. There is a sugar-phosphate backbone of a DNA chain / strand (compulsory point), with phosphodiester bonds formed between the 5' phosphate and 3' hydroxyl groups of nucleotides.

4. These phosphodiester bonds are covalent bonds that confer strength and stability, and they are formed by condensation, with the loss of one water molecule per bond. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 1 §B3]

5. There is a double helix structure / two polynucleotide strands (compulsory point), and DNA coils into a tight helix, hence DNA is less susceptible to degradation.

6. The phosphate groups project outside the double helix since they are hydrophilic, so only the sugar-phosphate backbone is exposed to outside influences.

7. The nitrogenous bases orientate inwards toward the central axis at almost right angles and are safely tucked inside the double helix.

8. Extensive hydrogen bonds between base pairs confer stability / prevent separation of the double helix.

9. Hydrophobic interactions between the stacked base pairs confer stability / prevent separation of the double helix, the bases being stacked 0.34 nm apart along the central axis. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 1 §C3, a hydrophobic interaction is a force of attraction between non-polar molecules]

Accurate replication and variation, max 5:

10. Complementary base pairing (compulsory point) between A and T and between C and G, which is specific because the 2 nm diameter leaves room for exactly one purine and one pyrimidine, and because each base carries defined hydrogen-bonding side groups.

11. The base sequence of one strand determines the base sequence of its complementary strand, so both strands act as templates for semi-conservative DNA replication.

12. Genetic information is redundant / present more than once in the DNA molecule, so the intact strand is used as a template for repair if the base sequence in one of the two strands is accidentally altered.

13. The base sequence along each DNA strand can be varied in countless ways (compulsory point), and each gene has a unique base sequence, so variation costs the structure nothing, because changing which base sits at a position does not change the backbone that carries it.

QWC: the answer must link the structural features to storage and to replication, not simply describe DNA. The examiner's report on this question records that the commonest failure was candidates who described DNA at length without linking it to the ability to store and replicate information, and that describing DNA replication itself, or transcription, was not required and earned nothing.

(b) Two DNA samples of equal length melt into single strands at different temperatures. Suggest what differs between them, and predict which sample would give up a site for the start of replication more readily. [4]

Answer - part (b)

(b) What differs between two DNA samples of equal length that melt at different temperatures. [4]

1. The samples differ in the proportion of G-C base pairs to A-T base pairs, that is, in GC content.

2. A-T pairs are held by 2 hydrogen bonds and G-C pairs by 3, so separating the A-T pair by heating is easier than separating the G-C pair.

3. The sample with the higher A-T content melts at the lower temperature, because less energy is needed to overcome the smaller number of hydrogen bonds.

4. That sample gives up a replication start site more readily. Each origin of replication (oriR) is a specific sequence of nucleotides which is generally A-T rich, precisely because there are only 2 hydrogen bonds between each A-T base pair, hence it is easier to disrupt the bonds as less energy is needed to overcome them.

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (a)

(a) Why the two daughter strands at one fork cannot be built alike, and every protein that gets around it. [12]

The forced asymmetry, max 4:

1. Fig. 2.1 shows that the two strands are antiparallel: one strand is oriented in the 5' to 3' direction whilst the other is oriented in the 3' to 5' direction, with a free 5' phosphate at one end and a free 3' hydroxyl at the other.

2. DNA polymerases only add dNTPs to the free 3' end of a growing DNA strand, never to the 5' end, so a growing DNA strand can only elongate in the 5' to 3' direction.

3. The two template strands at one fork therefore run in opposite directions relative to the fork, so continuous synthesis of both daughter DNA strands at a replication fork is not possible.

4. So one daughter strand is the leading strand, continuously synthesised as a single polymer, polymerised in the mandatory 5' to 3' manner towards the replication fork from one primer; and the other is the lagging strand, discontinuously synthesised as a series of short fragments known as Okazaki fragments, each polymerised in the mandatory 5' to 3' manner against the overall direction of the replication fork, each needing its own primer, and about 100 to 200 nucleotides long in eukaryotes.

The proteins, in order, name and job, max 8, one mark each:

5. Initiator proteins recognise and bind the A-T rich origin of replication and separate the helix into two strands, forming a replication bubble with a Y-shaped replication fork at each end, replication proceeding bidirectionally.

6. Topoisomerase (in E. coli, DNA gyrase) cleaves a strand of the helix to create a transient single-stranded nick, which relieves the strain / supercoiling / torque ahead of the replication fork by allowing free rotation (swivelling) around the intact strand, and then reseals the broken strand.

7. Helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds holding the two strands of DNA together, which unwinds the DNA double-helix and separates the parental DNA strands at the region of the replication fork, using ATP as an energy source.

8. Single-strand DNA-binding proteins temporarily stabilise the unwound single-stranded portion, prevent the ssDNA from re-annealing to reform the duplex, keep the two parental strands in the appropriate single-stranded condition to act as template, and protect the ssDNA, which is very unstable, from being degraded.

9. Primase joins the ribonucleotides to make the primer, about 10 nucleotides long in eukaryotes, which provides a free 3' OH end that DNA polymerase can extend.

10. DNA polymerase III reads the template and assembles dNTPs by complementary base pairing, catalysing phosphodiester bond formation between the free 3' OH of the growing strand and the 5' phosphate of the incoming dNTP; the incoming dNTP loses a pyrophosphate group, and the energy released from pyrophosphate bond breakage is coupled to bond formation. It also proofreads: on recognising an incorrect base pair it reverses direction by one base pair and excises it using its 3'→5' exonuclease activity.

11. DNA polymerase I removes the RNA primer using 5'→3' exonuclease activity and replaces the ribonucleotides with DNA nucleotides, adding them to the 3' end of the adjacent Okazaki fragment.

12. DNA ligase, a linking enzyme, catalyses the formation of a phosphodiester bond between the 3' end of each new Okazaki fragment and the 5' end of the growing daughter DNA strand.

R: naming an enzyme with no job, or a job with no enzyme. R: "DNA polymerase" alone where the scheme wants III for synthesis and I for primer removal.

(b) A specific inhibitor exists for each protein you have named. Decide which single inhibitor would halt replication soonest after being added to a cell, and which would let replication run furthest before it failed. [4]

Answer - part (b)

(b) Which inhibitor stops replication soonest, and which latest. [INFERRED: extension, no official key] [4]

1. Soonest: the helicase inhibitor, or an inhibitor of the initiator proteins if the cell has not yet fired its origins. Nothing downstream can happen until the parental strands are separated, because every later protein needs single-stranded template, so the fork never opens and no daughter strand of either kind is begun.

2. A topoisomerase inhibitor is nearly as fast, because supercoiling ahead of the fork builds up within a short distance and stalls unwinding, but replication does at least start.

3. Furthest: the DNA ligase inhibitor. Both daughter strands are still synthesised in full, the leading strand is completely unaffected, and even the lagging strand is primed, extended and has its primers replaced; the only defect is that the Okazaki fragments are left with an unsealed nick in the sugar-phosphate backbone.

4. The ranking follows the order of dependency, not the importance of the enzyme: the earlier a protein acts in the chain, the less of the chain can proceed without it. DNA polymerase I sits second last, since its loss leaves RNA primers embedded in the daughter strand but the strand is otherwise complete.

Question 3 [10 marks]

(a) Fig. 3.1 shows the three models that were proposed for DNA replication before the mechanism was settled.

Figure not recovered. Described from the source paper: Fig. 3.1, three rows, each tracing a parent cell through a first and a second replication, with parental strands drawn dark and newly synthesised strands drawn pale. (a) Conservative model: the two parental strands reassociate after acting as templates for new strands, thus restoring the parental double helix. (b) Semiconservative model: the two strands of the parental molecule separate, and each functions as a template for synthesis of a new, complementary strand. (c) Dispersive model: each strand of both daughter molecules contains a mixture of old and newly synthesised DNA.

"…1… percent of the DNA molecules extracted from the F2 generation contains ¹⁵N. When the DNA is extracted from the F3 generation, separated into single strands and centrifuged with caesium chloride, the DNA will separate into …2… and …3… bands in the relative proportion of …4…."

Answer - part (a)

(a) Option audit on the ¹⁵N to ¹⁴N experiment. MCQ key: C. Examiner's report: correct in only 27.65% of scripts, with D the commonest error. [5]

1. C is correct. F1 is 100% ¹⁵N/¹⁴N hybrid, so F2 is 50% ¹⁵N/¹⁴N hybrid and 50% ¹⁴N/¹⁴N. Since every hybrid molecule still contains ¹⁵N, 50% of the F2 molecules contain ¹⁵N, which fixes blank 1.

2. For blanks 2 to 4, the stem says the DNA has been separated into single strands, so the count is of strands, not of double-stranded molecules. At F3 there are 8 molecules, therefore 16 strands, of which 2 are the original ¹⁵N parental strands and 14 are newly made ¹⁴N strands, giving light : heavy = 7 : 1.

3. A is wrong on blank 1: it puts F2 at 100%, which would only be true of F1.

4. D is wrong, and it is the trap. It has blank 1 at 100%, and it reports an intermediate band, which is what would appear if the DNA had been left double-stranded. Candidates who chose it counted molecules when the question said strands.

5. B is wrong for the same band reason as D, once the strands are separated there is no hybrid molecule left to give an intermediate band, and its 3 : 1 ratio is the molecule count at F2, not the strand count at F3.

(b) Using Fig. 3.1, state what each of the other two models predicts at F1 and at F2, and decide which is the earliest generation whose banding pattern could tell all three models apart. [5]

Answer - part (b)

(b) What the other two models predict, and when all three become distinguishable. [INFERRED: extension, no official key] [5]

1. Conservative model: the parental DNA molecule emerges from the replication process intact, i.e. it is conserved, and generates DNA copies consisting of entirely new molecules. At F1 that gives one fully heavy molecule and one fully light molecule, so two bands and no intermediate. At F2, one heavy and three light.

2. Semi-conservative model: each of the two daughter DNA molecules consists of one parental DNA strand and one newly-synthesised daughter DNA strand. At F1, all molecules are hybrid, so a single intermediate band. At F2, 50% hybrid and 50% light.

3. Dispersive model: all four strands of DNA following replication have a mixture of old and new DNA. At F1 that also gives a single band at the intermediate position, indistinguishable from semi-conservative. At F2 it gives a single band that has moved further towards light, because every molecule is equally diluted.

4. F1 separates conservative from the other two, because only conservative predicts two bands, one at each extreme, with nothing in between.

5. F2 is the earliest generation at which all three can be told apart, because semi-conservative predicts two bands, hybrid and light, whereas dispersive predicts one band only, sitting between them. The point of the experiment is that one round of replication is not enough.

(a) Fig. 4.1 shows one level of DNA condensation in a eukaryotic nucleus.

Describe how a 2 nm DNA double helix is brought to the width of a metaphase chromatid, and account for what holds the particle in Fig. 4.1 together. [10]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (a)

(a) From 2 nm helix to metaphase chromatid, and what holds the nucleosome together. [10]

First level, max 4:

1. Negatively charged DNA is coiled around an octamer of histone proteins, two each of histones H2A, H2B, H3 and H4, forming a nucleosome core.

2. Histones are small proteins with a high concentration of positively-charged residues, e.g. lysine and arginine, which form ionic bonds with the negatively-charged sugar-phosphate backbone of DNA. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 1: the octamer is a quaternary structure, eight polypeptide subunits, and the attraction is an ionic bond between oppositely charged R groups]

3. 146 base pairs of DNA is bound to that core.

4. Multiple nucleosomes, joined by linker DNA, are packed together to produce the 10-nm chromatin fibre, also known as a nucleosome fibre, which gives the chromatin a "beads-on-a-string" look.

Second and third levels, max 4:

5. DNA is further folded or coiled to produce the 30-nm chromatin fibre, known as solenoid, and histone H1 and linker DNA are involved in this coiling.

6. Non-histone proteins known as scaffold proteins are involved in condensing the 30-nm chromatin fibre to form looped domains of 300 nm.

7. In mitotic and meiotic chromosomes, the looped domains themselves coil and fold, further compacting all the chromatin to produce the characteristic metaphase chromosome, and the width of one chromatid is 700 nm.

Why, max 2:

8. The purpose is to organise and pack the giant DNA molecules of eukaryotic chromosomes into structures that will facilitate their segregation onto daughter nuclei, so that DNA molecules of different chromosomes will not be entangled and as a consequence, break during separation at anaphase.

9. Packing also regulates gene accessibility: euchromatin is diffused and available for transcription whereas heterochromatin is highly condensed and transcriptionally inactive, because condensation prevents transcription factors and RNA polymerase from reaching the promoter.

R: stopping at "beads-on-a-string" without the higher levels. R: giving the levels with no protein named at each.

Answer - part (b)

(b) Effect of stripping the positive charge from the histone tails. [INFERRED: extension, no official key, and the mechanism itself is LO 2(i), Book 2 chapter 2.4] [4]

1. The ionic bonds between the positively-charged lysine and arginine residues and the negatively-charged sugar-phosphate backbone are lost, so the affinity of the histones for DNA falls.

Explain how the end replication problem arises, and account for the fact that a bacterium replicating its chromosome never meets it. [8]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (a)

(a) How the end replication problem arises, and why a bacterium escapes it. [8]

1. The end replication problem occurs in linear chromosomes, i.e. in eukaryotes only, because DNA polymerase is incapable of completely replicating all the way to the ends of a linear chromosome.

2. On the lagging strand the daughter strand is built as Okazaki fragments, each begun from its own RNA primer.

3. DNA polymerase only adds nucleotides to an existing free 3'-OH end, with elongation extending in a 5' to 3' direction.

4. So when the final RNA primer of the terminal Okazaki fragment at the end of the lagging strand is removed, the cell is unable to fill the resulting gap, because there is no upstream strand onto which DNA polymerase can build and therefore no free 3'-OH available.

5. A small section at the extreme 3' end of the parental strand does not undergo DNA replication, so the parental strand is left with a 3' single-stranded overhang.

6. Hence the daughter DNA strand resulting from lagging strand synthesis is shortened with each round of replication, and is shorter than its parental template strand.

7. A bacterium escapes it because the prokaryotic chromosome is a small circular DNA molecule.

8. A circular molecule has no ends, so there is no terminal primer whose removal leaves an unfillable gap, the last primer removed always has an upstream fragment supplying a 3'-OH, because the strand closes on itself.

(b) A student says the problem costs the chromosome sequence at one end only. Decide whether that is right. (answer in terms of what each daughter strand is doing at each of the two ends) [4]

---

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (b)

(b) Whether the loss falls at one end of the chromosome only. [INFERRED: extension, no official key] [4]

1. The student is wrong. Sequence is lost from both ends of the chromosome.

2. As Fig. 5.1 shows, each daughter strand is leading at one end of the chromosome and lagging at the other, because the two forks travel away from the origin in opposite directions along antiparallel templates.

3. So at each end of the chromosome one of the two new strands is being completed by lagging strand synthesis, and it is that strand which cannot be finished.

4. The correct statement is therefore that each daughter molecule is shortened at one end, and the two daughter molecules are shortened at opposite ends, so across the pair, both ends of the original chromosome lose sequence. The problem is a property of lagging strand synthesis, not of a particular end.

(c) Account for 85-90% against 1-5%, and judge efficiency. [INFERRED: extension, no official key] [4]

1. Answer - part (a)

(a) Classify every DNA region in Fig. 8.1, and account for the two senses of "non-coding". [8]

1. Non-coding DNA refers to portions that do not encode protein or RNA. On that definition only the exons in Fig. 8.1 are coding; every other region drawn on the DNA is non-coding.

2. Exons, coding. They are the discontinuous coding sequences that are transcribed and retained in the mature mRNA, and they carry the coding segment from the start codon to the stop codon.

3. Introns, non-coding. They are transcribed into the primary RNA transcript, but they are removed during RNA processing and are not present in the mature mRNA, so they are never translated. Fig. 8.1 shows them leaving as intron RNA.

4. Promoter, non-coding. It is a series of DNA sequences located upstream of the transcriptional start site where RNA polymerase and transcription factors bind to initiate transcription, and it determines which of the two strands is used as the template.

5. Proximal control elements and the enhancer, non-coding. They are segments of DNA involved in regulating the initiation and rate of transcription by binding particular proteins.

6. The poly-A signal sequence and the transcription termination region, non-coding. They are transcribed, but they are signals: the transcribed terminator codes for the polyadenylation signal, at which the transcript is cleaved.

7. The 5' UTR and 3' UTR shown on the mRNA correspond to DNA that is transcribed but not translated, the 5' UTR starts at the +1 position and ends one nucleotide before the start codon, and the 3' UTR starts after the stop codon.

8. So "non-coding" is covering two different situations, and a good answer separates them. Some regions are never transcribed at all, the promoter, the proximal control elements and the enhancer, which are read by proteins as binding sites, not copied. Others are transcribed and then discarded or left untranslated, the introns, the UTRs and the poly-A signal. The exon is the only category defined by being kept and read.

R: "introns are non-coding because they are not transcribed", they are transcribed. R: counting the UTRs as coding because they lie inside exons.

(b) How a distal control element acts over thousands of base pairs. [6]

1. The region is an enhancer, a group of distal control elements. Distal control elements are effective hundreds or thousands of base pairs upstream or downstream of the gene, may lie within an intron, and function independently of their orientation.

Account for the consequences of the single difference boxed in Fig. 9.1, as far as why a replication primer is built out of the less stable of the two nucleic acids and then destroyed again. [8]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (a)

(a) The consequences of the single difference in Fig. 9.1. [8]

1. At the 2' carbon of deoxyribose, the hydroxyl group (-OH) is replaced by a hydrogen atom (H). That is the whole difference, and it distinguishes RNA from DNA.

2. The partial negative charge of the hydroxyl group in ribose repels the negative charge of the phosphate, which prevents the RNA chain from coiling in as tight a helix as it does in DNA.

3. Hence RNA is more susceptible to chemical and enzyme degradation, whereas DNA, coiling into a tight helix with only the backbone exposed, is less susceptible to degradation. This is why the long-term store of genetic information is built from the deoxy sugar.

4. Both nucleotides are nevertheless interchangeable at the polymerase: both have a phosphate group bound to carbon-5 of the pentose sugar, which allows formation of phosphodiester bonds to form polynucleotides; both have an OH group bound to carbon-3; and both have nitrogenous bases bound to carbon-1, allowing complementary base pairing.

5. A primer is needed at all because none of the DNA polymerases can initiate the synthesis of a DNA strand on its own, DNA synthesis cannot occur de novo. The shape of the active site of DNA polymerase is complementary to the 5' phosphate group of the incoming nucleotide and the 3' -OH group of the last nucleotide of the growing strand, so it can only add a deoxyribonucleotide to a free 3' -OH end of an existing strand. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 1: active site complementarity]

6. Primase joins ribonucleotides to make the primer, and the RNA primer provides a free 3' OH end that DNA polymerase can extend. Primase can do this because, unlike DNA polymerase, it does not require a pre-existing 3' end.

7. Building the primer from RNA marks it as chemically distinguishable from the DNA around it, so it can be found and removed later, and its greater instability makes it a poor thing to leave in a molecule whose whole job is stable storage.

8. It is removed for exactly that reason: DNA polymerase I uses 5'→3' exonuclease activity to remove the RNA primer and replace the ribonucleotides with DNA nucleotides, and DNA ligase then seals the remaining nick. A daughter strand that kept its RNA primers would carry ~10-nucleotide stretches of the less stable nucleic acid at every Okazaki junction.

(b) Dideoxycytosine triphosphate (ddCTP) is identical to deoxycytosine triphosphate except that it carries H at the 3' position as well as at the 2' position. Answer - part (b)

(b) Predict the effect of ddCTP, and name the inhibition. [6]

1. ddCTP competes with dCTP for the active site of DNA polymerase, because the two are identical apart from the 3' position and so are similar enough in shape to bind the same active site.

2. This is competitive inhibition: the inhibitor is a substrate analogue that binds the active site rather than an allosteric site, so the effect can be reduced by raising the concentration of dCTP. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 1 enzymes]

3. Once bound, ddCTP is incorporated into the growing strand, since it forms a phospho(di)ester bond with the 3' hydroxyl group of the daughter strand by complementary base pairing with a G on the template.

4. But ddCTP lacks a 3' hydroxyl group on the deoxyribose / pentose sugar.

5. Therefore the incorporated ddCTP cannot form a phosphodiester bond with the next incoming dNTP, because there is no free 3' -OH for the polymerase to extend.

6. DNA replication is terminated prematurely at that point, and the strand stops wherever a ddCTP was taken up. The result is a population of daughter strands of differing lengths, each ending in C.
