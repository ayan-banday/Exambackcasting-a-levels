# Mark Schemes

### Question 1

**(a)** *Decide whether DNA defeats the claim that stability and variation cannot coexist in one
molecule.* **[12]**

**1.** The claim fails, because **stability is a property of the backbone and the helix, whereas
variation is a property of the base sequence carried on that backbone**, so the two demands are met by
different parts of the same molecule.

*Stability, max 8:*

**2.** **The 2' carbon of deoxyribose has an attached hydrogen atom** (compulsory point), which makes
DNA **less susceptible to chemical / enzyme degradation** than a sugar carrying an OH there.

**3.** There is a **sugar-phosphate backbone of a DNA chain / strand** (compulsory point), with
**phosphodiester bonds formed between the 5' phosphate and 3' hydroxyl groups of nucleotides**.

**4.** These **phosphodiester bonds are covalent bonds that confer strength and stability**, and they
are formed by **condensation, with the loss of one water molecule per bond**. [CROSS-BOOK — Book 1 §B3]

**5.** There is a **double helix structure / two polynucleotide strands** (compulsory point), and
**DNA coils into a tight helix, hence DNA is less susceptible to degradation**.

**6.** The **phosphate groups project outside the double helix since they are hydrophilic**, so **only
the sugar-phosphate backbone is exposed to outside influences**.

**7.** The **nitrogenous bases orientate inwards toward the central axis at almost right angles** and
are **safely tucked inside the double helix**.

**8.** **Extensive hydrogen bonds between base pairs confer stability / prevent separation of the
double helix.**

**9.** **Hydrophobic interactions between the stacked base pairs confer stability / prevent separation
of the double helix**, the bases being **stacked 0.34 nm apart along the central axis**. [CROSS-BOOK —
Book 1 §C3, a hydrophobic interaction is a force of attraction between non-polar molecules]

*Accurate replication and variation, max 5:*

**10.** **Complementary base pairing** (compulsory point) **between A and T and between C and G**,
which is **specific** because the **2 nm diameter leaves room for exactly one purine and one
pyrimidine**, and because each base carries **defined hydrogen-bonding side groups**.

**11.** The **base sequence of one strand determines the base sequence of its complementary strand**,
so **both strands act as templates for semi-conservative DNA replication**.

**12.** **Genetic information is redundant / present more than once in the DNA molecule**, so the
**intact strand is used as a template for repair if the base sequence in one of the two strands is
accidentally altered**.

**13.** The **base sequence along each DNA strand can be varied in countless ways** (compulsory
point), and **each gene has a unique base sequence** — so variation costs the structure nothing,
because **changing which base sits at a position does not change the backbone that carries it**.

**QWC:** the answer must link the structural features to storage and to replication, not simply
describe DNA. The examiner's report on this question records that the commonest failure was candidates
who **described DNA at length without linking it to the ability to store and replicate information**,
and that describing **DNA replication itself, or transcription, was not required and earned nothing**.

**(b)** *What differs between two DNA samples of equal length that melt at different temperatures.*
**[4]**

**1.** The samples differ in **the proportion of G–C base pairs to A–T base pairs**, that is, in **GC
content**.

**2.** **A–T pairs are held by 2 hydrogen bonds and G–C pairs by 3**, so **separating the A–T pair by
heating is easier than separating the G–C pair**.

**3.** The sample with the **higher A–T content melts at the lower temperature**, because **less energy
is needed to overcome the smaller number of hydrogen bonds**.

**4.** That sample gives up a replication start site more readily. **Each origin of replication (oriR)
is a specific sequence of nucleotides which is generally A-T rich**, precisely because **there are only
2 hydrogen bonds between each A-T base pair, hence it is easier to disrupt the bonds as less energy is
needed to overcome them**.

**Source:** School Prelim 2024 P3 Q4a and HCI Prelim 2024 P3 Q4(a) (a — the marking points, the
compulsory-point flags, the QWC line and the examiner's comment are verbatim from that 15-mark key,
compressed to 12 and re-fronted as a claim to be defeated, which is the extension); TPE 2025 P3 Q4a
[15] and DHS Prelim 2018 P2 Q5a [3] (a — the helix, stacking and steric points, verbatim); MI Prelim
2023 P2 Q3a [2] (a — the three properties DNA must have, verbatim); EJC Prelim 2018 P2 Q4a(ii) [1] and
CJC Prelim 2023 P2 Q3a [1] (a — the redundancy and repair point, verbatim); Topical 2007 STQ Q2a(ii),
from MJC Prelim P2 Q5(a)(ii) [2] (a — the steric and hydrogen-bonding reasons for specific pairing,
verbatim). Part (b) is built from the notes' own melting-temperature and oriR statements, 2.1 pp. 16
and 27; **[INFERRED — extension, no official key]** for the two-sample framing.
**Archetype:** structure → function, run as an evaluate-level claim to be tested. **Trap:** listing
structural features without linking each to storage, replication or variation. A feature with no
consequence attached scores nothing here.
**Concepts fused:** (1) the 2' hydrogen of deoxyribose as a stability feature; (2) the phosphodiester
backbone as a covalent bond built by condensation; (3) hydrophilic phosphates outward and hydrophobic
bases inward as a protection argument; (4) base stacking as a hydrophobic interaction; (5) hydrogen
bonding as collectively strong and individually weak; (6) steric restriction and the 2 nm diameter;
(7) hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor geometry as the second reason for specificity; (8) one strand
determining the other, and what that buys for replication; (9) informational redundancy and
template-directed repair; (10) sequence variability as the source of variation and why it does not
cost stability; (11) GC content and melting temperature; (12) A-T rich origins of replication as the
same fact used in reverse.

### Question 2

**(a)** *Why the two daughter strands at one fork cannot be built alike, and every protein that gets
around it.* **[12]**

*The forced asymmetry, max 4:*

**1.** Fig. 2.1 shows that the two strands are **antiparallel**: **one strand is oriented in the 5' to
3' direction whilst the other is oriented in the 3' to 5' direction**, with a **free 5' phosphate at
one end and a free 3' hydroxyl at the other**.

**2.** **DNA polymerases only add dNTPs to the free 3' end of a growing DNA strand, never to the 5'
end**, so **a growing DNA strand can only elongate in the 5' to 3' direction**.

**3.** The two template strands at one fork therefore run in opposite directions relative to the fork,
so **continuous synthesis of both daughter DNA strands at a replication fork is not possible**.

**4.** So one daughter strand is the **leading strand, continuously synthesised as a single polymer,
polymerised in the mandatory 5' to 3' manner towards the replication fork** from **one primer**; and
the other is the **lagging strand, discontinuously synthesised as a series of short fragments known as
Okazaki fragments**, each **polymerised in the mandatory 5' to 3' manner against the overall direction
of the replication fork**, each needing **its own primer**, and **about 100 to 200 nucleotides long in
eukaryotes**.

*The proteins, in order, name and job — max 8, one mark each:*

**5.** **Initiator proteins** recognise and bind the **A-T rich origin of replication** and separate the
helix into two strands, forming a **replication bubble** with a **Y-shaped replication fork at each
end**, replication proceeding **bidirectionally**.

**6.** **Topoisomerase** (in *E. coli*, **DNA gyrase**) **cleaves a strand of the helix to create a
transient single-stranded nick**, which **relieves the strain / supercoiling / torque ahead of the
replication fork by allowing free rotation (swivelling) around the intact strand**, and then
**reseals the broken strand**.

**7.** **Helicase** **breaks the hydrogen bonds holding the two strands of DNA together**, which
**unwinds the DNA double-helix and separates the parental DNA strands at the region of the replication
fork**, **using ATP as an energy source**.

**8.** **Single-strand DNA-binding proteins** **temporarily stabilise the unwound single-stranded
portion**, **prevent the ssDNA from re-annealing to reform the duplex**, **keep the two parental
strands in the appropriate single-stranded condition to act as template**, and **protect the ssDNA,
which is very unstable, from being degraded**.

**9.** **Primase** **joins the ribonucleotides to make the primer**, about **10 nucleotides long in
eukaryotes**, which **provides a free 3' OH end that DNA polymerase can extend**.

**10.** **DNA polymerase III** reads the template and assembles **dNTPs by complementary base
pairing**, catalysing **phosphodiester bond formation between the free 3' OH of the growing strand and
the 5' phosphate of the incoming dNTP**; the **incoming dNTP loses a pyrophosphate group, and the
energy released from pyrophosphate bond breakage is coupled to bond formation**. It also **proofreads:
on recognising an incorrect base pair it reverses direction by one base pair and excises it using its
3'→5' exonuclease activity**.

**11.** **DNA polymerase I** **removes the RNA primer using 5'→3' exonuclease activity and replaces the
ribonucleotides with DNA nucleotides**, adding them to the **3' end of the adjacent Okazaki fragment**.

**12.** **DNA ligase, a linking enzyme, catalyses the formation of a phosphodiester bond between the 3'
end of each new Okazaki fragment and the 5' end of the growing daughter DNA strand.**

R: naming an enzyme with no job, or a job with no enzyme. R: "DNA polymerase" alone where the scheme
wants **III** for synthesis and **I** for primer removal.

**(b)** *Which inhibitor stops replication soonest, and which latest.* [INFERRED — extension, no
official key] **[4]**

**1.** Soonest: **the helicase inhibitor**, or an inhibitor of the **initiator proteins** if the cell
has not yet fired its origins. **Nothing downstream can happen until the parental strands are
separated**, because **every later protein needs single-stranded template**, so the fork **never
opens** and **no daughter strand of either kind is begun**.

**2.** A **topoisomerase inhibitor is nearly as fast**, because **supercoiling ahead of the fork builds
up within a short distance** and stalls unwinding, but replication does at least start.

**3.** Furthest: **the DNA ligase inhibitor**. **Both daughter strands are still synthesised in full**,
the **leading strand is completely unaffected**, and even the lagging strand is **primed, extended and
has its primers replaced**; the only defect is that the **Okazaki fragments are left with an unsealed
nick in the sugar-phosphate backbone**.

**4.** The ranking follows the **order of dependency, not the importance of the enzyme**: the earlier a
protein acts in the chain, the less of the chain can proceed without it. **DNA polymerase I sits second
last**, since its loss leaves **RNA primers embedded in the daughter strand** but the strand is
otherwise complete.

**Source:** JPJC Prelim 2025 P3 Q4a [13] (a — the enzyme roster with the job attached to each name,
verbatim from the key); CJC Prelim 2017 P2 Q3b(i) and Q3b(ii) (a — the four axes of asymmetry and the
two reasons for it, verbatim); Topical 2007 MCQ Q13, from NYJC Prelim P1 Q6, and Topical 2007 MCQ Q18,
from VJC Prelim P1 Q8 (a — the reason for the difference between the strands, key D); RVHS Prelim 2023
P2 Q4a(ii) [3] (a — lagging strand synthesis, verbatim); ACJC Prelim 2024 P2 Q4(a)(i) [1] (a — DNA
polymerase I removes the primers, verbatim including the reject on DNA polymerase III); Topical 2017
DNA-STQ Q2b, from DHS Prelim P2 Q3(b) [2] (a — two enzymes and their functions). The figure is the
school's own Fig. 35, 2.1 p. 34. Part (b) is an extension with no key.
**Archetype:** mechanism explain-chain, the signature Book 2 archetype, closed with a ranking.
**Trap:** direction reversal. Reversing 5'→3', or saying the lagging strand is made continuously, is
the commonest way to lose a mark you otherwise understood. Second trap: writing the roster as a list of
names, which scores nothing without the job.
**Concepts fused:** (1) antiparallel geometry read off a figure; (2) Limitation 2 of DNA polymerases as
the premise of the whole answer; (3) the impossibility of continuous synthesis on both strands as a
derived conclusion rather than a memorised fact; (4) leading and lagging strand definitions with
direction and primer number; (5) Okazaki fragment size in eukaryotes; (6) origin firing and
bidirectional forks; (7) topoisomerase and the mechanical problem of supercoiling; (8) helicase and
the hydrogen bonds it breaks; (9) single-strand binding proteins and the three separate jobs they do;
(10) primase and the free 3' OH; (11) DNA polymerase III, its bond, its energy source and its
proofreading; (12) DNA polymerase I and the exonuclease activity that distinguishes it from III;
(13) ligase and the bond it seals; (14) ordering a pathway by dependency in order to rank inhibitors.

### Question 3

**(a)** *Option audit on the ¹⁵N to ¹⁴N experiment.* MCQ key: **C**. Examiner's report: correct in only
**27.65%** of scripts, with **D** the commonest error. **[5]**

**1.** **C is correct.** **F1 is 100% ¹⁵N/¹⁴N hybrid**, so **F2 is 50% ¹⁵N/¹⁴N hybrid and 50%
¹⁴N/¹⁴N**. Since **every hybrid molecule still contains ¹⁵N**, **50% of the F2 molecules contain
¹⁵N**, which fixes blank 1.

**2.** For blanks 2 to 4, the stem says the **DNA has been separated into single strands**, so the
count is **of strands, not of double-stranded molecules**. At F3 there are **8 molecules, therefore 16
strands**, of which **2 are the original ¹⁵N parental strands and 14 are newly made ¹⁴N strands**,
giving **light : heavy = 7 : 1**.

**3.** **A is wrong** on blank 1: it puts F2 at **100%**, which would only be true of F1.

**4.** **D is wrong**, and it is the trap. It has blank 1 at **100%**, and it reports an
**intermediate** band, which is what would appear if the DNA had been left **double-stranded**.
Candidates who chose it **counted molecules when the question said strands**.

**5.** **B is wrong** for the same band reason as D — **once the strands are separated there is no
hybrid molecule left to give an intermediate band** — and its **3 : 1** ratio is the molecule count at
F2, not the strand count at F3.

**(b)** *What the other two models predict, and when all three become distinguishable.* [INFERRED —
extension, no official key] **[5]**

**1.** **Conservative model:** **the parental DNA molecule emerges from the replication process
intact, i.e. it is conserved, and generates DNA copies consisting of entirely new molecules**. At F1
that gives **one fully heavy molecule and one fully light molecule, so two bands and no intermediate**.
At F2, **one heavy and three light**.

**2.** **Semi-conservative model:** **each of the two daughter DNA molecules consists of one parental

### Question 5

**(a)** *How the end replication problem arises, and why a bacterium escapes it.* **[8]**

**1.** **The end replication problem occurs in linear chromosomes, i.e. in eukaryotes only**, because
**DNA polymerase is incapable of completely replicating all the way to the ends of a linear
chromosome**.

**2.** On the lagging strand the daughter strand is built as **Okazaki fragments, each begun from its
own RNA primer**.

**3.** **DNA polymerase only adds nucleotides to an existing free 3'-OH end**, with **elongation
extending in a 5' to 3' direction**.

**4.** So when the **final RNA primer of the terminal Okazaki fragment at the end of the lagging strand
is removed**, the cell is **unable to fill the resulting gap**, because **there is no upstream strand
onto which DNA polymerase can build** and therefore **no free 3'-OH available**.

**5.** **A small section at the extreme 3' end of the parental strand does not undergo DNA
replication**, so the parental strand is left with a **3' single-stranded overhang**.

**6.** Hence the **daughter DNA strand resulting from lagging strand synthesis is shortened with each
round of replication**, and is **shorter than its parental template strand**.

**7.** A bacterium escapes it because the **prokaryotic chromosome is a small circular DNA molecule**.

**8.** A **circular molecule has no ends**, so **there is no terminal primer whose removal leaves an
unfillable gap** — the last primer removed always has an upstream fragment supplying a 3'-OH, because
the strand closes on itself.

**(b)** *Whether the loss falls at one end of the chromosome only.* [INFERRED — extension, no official
key] **[4]**

**1.** The student is **wrong**. Sequence is lost from **both ends of the chromosome**.

**2.** As Fig. 5.1 shows, **each daughter strand is leading at one end of the chromosome and lagging at
the other**, because the two forks travel away from the origin in opposite directions along
antiparallel templates.

**3.** So at each end of the chromosome **one of the two new strands is being completed by lagging
strand synthesis**, and it is that strand which cannot be finished.

**4.** The correct statement is therefore that **each daughter molecule is shortened at one end, and
the two daughter molecules are shortened at opposite ends** — so across the pair, **both ends of the
original chromosome lose sequence**. The problem is a property of **lagging strand synthesis**, not of
a particular end.

**Source:** CJC Prelim 2023 P2 Q3c [2] (a — the fullest written-out version of this key, verbatim,
including "no upstream free 3'-OH available" and "elongation extending in a 5' to 3' direction");
Topical 2017 DNA-STQ Q3c, from RVHS Prelim 2017 P2 Q3(c) [2] (a — the four-clause short key,
verbatim); Topical 2007 STQ Q1c(ii), from IJC Prelim P2 Q3(c)(ii) [1] (a — "chromosome is circular, no
telomeres", verbatim); ACJC Prelim 2017 P2 Q3c [4] (a and b — this key is the one that says the **3'
end of the leading strand** is not fully replicated, which is what makes part (b) worth asking).
The figure is the school's own Fig. 37, 2.1 p. 35, cropped to stop before the notes' own explanation.
**Archetype:** mechanism explain-chain on a required, high-yield, easily-dropped outcome.

**Concepts fused:** (1) linearity as the precondition; (2) Okazaki fragments each with their own

### Question 6

**(a)** *Compare the centromere and the telomere, then rank them by speed of failure.* **[10]**

*Shared, max 2:*

**1.** Both are **non-coding DNA**: neither is **transcribed into a product**, so neither **encodes
protein or RNA**.

**2.** Both consist of **tandem repetitive DNA sequences**, and both are **associated with specific
proteins** — the centromere with **centromere-specific histones forming specialised nucleosomes**, the
telomere with **telomere-binding proteins**, making it a **specialised nucleoprotein complex**.

*Contrasted, point to point, max 4:*

**3.** **Position:** the **centromere is the region where two sister chromatids are joined in a
replicated chromosome during cell division**, whereas the **telomere is located at the two physical
ends or tips of a linear eukaryotic chromosome**. So there is **one centromere per chromosome and two
telomeres per chromatid end**.

**4.** **Sequence:** centromeric DNA is **short, AT-rich tandem repeats with no centromere-specific
sequence**, whereas human telomeres are **hundreds to as many as 2000 tandem repeats of 5'-TTAGGG-3'**,
a defined and **G-rich** sequence.

**5.** **Higher-order structure:** the centromere is **embedded in a very large stretch of
heterochromatin** and **nucleates the kinetochore**, whereas the telomere carries a **3' single-stranded
overhang extending 12–16 nucleotides beyond the 5' end of the complementary C-rich strand**, which
**folds back on itself to form a hairpin loop called the telomere loop (t-loop)**.

**6.** **Persistence:** the centromere is **conserved through the life of the cell**, whereas the
telomere **shortens with every round of DNA replication**.

*Functions, max 3:*

**7.** Centromere, any two of: **sister chromatid adhesion**; **kinetochore formation**, the
**kinetochore being the protein complex that attaches to the microtubules of the mitotic / meiotic
spindle**; and **proper chromosome segregation**, so that **one copy goes to each of the two daughter
cells**.

**8.** Telomere, any two of: **protective**, the t-loop cap **preventing degradation of the chromosome
end by exonucleases** and stopping it being recognised as damaged DNA; **maintaining stability**, by
**preventing end-to-end fusion of chromosomes**; **preventing the loss of genes**, since the erosion
falls on repeats rather than on **interior coding sequence**; and **regulating replicative cell
senescence**, the length acting as a **counting mechanism** for divisions undergone.

*The ranking, max 1:*

**9.** The **centromere kills faster**. Losing it is **catastrophic at the very next division** —
without a kinetochore there is **no spindle attachment**, so the daughter chromosomes **segregate
randomly**. Losing the telomeres is **cumulative rather than immediate**: the chromosome is **still
segregated correctly**, and the cell divides on until **erosion reaches coding sequence** or the
**critical length triggers senescence**. One is a **single-division failure**, the other a **failure
measured in tens of divisions**.

**(b)** *Account for row (B), and predict the two-centromere case.* **[6]**

**1.** In row (B) there is **no centromere, so there is no site for assembly of the kinetochore**.

**2.** Without a kinetochore the chromatids have **no attachment to the kinetochore microtubules of the
spindle**, so **no poleward force acts on them**.

**3.** Therefore **the daughter chromosomes segregate randomly, leading to loss or duplication of
chromosomes in the daughter cells** — the outcome annotated on the figure.

**4.** Over successive divisions this gives daughter cells with **the wrong number of chromosomes**,
and some receiving **no copy of that chromosome at all**.

*The prediction.* [INFERRED — extension, no official key]

**5.** With **two centromeres, two kinetochores assemble on the same chromatid**.

**6.** They can be **captured by microtubules from opposite poles**, so the **single chromatid is pulled
in two directions at once**, and the chromosome is **stretched across the spindle and broken**.

**7.** This is why the notes state that **the presence of only one centromere on each chromosome is
critical** — both **none** and **more than one** defeat segregation, for opposite mechanical reasons.

### Question 8

**(a)** *Classify every DNA region in Fig. 8.1, and account for the two senses of "non-coding".*
**[8]**

**1.** **Non-coding DNA refers to portions that do not encode protein or RNA.** On that definition
only the **exons** in Fig. 8.1 are **coding**; **every other region drawn on the DNA is non-coding**.

**2.** **Exons — coding.** They are the **discontinuous coding sequences** that are **transcribed and
retained in the mature mRNA**, and they carry the **coding segment from the start codon to the stop
codon**.

**3.** **Introns — non-coding.** They **are transcribed into the primary RNA transcript**, but they are
**removed during RNA processing** and **are not present in the mature mRNA**, so they are never
translated. Fig. 8.1 shows them leaving as **intron RNA**.

**4.** **Promoter — non-coding.** It is **a series of DNA sequences located upstream of the
transcriptional start site** where **RNA polymerase and transcription factors bind to initiate
transcription**, and it **determines which of the two strands is used as the template**.

**5.** **Proximal control elements and the enhancer — non-coding.** They are **segments of DNA involved
in regulating the initiation and rate of transcription by binding particular proteins**.

**6.** **The poly-A signal sequence and the transcription termination region — non-coding.** They are
transcribed, but they are **signals**: the transcribed terminator **codes for the polyadenylation
signal**, at which the transcript is **cleaved**.

**7.** **The 5' UTR and 3' UTR** shown on the mRNA correspond to DNA that is **transcribed but not
translated** — the 5' UTR **starts at the +1 position and ends one nucleotide before the start codon**,
and the 3' UTR **starts after the stop codon**.

**8.** So "non-coding" is covering two different situations, and a good answer separates them. Some
regions are **never transcribed at all** — the promoter, the proximal control elements and the
enhancer, which are **read by proteins as binding sites, not copied**. Others **are transcribed and
then discarded or left untranslated** — the introns, the UTRs and the poly-A signal. The **exon** is
the only category defined by being **kept and read**.

R: "introns are non-coding because they are not transcribed" — they are transcribed. R: counting the
UTRs as coding because they lie inside exons.

**(b)** *How a distal control element acts over thousands of base pairs.* **[6]**

**1.** The region is an **enhancer, a group of distal control elements**. **Distal control elements are
effective hundreds or thousands of base pairs upstream or downstream of the gene, may lie within an
intron, and function independently of their orientation.**

**2.** **Specific transcription factors called activators bind to the enhancer.** Each has **two kinds
of binding domain — a DNA binding domain and one or more protein binding (activation) domains.**

**3.** Separately, **general transcription factors bind the promoter and mediate the binding of RNA
polymerase, forming the transcription initiation complex**, at which the rate of transcription is
**basal**.

**4.** A **DNA-bending protein loops the intervening DNA**, which **brings the distant bound activators
into close proximity with the promoter**. Distance in base pairs is irrelevant once the DNA can fold,
and so is orientation, because the loop can form either way round.

**5.** The activators then **interact with mediator proteins**, adaptor molecules that **facilitate
activator–general transcription factor–polymerase interaction**, improving recruitment to form a
**stable transcription initiation complex** and positioning it correctly. The rate of transcription is
**increased above basal**.

**6.** Which is DNA and which is protein: **enhancers and silencers are DNA sequences**; **activators
and repressors are proteins**, as are **transcription factors, mediator proteins and the DNA-bending
protein**. **Activators bind enhancers to increase the transcription rate; repressors bind silencers
to decrease it.**

### Question 9

**(a)** *The consequences of the single difference in Fig. 9.1.* **[8]**

**1.** **At the 2' carbon of deoxyribose, the hydroxyl group (–OH) is replaced by a hydrogen atom
(H).** That is the whole difference, and **it distinguishes RNA from DNA**.

**2.** **The partial negative charge of the hydroxyl group in ribose repels the negative charge of the
phosphate**, which **prevents the RNA chain from coiling in as tight a helix as it does in DNA**.

**3.** **Hence RNA is more susceptible to chemical and enzyme degradation**, whereas DNA, coiling into
a tight helix with only the backbone exposed, is **less susceptible to degradation**. This is why the
long-term store of genetic information is built from the deoxy sugar.

**4.** Both nucleotides are nevertheless **interchangeable at the polymerase**: **both have a phosphate
group bound to carbon-5 of the pentose sugar**, which **allows formation of phosphodiester bonds to
form polynucleotides**; **both have an OH group bound to carbon-3**; and **both have nitrogenous bases
bound to carbon-1**, allowing **complementary base pairing**.

**5.** A primer is needed at all because **none of the DNA polymerases can initiate the synthesis of a
DNA strand on its own — DNA synthesis cannot occur de novo.** The **shape of the active site of DNA
polymerase is complementary to the 5' phosphate group of the incoming nucleotide and the 3' –OH group
of the last nucleotide of the growing strand**, so it **can only add a deoxyribonucleotide to a free
3' –OH end of an existing strand**. [CROSS-BOOK — Book 1: active site complementarity]

**6.** **Primase joins ribonucleotides to make the primer**, and the **RNA primer provides a free 3' OH
end that DNA polymerase can extend**. Primase can do this because, unlike DNA polymerase, it **does not
require a pre-existing 3' end**.

**7.** Building the primer from RNA marks it as **chemically distinguishable from the DNA around it**,
so it can be **found and removed later** — and its **greater instability** makes it a poor thing to
leave in a molecule whose whole job is stable storage.

**8.** It is removed for exactly that reason: **DNA polymerase I uses 5'→3' exonuclease activity to
remove the RNA primer and replace the ribonucleotides with DNA nucleotides**, and **DNA ligase then
seals the remaining nick**. A daughter strand that kept its RNA primers would carry **~10-nucleotide
stretches of the less stable nucleic acid** at every Okazaki junction.

**(b)** *Predict the effect of ddCTP, and name the inhibition.* **[6]**

**1.** **ddCTP competes with dCTP for the active site of DNA polymerase**, because the two are
**identical apart from the 3' position** and so are **similar enough in shape to bind the same active
site**.

**2.** This is **competitive inhibition**: the inhibitor is a **substrate analogue** that **binds the
active site rather than an allosteric site**, so the effect **can be reduced by raising the
concentration of dCTP**. [CROSS-BOOK — Book 1 enzymes]

**3.** Once bound, ddCTP is **incorporated into the growing strand**, since it **forms a
phospho(di)ester bond with the 3' hydroxyl group of the daughter strand** by complementary base pairing
with a G on the template.

**4.** But **ddCTP lacks a 3' hydroxyl group on the deoxyribose / pentose sugar**.

**5.** Therefore **the incorporated ddCTP cannot form a phosphodiester bond with the next incoming
dNTP**, because there is **no free 3' –OH for the polymerase to extend**.

**6.** **DNA replication is terminated prematurely** at that point, and the strand stops wherever a
ddCTP was taken up. The result is a **population of daughter strands of differing lengths, each ending
in C**.
