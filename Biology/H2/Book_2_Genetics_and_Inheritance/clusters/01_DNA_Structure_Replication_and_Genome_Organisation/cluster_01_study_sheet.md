## A. The monomer, the bonds, and why directionality exists

**A1. Three parts, and the two of them that get confused.** **"Nucleic acids are macromolecules that
exist as polymers called polynucleotides. Each polynucleotide is composed of repeating units /
monomers called nucleotides."** Each nucleotide is **"composed of three parts: a. a five-carbon
sugar, i.e. pentose; b. a nitrogenous base; and c. a phosphate group."** A **nucleoside** is only two
of those: **"the combination of a pentose with a nitrogenous base."** Nucleoside plus phosphate gives
a nucleotide. Examiners test the distinction directly.

**A2. Three named bonds, all built by condensation.** This is the Book 1 machinery running again, and
the bond names are marks.

| Bond | Joins | Made by |
|---|---|---|
| **glycosidic** | **1' carbon of the pentose** to the **nitrogenous base** | condensation, water eliminated |
| **phosphoester** | **5' carbon of pentose** to the **phosphate group** | condensation |
| **phosphodiester** | **5'-phosphate group of one nucleotide** to the **3'-hydroxyl group of the other** | condensation |

**Phosphodiester bonds "are strong covalent bonds. They confer strength and stability on the
polynucleotide chain. This is the basis in preventing breakage of the chain during DNA
replication."** That last clause is the *reason*, and reasons are what get marked.

**A3. Directionality is a chemical fact, not a convention.** **"Each DNA or RNA strand / chain has
two free ends that are chemically different from each other: 5' end with a free 5' carbon carrying a
phosphate group; and 3' end with a free 3' carbon carrying a hydroxyl (–OH) group."** The sequence is
**"read in a 5' to 3' direction."** Every direction mark in this Book — polymerase building 5'→3',
template read 3'→5', the primer supplying a free 3' OH — traces back to this one asymmetry.

**A4. Terminology the mark scheme uses.** **"1 DNA molecule = 1 DNA double-helix = 2 polynucleotide
strands / chains."** Say "molecule" when you mean the duplex and "strand" when you mean one chain. A
banding-pattern question can turn entirely on which one you counted.

## B. The double helix, and why every dimension is forced

**B1. Antiparallel.** **"The strands run in opposite directions, i.e. they are antiparallel. One
strand is oriented in the 5' to 3' direction whilst the other is oriented in the 3' to 5'
direction."** This is the structural fact the pre-study sheet told you to find. It forces the
leading/lagging split in section E, it forces one strand to be the template in transcription, and it
is why a probe finds its target.

**B2. Complementary base pairing, with the reason attached.** **"Specific, complementary base pairing
occurs between A and T (2 hydrogen bonds) and between C and G (3 hydrogen bonds)."** Two separate
reasons make the pairing specific, and the mark scheme wants both:

- **Steric.** **"The diameter of the helix is uniformly 2 nm. Hence, there is just enough space for
  1 purine and 1 pyrimidine in the centre of the double-helix."** A purine must face a pyrimidine or
  the width changes.
- **Chemical.** Each base carries **defined hydrogen-bonding side groups**, so A fits T and C fits G
  and nothing else does.

Two consequences follow, and both are examined. **"The base sequence in one strand determines the
base sequence in the complementary strand."** And **"separating the A–T pair by heating is easier
than separating the G–C pair, since the A–T pair involves 2 hydrogen bonds and the G–C pair involves
3 hydrogen bonds"** — which is why melting temperature rises with GC content and why origins of
replication are A–T rich.

**B3. The dimensions, and the one that is a stabilising force rather than a measurement.**
**Diameter uniformly 2 nm.** **"Base pairs are stacked 0.34 nm apart along the central axis of the
helix as a result hydrophobic interaction contributing to overall stability of the molecule."**
**"The double-helix makes a complete turn every 10 base pairs, so each turn is 3.4 nm."** And
**"there are grooves of unequal sizes between the sugar-phosphate backbones called the major groove
and minor groove."** Base stacking is the one people drop: it is a **Book 1 hydrophobic interaction**
doing structural work inside DNA.

**B4. Stability and heredity are the same argument run twice.** DNA must **store information with
chemical stability**, **replicate accurately**, and **remain capable of variation**. Every one of
those is a structural consequence, which is why the 15-mark question is *"describe the features of
DNA structure that allow DNA to stably store and accurately replicate a large amount of genetic
information"* and not *"describe DNA"*.

- **Stability:** covalent phosphodiester backbone; extensive hydrogen bonds; hydrophobic stacking;
  **only the sugar-phosphate backbone is exposed**, with the **hydrophilic phosphates projecting
  outwards** and the **nitrogenous bases orientated inwards at almost right angles, safely tucked
  inside**; in eukaryotes, winding around histones.
- **Accurate replication:** complementary base pairing means one strand specifies the other, so
  **both strands act as templates**, and the information is **redundant — present more than once in
  the molecule**, so a damaged strand can be **repaired from its intact partner**.
- **Variation:** the **base sequence along each DNA strand can be varied in countless ways**, so
  stability and variability are not in conflict. The backbone is invariant; the sequence carried on
  it is free.

## C. Replication is semi-conservative, and two rival models were live

**C1. The definition, verbatim.** **"Each of the two daughter DNA molecules consists of one parental
DNA strand and one newly-synthesised daughter DNA strand."**

**C2. The two models it beat.** **Conservative** — **"parental DNA molecule emerges from the
replication process intact, i.e. it is conserved, and generates DNA copies consisting of entirely new
molecules."** **Dispersive** — **"all four strands of DNA following replication have a mixture of old
and new DNA."** You do not have to recite Meselson and Stahl (the notes list it as a Learning
Experience, and no LO names it), but you must be able to say what each model **predicts** after one
and after two rounds, because the banding pattern is the commonest data question in this chapter.

**C3. Where replication starts.** **"DNA replication begins at one or more sites on the DNA molecule
called origins of replication (oriR). Each oriR is a specific sequence of nucleotides, which is
generally A-T rich (there are only 2 hydrogen bonds between each A-T base pair, hence it is easier to
disrupt the bonds as less energy is needed to overcome them)."** The bracket is the mark.

**C4. The fork, and the eukaryotic advantage.** **"At each end of a replication bubble Y-shaped
structure called a replication fork, where the new strands of DNA are synthesised. The two
replication forks move away from the oriR as replication proceeds bidirectionally."** Prokaryotes:
one circular chromosome, **a single oriR**, termination halfway round. Eukaryotes: linear
chromosomes, **multiple oriR**, bubbles expand laterally and fuse. The advantage of many origins is
**speed** — a human genome takes hours with them and would take orders of magnitude longer with one.

## D. The enzyme roster — name AND job, every time

Naming an enzyme without its job scores nothing, and so does the job without the enzyme. Write
*"helicase unwinds the double helix by breaking the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases"*, not
*"helicase unwinds DNA"*.

| Order | Enzyme or protein | The job, as the notes state it | Energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Initiator proteins** | recognise and bind the **A-T rich oriR**; separate the helix into two strands, forming a **replication bubble** | ATP |
| 2 | **Topoisomerase** (in *E. coli*, **DNA gyrase**) | **"cleave a strand of the helix to create a transient single-stranded nick"**, relieving the **supercoiling / torque ahead of the fork** by allowing **free rotation (swivelling) around the intact strand**, then **reseal the broken strand** | — |
| 3 | **Helicase** | **"break the hydrogen bonds holding the two strands of DNA together. This unwinds the DNA double-helix and separates the parental DNA strands at the region of the replication fork."** | **ATP as energy source** |
| 4 | **Single-strand DNA-binding proteins** | **"temporarily stabilise"** the unwound ssDNA; **"prevents the single-stranded DNA from re-annealing to reform the duplex"**; **"keeps the two parental strands in the appropriate single-stranded condition to act as template"**; **protects the very unstable ssDNA from degradation** | — |
| 5 | **Primase** | **"joins the ribonucleotides to make the primer"**, about **10 nucleotides long in eukaryotes**; **"the RNA primer provides a free 3' OH end that DNA polymerase can extend"** | **hydrolysis of ATP** |
| 6 | **DNA polymerase III** | reads the template, assembles **dNTPs by complementary base pairing**, and forms the **phosphodiester bond** between the **free 3' OH of the growing strand** and the **5' phosphate of the incoming dNTP**; builds the leading strand and every Okazaki fragment | **"the incoming dNTP loses a pyrophosphate group… the energy released from pyrophosphate bond breakage is coupled to"** bond formation |
| 6b | **DNA polymerase III, proofreading** | **"when an incorrect base pair is recognised, DNA polymerase reverses its direction by one base pair… the 3'→5' exonuclease activity of the enzyme allows the incorrect base pair to be excised"** | — |
| 7 | **DNA polymerase I** | uses **5'→3' exonuclease activity** to **remove the RNA primer** and replace the ribonucleotides with DNA nucleotides, adding them to the **3' end of the adjacent Okazaki fragment** | — |
| 8 | **DNA ligase** | **"a linking enzyme — catalyses the formation of a phosphoester bond between the 3' end of each new Okazaki fragment and the 5' end of the growing daughter DNA strand"** | — |

> **A wording trap inside your own notes.** Section 2.1.3 calls the sugar–phosphate linkage a
> **phosphodiester bond**; sections 3.3.4 calls the identical linkage a **phosphoester bond** twice,
> on the same page as "phosphodiester bond formation". **Write phosphodiester.** It is the term the
> notes define formally and the one the structural chapters use.

## E. Leading and lagging: the asymmetry is forced, not chosen

**E1. Two limitations, stated as limitations.** The notes box them, and the exam expects them as the
*premises* of the answer.

- **Limitation 1:** **"None of the DNA polymerases can initiate the synthesis of a DNA strand on its
  own. In other words, DNA synthesis cannot occur de novo."** Hence a primer, and hence primase.
- **Limitation 2:** **"DNA polymerases only add dNTPs to the free 3' end of a growing DNA strand,
  never to the 5' end. Thus, a growing DNA strand can only elongate in the 5' to 3' direction."**

**E2. Add antiparallel and the conclusion is unavoidable.** Two templates run in opposite directions;
polymerase can only extend one way; therefore **continuous synthesis of both daughter strands at one
fork is impossible.** That sentence is the answer to *"explain why one strand is synthesised
discontinuously"*, which is the highest-frequency 4–6 mark question in this chapter. Everything else
is detail hung off it.

**E3. The two strands, defined verbatim.**

- **Leading strand** — **"the complementary daughter DNA strand that is continuously synthesised as a
  single polymer along the template strand. Polymerised in the mandatory 5' to 3' manner towards the
  replication fork."** One primer.
- **Lagging strand** — **"the complementary DNA strand that is discontinuously synthesised as a
  series of short fragments known as Okazaki fragments. Each Okazaki fragment is polymerised in the
  mandatory 5' to 3' manner against the overall direction of the replication fork. About 100 to 200
  nucleotides per fragment in eukaryotes."** One primer **per fragment**, then **DNA polymerase I**,
  then **ligase**.

**E4. The four axes a "compare" question wants.** Point to point, or the marks go: **continuous vs
discontinuous**; **towards vs against the replication fork**; **one primer vs many**; **ligase not
required vs ligase required**.

## F. The end replication problem

**Required by LO 2(b), and easy to lose by accident** because it sits next to content that has been
cut. The problem and the telomere are both examinable. Telomerase is not — see §K.

**F1. Where it happens.** **"The end replication problem occurs in linear chromosomes (i.e. in
eukaryotes only) as DNA polymerase is incapable of completely replicating all the way to the ends of
linear chromosome, leading to shortening of telomeres with each round of DNA replication."** A
circular chromosome has no ends, so it never arises.

**F2. Why the gap cannot be filled.** **"When the final RNA primer at the end of lagging strand is
removed, there is no upstream strand onto which DNA polymerase can build to fill the resulting
gap."** Unpack that into the two facts it rests on: **DNA polymerase only extends an existing 3' OH**,
and at the extreme end there is **no upstream fragment to supply one**.

**F3. What is left behind.** **"Each time a cell with linear chromosome divides, a small section at
the extreme 3' end of the parental strand does not undergo DNA replication."** So the parental strand
keeps a **3' overhang** and **"the daughter DNA strand resulting from lagging strand synthesis would
be shortened with each round of replication."**

**F4. The distinction that separates a good answer from a vague one.** Each daughter strand is
**leading at one end of the chromosome and lagging at the other**. The loss is therefore a
**lagging-strand-synthesis** problem at each end, not a whole-strand problem, and the chromosome
loses sequence from **both** ends across the two daughter molecules.

## G. Packing: three named levels, each with its protein

**G1. Why pack at all.** **"To organise and pack the giant DNA molecules of eukaryotic chromosomes
into structures that will facilitate their segregation onto daughter nuclei"** so that **"DNA
molecules of different chromosomes will not be entangled and as a consequence, break during
separation at anaphase."** Compaction is the first argument. Access is the second, and it is the one
Book 2 spends the rest of itself on.

**G2. First level — the nucleosome.** **"Nucleosomes packing process involves a molecule of DNA
coiled around an octamer of histone proteins, two each of histones H2A, H2B, H3 and H4."** The
mechanism is charge: **"histones are small proteins with a high concentration of positively-charged
residues, e.g. lysine and arginine, which form ionic bonds with the negatively-charged
sugar-phosphate backbone of DNA."** **146 base pairs** bind the core. Multiple nucleosomes give the
**"beads-on-a-string"** look, the **10-nm chromatin fibre / nucleosome fibre**.

> This is a **Book 1 protein** doing a Book 2 job. The octamer is a **quaternary structure**, eight
> polypeptide subunits; the attraction is an **ionic bond between charged R groups** and a charged
> backbone. If you can say that, you can also say why anything that neutralises those positive
> charges loosens the DNA — which is the whole of Cluster 2's chromatin-level control.

**G3. Second level — the solenoid.** **"DNA is further folded or coiled to produce the 30-nm
chromatin fibre, known as solenoid. Histone H1 and linker DNA are involved in this coiling."** H1 is
the discriminating detail: it is **not** one of the eight.

**G4. Third level — scaffold and looped domains.** **"Non-histone proteins known as scaffold proteins
are involved in condensing the 30-nm chromatin fibre to form looped domains. In mitotic and meiotic
chromosomes, the looped domains themselves coil and fold, further compacting all the chromatin to
produce the characteristic metaphase chromosome."** **"The width of one chromatid is 700 nm."**

**G5. The dimension ladder, because schemes award the numbers.**
**2 nm double helix → 10 nm nucleosome fibre → 30 nm chromatin fibre → 300 nm looped domains →
700 nm chromatid → 1400 nm replicated metaphase chromosome.**

**G6. Packing state is itself information.** **Euchromatin** is **diffuse and available for
transcription**; **heterochromatin** is **highly condensed and transcriptionally inactive**, because
condensation prevents transcription factors and RNA polymerase from reaching the promoter.
Centromeres sit **embedded in heterochromatin**. That is as far as Cluster 1 goes — how a cell moves
DNA between the two states is LO 2(i), Cluster 2.

## H. The eukaryotic genome at scale

**H1. Definitions, verbatim.** **"Genome: The genetic material of an organism or virus; the complete
complement of an organism's or virus's genes along with its non-coding nucleic acid sequences."**
**"Gene: A gene is a section of the DNA that contains the information in the form of a specific
sequence of nucleotides/bases to direct the synthesis of one polypeptide chain or RNA. It is a unit
of inheritance located in a fixed position (locus) on the chromosome which specifies a particular
character of an organism."** The second sentence is the half students drop, and it is worth a mark on
its own. **"Gene density"** is **"the measure of the number of genes per million base pairs (Mb) in
the genome."**

**H2. A complete eukaryotic genome is more than the nucleus.** Haploid set of **linear nuclear
chromosomes**, plus the **mitochondrial genome** (a single small circular DNA), plus a **chloroplast
genome** in photosynthetic organisms. LO 2(a) says the *structure and roles* of mitochondrial and
chloroplast DNA are **not required** — their existence as components of the genome still is.

**H3. Four claims about size and complexity, and only the first is a clean correlation.**

1. More complex organisms **tend** to have larger genomes — a mammalian genome is **100–1000 times**
   a bacterial one.
2. **The correlation breaks down among higher eukaryotes.** Fish, amphibians, reptiles, mammals and
   birds have comparable genome sizes despite very different complexity.
3. **Genome size is not proportional to gene number.** The human genome is far larger than the
   pufferfish genome and carries fewer genes.
4. **Gene density is far higher in prokaryotes.** The human genome is roughly 1000× larger than
   *E. coli*'s and has roughly 100× lower gene density.

The explanation for 2, 3 and 4 is the same one thing: **eukaryotic genomes are mostly non-coding**.
**Prokaryotes typically have 85–90% of their genomes containing structural genes, against only 1–5%
for the eukaryotic genome.** Everything else is introns, control elements, repetitive DNA and
intergenic DNA.

## I. Non-coding DNA — the six the syllabus names

LO 2(h) is a list, and the list is closed: **introns, centromeres, telomeres, promoters, enhancers
and silencers**, each with **structure and function**. **"Non-coding"** means **portions that do not
encode protein or RNA**, and it covers two different situations that you must be able to separate:
sequences that are **never transcribed** (promoters, enhancers, silencers, centromeres, telomeres),
and sequences that **are transcribed but removed before translation** (introns).

**I1. The gene, and what surrounds it.** A eukaryotic gene splits into a **transcription unit**
(**exons**, the only coding category, and **introns**) and **regulatory sequences**.
**"Regulatory sequences: Regions of DNA sequence where gene regulatory proteins bind to control the
rate of assembly of protein complexes required for gene expression."** **Intron DNA usually exceeds
exon DNA**, and the number and size of introns per gene varies.

**I2. Promoter.** **"A series of DNA sequences located upstream of the transcriptional start site.
RNA polymerase and transcription factors bind to the promoter to initiate transcription."** It also
**determines which of the two strands is used as the template**.

**I3. Control elements.** **"Segments of DNA involved in regulating the initiation and rate of
transcription by binding particular proteins; these include proximal and distal control elements that
are located near to and far from the promoter respectively."**

- **Proximal control elements** — where **general (or basal) transcription factors** bind to initiate
  transcription.
- **Distal control elements** — **"enhancers, DNA sequences that bind specific regulatory proteins
  known as activators to increase transcription rate, and silencers which interact with other
  specific regulatory proteins known as repressors to decrease transcription rate."** They work from
  **hundreds or thousands of base pairs away, upstream or downstream, and may lie inside an intron**,
  because the DNA **loops** to bring the bound protein next to the promoter. Enhancer and silencer
  are **DNA**; activator and repressor are **protein**. Schemes set traps on exactly that.

**I4. Telomere — structure.** **"Telomeres consist of specialised nucleoprotein which are complexes
composed of telomeric DNA bound by specific proteins."** **"Human telomeres contain hundreds to as
many as 2000 tandem repeats of the sequence 5'-TTAGGG-3'."** **"The 3' end of the G-rich strand
extends 12–16 nucleotides beyond the 5' end of the complementary C-rich strand, forming a 3'
single-stranded overhang"**, and **"this overhang folds back on itself to form a hairpin loop called
telomere loop (t-loop)."** Located **at the two physical ends or tips of a linear eukaryotic
chromosome**.

**I5. Telomere — four functions, tightly worded.**

1. **Protective** — the t-loop cap **prevents degradation of the chromosome end by exonucleases** and
   stops the end being **recognised as damaged DNA**.
2. **Maintaining stability** — **prevents end-to-end fusion** of chromosomes.
3. **Preventing loss of genes** — the erosion caused by the end replication problem falls on
   **non-coding repeats instead of on interior coding sequence**.
4. **Regulating replicative cell senescence** — telomere length acts as a **counting mechanism** for
   how many divisions a cell has undergone.

**I6. Centromere — structure.** **"The centromere is the region on where two sister chromatids are
joined in a replicated chromosome during cell division."** The DNA is **short, AT-rich, non-coding
sequence repeated thousands of times in tandem**; **"the numerous repeats vary slightly from one
another in sequence; there is apparently no centromere-specific DNA sequence"**; it is **embedded in
a very large stretch of heterochromatin**; and it is **bound by centromere-specific histones to form
specialised nucleosomes**, whose folding **"facilitates the assembly of other centromere-binding
proteins to form the kinetochore that associates the centromere to the mitotic spindle."**

**I7. Centromere — three functions.**

1. **Sister chromatid adhesion.**
2. **Kinetochore formation** — the protein complex that **attaches to spindle microtubules**.
3. **Proper chromosome segregation** — **exactly one centromere per chromosome is critical**; with
   none, **daughter chromosomes segregate randomly, leading to loss or duplication of chromosomes in
   the daughter cells**.

**I8. Introns.** Transcribed into pre-mRNA, then **removed**, so a mutation inside an intron usually
leaves the amino acid sequence untouched — provided the splice sites themselves are intact. The
mechanism of splicing is 2.2, Cluster 2. What Cluster 1 owes you is the **classification**: an intron
is non-coding, it is inside the transcription unit, and it is the main reason a eukaryotic gene is
much longer than its own mRNA.

## L. Open recall prompts

Memorisation targets stay as prompts, not as statements to read. **Say each aloud before checking
anything**, and keep them in rotation until they are automatic.

1. Define a nucleotide and a nucleoside, and state what separates them.
2. Name the three bonds in a polynucleotide, what each joins, and the reaction that forms all three.
3. State what makes the 5' end and the 3' end chemically different.
4. Give the two separate reasons why A pairs only with T and C only with G.
5. State the four dimensions of the double helix and which one is caused by hydrophobic interaction.
6. Define semi-conservative replication in the notes' own words, and state what the conservative and
   dispersive models predict after two rounds.
7. Explain why origins of replication are A-T rich.
8. Name every enzyme and protein at the fork **in order**, each with its exact job.
9. State both limitations of DNA polymerases, and derive leading and lagging strand synthesis from
   them plus antiparallel strands.
10. Give the four axes on which leading and lagging strand synthesis differ.
11. Explain how the end replication problem arises, in the four steps of §F2 and §F3.
12. Give the packing hierarchy with every protein and every dimension.
13. State the two arguments for why DNA must be condensed.
14. Define genome, gene and gene density, verbatim.
15. Explain why a larger genome does not mean more genes.
16. Give the structure and the function of each of the six non-coding categories LO 2(h) names.

## M. Cautions found by checking the notes against themselves

1. **Two versions of the gene definition** exist across chapters (2.3 p. 6 and 2.2 p. 10). They
   differ only in *"contains the information in the form of a specific sequence of nucleotides/bases"*
   versus *"encodes information in the form of a specific base sequence"*. Pick one and stay with it.
   The second clause is identical in both and is the half most often dropped.
2. **Phosphodiester versus phosphoester** — see the box under §D. Write phosphodiester.
3. **A mislabelled caption on 2.1 p. 35** reads "Fig. 19: Illustration and explanation of the
   end-replication problem" immediately above the correct "Fig. 37". It is a duplication artifact
   from chapter 2.3, where the same diagram genuinely is Fig. 19. Ignore it.
4. **Two chapter headers in Book 2 quote the wrong LO letter** (2.4 says 2(j) but is 2(i); 2.6 says
   2(k) but is 2(j)). Neither affects Cluster 1, but do not build an LO index off the chapter fronts.
5. **The three RNAs are not in this cluster.** LO 2(a) requires the structure and roles of **mRNA,
   tRNA and rRNA**, and chapter 2.1 gives you only the pentose and base differences between DNA and
   RNA. The roles, the cloverleaf, the ribosome and the rest arrive with chapter 2.2 in **Cluster 2**.
   What Cluster 1 owes you is the chemistry: **ribose carries an OH at the 2' carbon where
   deoxyribose carries an H**, and that **"small difference has led to significant differences in
   structure and therefore, functions of the two types of nucleic acids"** — the 2' OH **repels the
   negative charge of the phosphate**, so RNA **cannot coil into as tight a helix as DNA**, and
   **RNA is more susceptible to chemical and enzyme degradation**. That instability is the reason a
   replication primer made of RNA is a sensible thing to build and then destroy.
