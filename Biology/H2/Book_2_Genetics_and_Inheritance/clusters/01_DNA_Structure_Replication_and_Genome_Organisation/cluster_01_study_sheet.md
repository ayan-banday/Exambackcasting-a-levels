## A. The monomer, the bonds and directionality

**A1. A nucleoside is two parts; a nucleotide is three.** Nucleic acids are **macromolecules that
exist as polymers called polynucleotides**, built from repeating **nucleotides**. Each nucleotide is
**a five-carbon sugar (pentose), a nitrogenous base and a phosphate group**. **A pentose combined
with a nitrogenous base** alone is a **nucleoside**; add the phosphate and it becomes a nucleotide.

**A2. Three named bonds, all made by condensation.** This is the Book 1 machinery running again, and
each bond name carries a mark.

| Bond | Joins | Made by |
|---|---|---|
| **glycosidic** | **1' carbon of the pentose** to the **nitrogenous base** | condensation, water eliminated |
| **phosphoester** | **5' carbon of pentose** to the **phosphate group** | condensation |
| **phosphodiester** | **5'-phosphate group of one nucleotide** to the **3'-hydroxyl group of the other** | condensation |

Phosphodiester bonds are **strong covalent bonds** that **confer strength and stability on the
polynucleotide chain**, which is **the basis in preventing breakage of the chain during DNA
replication**.

**A3. The two ends of a strand are chemically different.** A strand has a **5' end with a free 5'
carbon carrying a phosphate group** and a **3' end with a free 3' carbon carrying a hydroxyl (–OH)
group**, and sequences are **read in a 5' to 3' direction**. Polymerase building 5'→3', the template
read 3'→5' and the primer supplying a free 3' OH all trace back to that asymmetry.

**A4. Molecule and strand are not interchangeable.** **1 DNA molecule = 1 DNA double-helix = 2
polynucleotide strands / chains.** A banding-pattern question can turn on which one you counted.

## B. The double helix

**B1. The two strands are antiparallel.** **The strands run in opposite directions, i.e. they are
antiparallel**: **one is oriented in the 5' to 3' direction whilst the other is oriented in the 3' to
5' direction.** This forces the leading and lagging split in section E and makes one strand the
transcription template.

**B2. Base pairing is specific for two separate reasons.** **Specific, complementary base pairing
occurs between A and T (2 hydrogen bonds) and between C and G (3 hydrogen bonds).** Sterically, **the
diameter of the helix is uniformly 2 nm**, leaving **just enough space for 1 purine and 1 pyrimidine
in the centre of the double-helix**. Chemically, each base carries **defined hydrogen-bonding side
groups**, so A fits T and C fits G and nothing else does. Give both reasons.

Two consequences. **The base sequence in one strand determines the base sequence in the complementary
strand.** And **separating the A–T pair by heating is easier than separating the G–C pair, since the
A–T pair involves 2 hydrogen bonds and the G–C pair involves 3**, so melting temperature rises with
GC content and origins of replication are A–T rich.

**B3. The four dimensions of the helix.** **Diameter uniformly 2 nm.** **Base pairs stacked 0.34 nm
apart along the central axis**, as a result of **hydrophobic interaction contributing to overall
stability of the molecule**. **A complete turn every 10 base pairs, so each turn is 3.4 nm.** And
**grooves of unequal sizes between the sugar-phosphate backbones, called the major groove and minor
groove**, both wide enough for proteins to reach the bases. Base stacking is a force, not a
measurement: a Book 1 hydrophobic interaction doing structural work inside DNA.

**B4. Stability, accurate replication and variation are all structural consequences.** DNA must
**store information with chemical stability**, **replicate accurately** and **remain capable of
variation**, and each traces back to structure.

- **Stability:** the covalent phosphodiester backbone, extensive hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic
  stacking, **only the sugar-phosphate backbone exposed** with **hydrophilic phosphates projecting
  outwards** and **nitrogenous bases orientated inwards at almost right angles, safely tucked
  inside**, plus winding around histones in eukaryotes.
- **Accurate replication:** complementary base pairing means one strand specifies the other, so
  **both strands act as templates** and the information is **redundant, present more than once in the
  molecule**, letting a damaged strand be **repaired from its intact partner**.
- **Variation:** the **base sequence along each DNA strand can be varied in countless ways**. The
  backbone is invariant; the sequence carried on it is free.

The stock 15-mark question asks which structural features let DNA stably store and accurately
replicate genetic information, so this three-way split is the answer shape.

## C. Replication is semi-conservative

**C1. What semi-conservative means.** **Each of the two daughter DNA molecules consists of one
parental DNA strand and one newly-synthesised daughter DNA strand.**

**C2. The two rival models.** The **conservative** model had the **parental DNA molecule emerge from
replication intact**, generating **copies consisting of entirely new molecules**. The **dispersive**
model had **all four strands carry a mixture of old and new DNA**. Meselson and Stahl sit outside the
learning outcomes, but you must be able to state what all three models predict after one round and
after two, because the banding pattern is the commonest data question here.

**C3. Replication starts at A-T rich origins.** Replication **begins at one or more sites called
origins of replication (oriR)**, each **a specific sequence of nucleotides** that is **generally A-T
rich**, because **there are only 2 hydrogen bonds between each A-T base pair**, so **less energy is
needed** to disrupt them. The reason is the mark, not the location.

**C4. Forks move out from the origin in both directions.** At each end of a replication bubble sits a
**Y-shaped replication fork, where the new strands of DNA are synthesised**, and **the two forks move
away from the oriR as replication proceeds bidirectionally**. Prokaryotes have one circular
chromosome with **a single oriR**, terminating halfway round. Eukaryotes have linear chromosomes with
**multiple oriR**, and the bubbles expand laterally until they fuse. Many origins buy **speed**:
roughly eight hours for a human genome, against orders of magnitude longer from one.

## D. The enzymes at the fork, name and job together

Naming an enzyme without its job scores nothing, and so does the job without the enzyme.

| Order | Enzyme or protein | The job | Energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Initiator proteins** | recognise and bind the **A-T rich oriR**, separating the helix into two strands to form a **replication bubble** | ATP |
| 2 | **Topoisomerase** (in *E. coli*, **DNA gyrase**) | **cleave a strand of the helix to create a transient single-stranded nick**, relieving the **supercoiling / torque ahead of the fork** by allowing **free rotation (swivelling) around the intact strand**, then **reseal the broken strand** | not stated |
| 3 | **Helicase** | **break the hydrogen bonds holding the two strands together**, which **unwinds the double-helix and separates the parental strands at the replication fork** | **ATP as energy source** |
| 4 | **Single-strand DNA-binding proteins** | **temporarily stabilise** the unwound ssDNA, **prevent it re-annealing to reform the duplex**, **keep both parental strands in the appropriate single-stranded condition to act as template**, and protect the unstable ssDNA from degradation | not stated |
| 5 | **Primase** | **joins the ribonucleotides to make the primer**, **about 10 nucleotides long in eukaryotes**; the **RNA primer provides a free 3' OH end that DNA polymerase can extend** | **hydrolysis of ATP** |
| 6 | **DNA polymerase III** | assembles **dNTPs by complementary base pairing** against the template and forms the **phosphodiester bond** between the **free 3' OH of the growing strand** and the **5' phosphate of the incoming dNTP**; builds the leading strand and every Okazaki fragment | the dNTP **loses a pyrophosphate group**, and **pyrophosphate bond breakage is coupled to** bond formation |
| 6b | **DNA polymerase III, proofreading** | **when an incorrect base pair is recognised, DNA polymerase reverses its direction by one base pair**, and its **3'→5' exonuclease activity** excises the wrong pair | not stated |
| 7 | **DNA polymerase I** | uses **5'→3' exonuclease activity** to **remove the RNA primer**, replacing the ribonucleotides with DNA nucleotides added to the **3' end of the adjacent Okazaki fragment** | not stated |
| 8 | **DNA ligase** | a **linking enzyme** that **catalyses a phosphodiester bond between the 3' end of each new Okazaki fragment and the 5' end of the growing daughter DNA strand** | not stated |

> **A wording trap inside your own notes.** Section 2.1.3 calls the sugar-phosphate linkage a
> **phosphodiester bond**; section 3.3.4 calls the identical linkage a phosphoester bond twice. Write
> **phosphodiester** for any bond between two nucleotides, and keep **phosphoester** for the single
> sugar-to-phosphate ester inside one nucleotide.

## E. Leading and lagging strands

**E1. DNA polymerase has two limitations**, and the exam expects them as the premises of the answer.

- **Limitation 1: none of the DNA polymerases can initiate the synthesis of a DNA strand on its
  own**, so **DNA synthesis cannot occur de novo**. Hence a primer, and hence primase.
- **Limitation 2: DNA polymerases only add dNTPs to the free 3' end of a growing DNA strand, never
  to the 5' end**, so **a growing strand can only elongate in the 5' to 3' direction**.

**E2. Antiparallel strands plus limitation 2 force discontinuous synthesis.** The two templates run
in opposite directions and polymerase extends one way only, therefore **continuous synthesis of both
daughter strands at one fork is impossible**. That sentence answers why one strand is synthesised
discontinuously, the highest-frequency 4 to 6 mark question in this chapter.

**E3. Leading and lagging defined.** The **leading strand** is **continuously synthesised as a single
polymer along the template strand**, **polymerised in the mandatory 5' to 3' manner towards the
replication fork**, from one primer. The **lagging strand** is **discontinuously synthesised as a
series of short fragments known as Okazaki fragments**, each **polymerised in the mandatory 5' to 3'
manner against the overall direction of the fork**, **about 100 to 200 nucleotides per fragment in
eukaryotes**, needing one primer per fragment, then **DNA polymerase I**, then **ligase**.

**E4. Four axes for a compare question.** **Continuous vs discontinuous**, **towards vs against the
replication fork**, **one primer vs many**, **ligase not required vs ligase required**.

## F. The end replication problem

**F1. It happens only in linear chromosomes.** The **end replication problem occurs in linear
chromosomes, i.e. in eukaryotes only**, because **DNA polymerase cannot completely replicate all the
way to the ends**, which **shortens telomeres with each round of DNA replication**. A circular
chromosome has no ends, so it never arises there.

**F2. The gap cannot be filled because there is nothing to extend from.** **When the final RNA primer
at the end of the lagging strand is removed, there is no upstream strand onto which DNA polymerase
can build to fill the resulting gap.** Two facts underneath: polymerase can only extend an existing
free 3' OH, and at the extreme end there is no upstream Okazaki fragment to supply one.

**F3. What is left behind.** **A small section at the extreme 3' end of the parental strand does not
undergo DNA replication**, so the parental strand keeps a **3' overhang** and **the daughter strand
from lagging strand synthesis is shortened with each round**.

**F4. Each daughter strand is leading at one end of the chromosome and lagging at the other.** The
loss is a lagging-strand problem at each end rather than a whole-strand problem, so the chromosome
loses sequence from **both** ends across the two daughter molecules. Telomerase is not examinable;
see section K.

## G. Packing DNA into a chromosome

**G1. Condensation exists to allow segregation.** Packing **organises the giant DNA molecules of
eukaryotic chromosomes into structures that facilitate their segregation onto daughter nuclei**, so
that **chromosomes will not be entangled and break during separation at anaphase**. Compaction is the
first argument; controlled access to the DNA is the second, and Book 2 spends the rest of itself on
that one.

**G2. First level: the nucleosome.** A **molecule of DNA coils around an octamer of histone proteins,
two each of histones H2A, H2B, H3 and H4**, with **146 base pairs** bound to the core. The attraction
is electrostatic: **histones are small proteins with a high concentration of positively-charged
residues, e.g. lysine and arginine, which form ionic bonds with the negatively-charged
sugar-phosphate backbone of DNA**. Nucleosomes in a row give the **beads-on-a-string** appearance,
the **10-nm chromatin fibre** or **nucleosome fibre**. The octamer is a **quaternary structure** of
eight subunits, so neutralising those positive charges loosens the DNA, which is the whole of
Cluster 2's chromatin control.

**G3. Second level: the solenoid.** **DNA is further folded or coiled to produce the 30-nm chromatin
fibre, known as solenoid. Histone H1 and linker DNA are involved in this coiling.** H1 is the
discriminating detail, because it is not one of the eight in the core octamer.

**G4. Third level: scaffold proteins and looped domains.** **Non-histone proteins known as scaffold
proteins condense the 30-nm chromatin fibre to form looped domains**, and in mitotic and meiotic
chromosomes **the looped domains themselves coil and fold, further compacting all the chromatin to
produce the characteristic metaphase chromosome**. **The width of one chromatid is 700 nm.**

**G5. The dimension ladder.** **2 nm double helix → 10 nm nucleosome fibre → 30 nm chromatin fibre →
300 nm looped domains → 700 nm chromatid → 1400 nm replicated metaphase chromosome.**

**G6. How tightly a region is packed is itself information.** **Euchromatin** is **diffuse and
available for transcription**; **heterochromatin** is **highly condensed and transcriptionally
inactive**, because condensation stops transcription factors and RNA polymerase reaching the
promoter. Centromeres sit **embedded in heterochromatin**. Moving DNA between the two states is
LO 2(i), Cluster 2.

## H. The eukaryotic genome at scale

**H1. Three definitions to hold verbatim.** A **genome** is **the genetic material of an organism or
virus; the complete complement of an organism's or virus's genes along with its non-coding nucleic
acid sequences**. A **gene** is **a section of the DNA that contains the information in the form of a
specific sequence of nucleotides/bases to direct the synthesis of one polypeptide chain or RNA. It is
a unit of inheritance located in a fixed position (locus) on the chromosome which specifies a
particular character of an organism.** That second sentence is the half most candidates drop and it
is worth a mark on its own. **Gene density** is **the number of genes per million base pairs (Mb) in
the genome**.

**H2. A complete eukaryotic genome is more than the nucleus.** It is the haploid set of **linear
nuclear chromosomes**, plus the **mitochondrial genome**, a single small circular DNA, plus a
**chloroplast genome** in photosynthetic organisms. LO 2(a) does not require the structure and roles
of mitochondrial and chloroplast DNA, but their existence as genome components is still required.

**H3. Four claims about size and complexity, and only the first is a clean correlation.**

1. More complex organisms **tend** to have larger genomes, a mammalian genome being **100 to 1000
   times** a bacterial one.
2. **The correlation breaks down among higher eukaryotes.** Fish, amphibians, reptiles, mammals and
   birds have comparable genome sizes despite very different complexity.
3. **Genome size is not proportional to gene number.** The human genome is far larger than the
   pufferfish genome and carries fewer genes.
4. **Gene density is far higher in prokaryotes.** The human genome is roughly 1000 times larger than
   that of *E. coli* and has roughly 100 times lower gene density.

One fact explains claims 2, 3 and 4 together: **eukaryotic genomes are mostly non-coding**.
**Prokaryotes typically have 85–90% of their genomes containing structural genes, against only 1–5%
for the eukaryotic genome.** The rest is introns, control elements, repetitive DNA and intergenic
DNA.

## I. Non-coding DNA: the six categories the syllabus names

LO 2(h) is a closed list: **introns, centromeres, telomeres, promoters, enhancers and silencers**,
each needing a **structure** and a **function** statement. **Non-coding** means the **portions that
do not encode protein or RNA**, covering two situations you must keep apart: sequences **never
transcribed** (promoters, enhancers, silencers, centromeres, telomeres), and sequences **transcribed
but removed before translation** (introns).

**I1. What a eukaryotic gene contains.** A **transcription unit**, holding **exons**, the only coding
category, and **introns**; plus **regulatory sequences**, **regions of DNA sequence where gene
regulatory proteins bind to control the rate of assembly of protein complexes required for gene
expression**. **Intron DNA usually exceeds exon DNA.**

**I2. Promoter.** **A series of DNA sequences located upstream of the transcriptional start site.
RNA polymerase and transcription factors bind to the promoter to initiate transcription.** It also
**determines which of the two strands is used as the template**.

**I3. Control elements.** **Segments of DNA involved in regulating the initiation and rate of
transcription by binding particular proteins**, split into **proximal and distal control elements
located near to and far from the promoter respectively**. **Proximal control elements** bind
**general (or basal) transcription factors** to initiate transcription. **Distal control elements**
consist of **enhancers, DNA sequences that bind specific regulatory proteins known as activators to
increase transcription rate, and silencers which interact with other specific regulatory proteins
known as repressors to decrease transcription rate**. They act from **hundreds or thousands of base
pairs away, upstream or downstream, and may lie inside an intron**, because the DNA **loops** to
bring the bound protein next to the promoter. Enhancer and silencer are **DNA**; activator and
repressor are **protein**, and schemes set traps on that swap.

**I4. Telomere structure.** **Telomeres consist of specialised nucleoprotein, complexes composed of
telomeric DNA bound by specific proteins**, at the **two physical ends or tips of a linear eukaryotic
chromosome**. **Human telomeres contain hundreds to as many as 2000 tandem repeats of the sequence
5'-TTAGGG-3'.** **The 3' end of the G-rich strand extends 12–16 nucleotides beyond the 5' end of the
complementary C-rich strand, forming a 3' single-stranded overhang**, which **folds back on itself to
form a hairpin loop called telomere loop (t-loop)**.

**I5. Telomeres have four functions.** **Protective**: the t-loop cap **prevents degradation of the
chromosome end by exonucleases** and stops the end being **recognised as damaged DNA**.
**Maintaining stability**: it **prevents end-to-end fusion** of chromosomes. **Preventing loss of
genes**: erosion falls on **non-coding repeats instead of interior coding sequence**. **Regulating
replicative cell senescence**: telomere length is a **counting mechanism** for divisions undergone.

**I6. Centromere structure.** **The centromere is the region on the chromosome where two sister
chromatids are joined in a replicated chromosome during cell division.** Its DNA is **short, AT-rich,
non-coding sequence repeated thousands of times in tandem**, and **the repeats vary slightly from one
another, so there is apparently no centromere-specific DNA sequence**. It is **embedded in a very
large stretch of heterochromatin** and **bound by centromere-specific histones to form specialised
nucleosomes**, whose folding **facilitates the assembly of other centromere-binding proteins to form
the kinetochore that associates the centromere to the mitotic spindle**.

**I7. Centromeres have three functions.** **Sister chromatid adhesion**; **kinetochore formation**,
the protein complex that **attaches to spindle microtubules**; and **proper chromosome segregation**,
where **exactly one centromere per chromosome is critical**, because with none **daughter chromosomes
segregate randomly, leading to loss or duplication of chromosomes in the daughter cells**.

**I8. Introns.** Transcribed into pre-mRNA and then **removed**, so a mutation inside an intron
usually leaves the amino acid sequence untouched, provided the splice sites are intact. Splicing is
chapter 2.2, Cluster 2. Cluster 1 owes you the classification: non-coding, inside the transcription
unit, and the main reason a eukaryotic gene is much longer than its own mRNA.

## M. Cautions from checking the notes against themselves

1. **Two versions of the gene definition** exist, in 2.3 p. 6 and 2.2 p. 10, differing only in
   whether a gene contains the information in the form of a specific sequence of nucleotides/bases or
   encodes information in the form of a specific base sequence. Pick one and stay with it. The second
   clause is identical in both and is the half most often dropped.
2. **Ten base pairs per turn, not ten bases.** Page 12 and the concept map say 10 bases or
   nucleotides per turn, which halves the true figure. Only page 16 is right: **a complete turn every
   10 base pairs, so each turn is 3.4 nm**.
3. **A mislabelled caption on 2.1 p. 35** gives the end-replication diagram the number Fig. 19 above
   the correct Fig. 37. It is a duplication artifact from 2.3, where that diagram genuinely is
   Fig. 19. Ignore it.
4. **Two chapter headers quote the wrong LO letter**: 2.4 says 2(j) but is 2(i), and 2.6 says 2(k)
   but is 2(j). Do not build an LO index off the chapter fronts.
5. **The three RNAs are not in this cluster.** LO 2(a) requires the structure and roles of **mRNA,
   tRNA and rRNA**, and 2.1 gives only the pentose and base differences between DNA and RNA. The
   roles, the cloverleaf and the ribosome arrive with chapter 2.2 in **Cluster 2**. Cluster 1 owes
   you the chemistry: **ribose carries an OH at the 2' carbon where deoxyribose carries an H**, and
   that difference **has led to significant differences in structure and therefore functions of the
   two types of nucleic acids**. The 2' OH **repels the negative charge of the phosphate**, so RNA
   **cannot coil into as tight a helix as DNA** and **is more susceptible to chemical and enzyme
   degradation**. That instability is why a replication primer made of RNA is sensible to build and
   then destroy.
