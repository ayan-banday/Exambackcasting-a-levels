# PART 1. GENE MUTATION (LO 2k)

## A. A mutation is a change in the DNA base sequence.

**A1. The definition.** A mutation is a **permanent change in the DNA base sequence** of a cell.
Whether it changes the phenotype, and whether it is passed on, are separate questions; neither
belongs in the definition. Two scales, and the split is the shape of LO 2(k): **a single or a few
nucleotide pairs**, gene mutation, and **large regions of a chromosome**, chromosomal aberration.

**A2. Germline against somatic decides who is affected.** Either half is a one-mark answer.

| | **Germline mutation** | **Somatic mutation** |
|---|---|---|
| Where | **germline cells**, meaning germ cells and gametes | **somatic cells** |
| Inherited? | **may be transmitted to the offspring and to successive generations** | **not inherited by the progeny** |
| Consequence | if adverse, the mutant condition is a **genetic disorder** or **hereditary disease** | affects only that individual, and only the clone descended from the mutated cell |

**A3. Gene mutations are also called point mutations:** **chemical changes that affect the DNA
sequence of just one gene**, that is **changes at specific sites in a gene resulting in a change in
one or a few bases**. Two kinds, and §B and §C are built on the split: **nucleotide substitution**,
**the replacement of one nucleotide pair with another**, and **nucleotide insertion or deletion**.

---

## B. A substitution changes at most one amino acid.

It alters one codon, so at most one amino acid. Which of four outcomes you get turns on two questions
asked in order. **Did the amino acid change?** Silent, no; the other three, yes. **Then did the
function change?** Neutral, no; missense and nonsense, yes. Getting silent and neutral the wrong way
round surrenders a free mark.

| Outcome | What happens to the codon | Amino acid sequence | 3D conformation | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Silent** | changes, but **the same amino acid is inserted because of the degeneracy of the genetic code** | unchanged | unchanged | **not altered** |
| **Neutral** | changes, **and the amino acid translated changes** | changed | **no change in overall conformation** | **not altered** |
| **Missense** | changes so **a different amino acid is translated** | changed | **changed** | **altered** |
| **Nonsense** | **a codon for an amino acid is changed into a stop codon**, causing **premature termination of translation** | **shortened, that is truncated** | changed | **altered**; **nearly all nonsense mutations result in non-functional proteins** |

**B1. Why degeneracy allows a silent mutation.** From Cluster 2: **64 codons for 20 amino acids**, so
most amino acids have several, and the redundancy sits **mostly at the third base**. A substitution
there very often gives a synonymous codon. **Only methionine (AUG) and tryptophan (UGG) have a single
codon each**, so those two have no silent option.

**B2. Two named routes to a neutral mutation:** **substitution of the original amino acid with an
amino acid of similar physical and chemical properties**, or **substitution of an amino acid residue
that is non-essential to that protein's structure and function**.

---

## C. For an insertion or deletion, the reading frame decides everything.

**C1. A multiple of three keeps the frame.** Addition or deletion **in multiples of 3** adds or
removes **whole amino acids**, one per three bases; if the added codon is a stop codon, translation
terminates prematurely. The frame survives, so **only the local region changes**. Conformation and
function may change, but the protein is not automatically wrecked.

> **The printed notes get this wrong in three places.** Chapter 2.5 calls in-frame indels missense
> and nonsense, on page 5, in Table 1 and in the summary map, contradicting its own definitions on
> the facing page where both are outcomes of a **nucleotide substitution**. An in-frame indel
> substitutes nothing and changes no amino acid into a different one; it adds or removes one.
> Describe what it does, never the label the notes attach to it.

**C2. Anything else shifts the frame.** The code is **triplet and non-overlapping** and the frame is
**set by the start codon AUG**. Because the mRNA is **read as a series of non-overlapping codons**,
an insertion or deletion **not in multiples of threes will result in a frameshift mutation**, so
**all the nucleotides downstream of the site will be improperly grouped into codons, resulting in
extensive missense**. It may also generate **a new, premature stop codon**, or cause **read-through
of the normal stop codon, resulting in polypeptides of altered lengths**. A frameshift **usually
results in a non-functional protein**. Nothing upstream of the site changes.

**C3. The consequence chain always starts at the gene.** This is the heaviest trap in Book 2. At
least one mark scheme refuses an answer that begins at the amino acid, because starting there assumes
the step you were asked to explain. The order is fixed:

**base sequence → amino acid sequence → 3D conformation → protein function**

For an indel, every arrow a mark:

**insertion or deletion of n bases → n not a multiple of 3 → all downstream nucleotides improperly grouped into codons → extensive missense, so the subsequent amino acid sequence is changed, and/or a new premature stop → primary structure changed → R groups in different positions → different hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds → changed tertiary conformation → active site or binding site no longer complementary → non-functional protein**

Change the first link and the same spine carries every mutation question in the Book.

> **Two symmetrical traps.** A single substitution cannot shift the frame, because no base is added
> or removed. And a three-base deletion does not frameshift at all.

# PART 2. FROM MUTATION TO DISEASE (LO 2l)

## D. Sickle cell anaemia is one chain from gene to symptom.

The only disease LO 2(l) names, so it is guaranteed. Learn it as a chain, and start at the gene.

1. **The gene:** a mutation in the **β-globin gene**, which encodes **one of the polypeptide subunits
   that make up haemoglobin**.
2. **The base change: substitution of a thymine for an adenine at one position in the Hb gene
   (template strand)**, giving a **missense mutation**. Schemes also accept **CTT to CAT** in the DNA
   triplet or **GAG to GUG** in the mRNA codon. Give one, exactly.
3. **The amino acid:** the **sixth amino acid residue in the polypeptide is changed from a glutamate
   (hydrophilic) to a valine (hydrophobic)**.
4. **The protein:** the **specific three-dimensional conformation and function of the Hb protein is
   altered**, because the changed primary structure puts different R groups in play. Glutamate's
   charged R group made **ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds**; valine's non-polar R group makes
   **hydrophobic interactions**.
5. **The exposed patch:** a **hydrophobic spot on the outside of the Hb protein that sticks to the
   hydrophobic region of an adjacent Hb protein's beta chain**.
6. **Polymerisation, and its trigger:** subunits stick together **when the oxygen concentration is
   low, particularly when the red blood cells are in capillaries and veins**, and the **aggregated
   proteins form fibre-like structures within red blood cells**. It reverses, since **at high oxygen
   concentration, haemoglobin resumes globular haemoglobin structure**, which is why symptoms are
   episodic.
7. **The cell:** those fibres make red blood cells **lose their normal morphology and become
   sickle-shaped**.
8. **The physiology, two separate effects:** sickled cells are **less able to move through
   capillaries and can block blood flow, resulting in severe pain and cell death of the surrounding
   tissue due to shortage in oxygen**; and they are **fragile and easily destroyed, further
   decreasing the oxygen carrying capacity of blood**.

**D1. The cross-book link.** Haemoglobin is Book 1's **quaternary structure** example, and the
**number and type of subunits is unchanged** here. What changes is the **surface chemistry of the β
subunit**, which is why HbS molecules stick to each other rather than falling apart.

**D2. The heterozygote.** In malarial regions **HbAHbS heterozygotes have higher reproductive success
than either homozygote**: **HbSHbS individuals die of sickle cell anaemia**, **HbAHbA individuals die
of malaria**, because **the malarial parasite can only infect normal red blood cells and not
sickle-shaped ones**. Harmful to the individual, advantageous to the population. Say both, and say
which level you are on.

> **Over-claiming scores nothing.** The marks are Glu to Val, hydrophilic to hydrophobic,
> polymerisation at low oxygen, and cell shape.

# PART 3. CHROMOSOMAL ABERRATION (LO 2k)

## E. Chromosomal aberration has two halves, and the definition needs both.

It is **1. a change in the structure of a chromosome (involving several gene loci)** or **2. a change
in the number of chromosomes**. Structural is §F, numerical is §G.

---

## F. Four structural aberrations, and only one loses genetic material.

| Type | What happens | Genetic consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Deletion** | a chromosome **breaks in one or more places, and a portion of it is lost**, either **terminal** or **intercalary** | the **genotype is altered due to the absence of certain gene loci**. Affecting the same loci **on both homologous chromosomes is usually lethal**. If **only one of a homologous pair is affected**, **alleles on the non-deficient homologue will be expressed, even if recessive** |
| **Duplication** | **any part of the genetic material, be it a single locus or a large piece of chromosome, is present more than once in the genome** | **gene redundancy**, **phenotypic variation**, **an important source of genetic variation during evolution** |
| **Inversion** | a **segment is turned around 180º within a chromosome**, needing **two breaks** and **reinsertion of the inverted segment** | **no loss of genetic information, but simply rearranges the linear sequence**. May cause **position effects**; heterozygotes may produce **aberrant gametes** |
| **Translocation** | the **movement of a chromosomal segment to a new location in the genome**; **reciprocal translocation involves the exchange of segments between two non-homologous chromosomes** | information is **not lost or gained**, only **rearranged**, so it **does not directly alter the viability of individuals bearing it**. May also produce a **position effect** |

**F1. Two clauses almost nobody writes.** The deletion row's last clause is the discriminating mark:
a deletion on one homologue can **unmask a recessive allele** on the surviving one. And
**non-homologous** is load-bearing in the translocation definition, since exchange between homologues
is crossing over, which is normal meiosis.

**F2. Duplications arise** from **unequal crossing over between synapsed chromosomes during
meiosis**, which yields a duplication and a deletion in one event, or from **a replication error
prior to meiosis**.

**F3. How inversions and translocations harm when nothing is lost.** Three routes, and a *suggest*
question wants them: a **position effect**, since a gene moved beside different regulatory sequences
is transcribed differently; **a break falling inside a gene**, destroying it; and **unbalanced
gametes**, since a rearranged chromosome cannot pair normally, so some gametes get a duplication and
others a deletion.

---

## G. Numerical aberration comes from non-disjunction.

**G1. Non-disjunction** is the **failure of chromosomes or chromatids to disjoin and move to opposite
poles during division**; in meiosis the **normal distribution of chromosomes into gametes is
disrupted**. Diagram questions turn on telling the two versions apart.

| | **Non-disjunction in meiosis I** | **Non-disjunction in meiosis II** |
|---|---|---|
| What fails to separate | **homologous chromosomes** | **sister chromatids** |
| Gametes | **n+1, n+1, n−1, n−1**, all abnormal | **n+1, n−1, n, n**, two abnormal |
| The n+1 gamete contains | **both homologues**, so **both parental alleles** | **two copies of one chromatid**, so the **same allele twice** |

That last row is how a paper makes you deduce which division failed from a child's genotype alone.

**G2. Aneuploidy** is **losing or gaining one or more chromosomes, but not a complete set**.
**Monosomy** is **the loss of a single chromosome from an otherwise diploid genome** (2n−1);
**trisomy** is **the gain of one chromosome** (2n+1). Contrast **euploidy**, **multiples of the
haploid chromosome set**, in one line only: LO 2(k) names aneuploidy alone.

> **Naming trap, from an examiner's comment.** The consequence of non-disjunction is **aneuploidy**.
> Down syndrome is refused unless the stem fixes which aneuploidy, and polyploidy is refused
> outright, because non-disjunction gives an unequal distribution rather than a whole extra set.

**G3. Down syndrome, trisomy 21**, is named in LO 2(k) and again in LO 2(m). It is **the most common
chromosomal abnormality syndrome in humans** and **the only human autosomal trisomy in which a
significant number of individuals survive longer than a year past birth**; **45,X Turner syndrome**
is the only tolerated monosomy. It occurs when there is an **additional chromosome for the 21st pair
of chromosomes**, and **non-disjunction accounts for 95% of cases, the other 5% caused by
translocation, mosaicism or partial trisomy**. That split bridges back to §F: a translocation
attaching most of chromosome 21 onto another chromosome delivers a third copy of those loci without
a third free chromosome 21 in the count.

# PART 4. THE THREE MOLECULAR TECHNIQUES (LO 2j)

> LO 2(j) names **exactly three**: **(i) polymerase chain reaction, including its advantages and
> limitations, (ii) gel electrophoresis, (iii) Southern blotting and nucleic acid hybridisation.**
> Nothing else in chapter 2.6 is examinable. §K lists what to skip.

## H. PCR amplifies a known sequence in a tube.

**H1. What it is:** a technique that can **amplify a specific region of DNA from a trace sample**,
carried out **in vitro**, meaning **heat is used to separate the two DNA strands**. It is
**cell-free**, uses a **thermostable DNA polymerase**, and requires that **at least part of the
sequence of the DNA sample must be known**.

**H2. Five components.** The mark is for the job, not the name.

| Component | Why it is there |
|---|---|
| **DNA template**, genomic or **cDNA reverse-transcribed from RNA** | supplies the strands to be copied |
| **PCR primers**, **two sets of short, single-stranded oligonucleotides about 20 bases in length** | **specific to the sequence of interest**; they **flank the sequence of interest and are complementary to the 3' end of both template strands**, and supply the **free 3ʹ-OH** the polymerase extends from |
| **Free dNTPs** in excess | raw material; the **pyrophosphate released supplies the energy** |
| **Taq polymerase from *Thermus aquaticus*** | **stable at high temperatures and not denatured by repeated heat treatments**, so it need not be replaced each cycle |
| **Reaction buffer** with **MgCl₂** | **Mg²⁺ ions function as the cofactors required for DNA polymerase activity** |

**H3. Three steps, and the temperatures carry marks.** **Denaturation of DNA template, 90–100 °C**
(95 °C, 30 s): the **hydrogen bonds holding the two strands together are broken**, giving
**single-stranded DNA**. **Annealing of primers, 50–65 °C** (54 °C, 1 min, with a large excess of
both primer sets): primers **anneal specifically to complementary sequences at the 3' end of
single-stranded DNA templates via hydrogen bonds**, and the **optimum annealing temperature depends
on primer length and base composition**. **Extension of primers, 60–75 °C** (about 72 °C, 2 min, near
the optimum for Taq): DNA **downstream of each of the two primers is extended in the 5' to 3'
direction**. All of it in a **programmable thermal cycler**, **repeated 20–30 times**.

**H4. Chain reaction and specificity.** It is a chain reaction because **newly-synthesised DNA strands
serve as templates for DNA synthesis in subsequent cycles**, so the predominant species soon becomes
**the sequence flanked by and including the two primers**; specific because **primers are specific
and do not attach elsewhere**. Amplification is **2ⁿ**, so after **30 cycles** there are
**2³⁰ ≈ 1.07 × 10⁹** molecules and **more than 99.99% are exact copies**.

**H5. Three advantages,** named in the outcome: **sensitivity**, since it **can amplify sequences
from trace amounts of DNA** down to a single cell; **speed and ease of automation**, since one cycle
takes **less than 5 minutes** and **20–30 cycles takes only 2–3 hours** in a single tube; and
**robustness**, since it amplifies from material in which the **DNA is badly degraded or embedded in
a medium** that defeats conventional isolation.

**H6. Five limitations,** the half candidates skip. **Contamination risk**, because **the extreme
sensitivity of PCR means any contamination by non-template nucleic acids in the environment could be
easily amplified**, giving a false result. **Infidelity of in-vitro replication**, because **Taq
polymerase lacks 3' to 5' exonuclease activity**, so **no proofreading**, an error rate of about **1
in 10 000 bases**, and an early error amplified in every later cycle. **Limited product size**,
efficient only over roughly **0.1–5 kb**. **Prior sequence information is required** to design the
primers. And it **applies only to nucleic acids, not proteins**. Sensitivity is advantage one and
limitation one, the same fact from both ends, so writing it as a trade-off covers both halves of the
outcome in one sentence.

> **PCR needs no cell.** It is **in vitro** and **cell-free**, so an answer describing a host cell is
> answering a different question.

---

## I. Gel electrophoresis separates DNA by size.

**I1. The principle, four links.** Electrophoresis **separates charged molecules based on their
different rates of movement in an electric field**. The **phosphate groups of the sugar-phosphate
backbone in DNA are negatively-charged, so DNA moves towards the positive electrode, the anode**. DNA
has a **constant charge density, that is charge per unit of mass**, so charge cancels out of the
comparison and fragments separate **primarily on the basis of size**. The **complex network of pores
in the gel matrix acts as a molecular sieve**, so **shorter DNA fragments are less impeded by the
pores than longer ones and move through the gel more quickly**, and different lengths **migrate as
distinct bands**. Constant charge density is the whole answer to why a gel sorts by size and not by
charge, and an answer without that phrase is incomplete.

Two conditions get dropped and should not be: **DNA to be separated by electrophoresis should be
linear**, and the gel suits the job, with **agarose** more porous, separating **0.5–20 kb**, while
**polyacrylamide** has smaller pores and resolves fragments **differing in length by just a single
nucleotide**.

**I2. The run, five steps.** Cast the gel from agarose in buffer, poured into a tray with a **comb**.
Set the tray in a chamber with **enough buffer to cover the gel**. Load samples mixed with **loading
dye** into wells **near the negatively-charged cathode**, keeping one well for a **molecular weight
marker or DNA ladder**. Apply a **direct current** with the **anode at the opposite end from the
samples**, until the dye front has moved about **two-thirds of the gel**. Stain with **methylene
blue**, or **ethidium bromide, which intercalates DNA and fluoresces under UV**; radioactively
labelled DNA is read by **autoradiography** instead.

**Loading dye is bromophenol blue, xylene cyanol and glycerol, and does three jobs:** it **makes the
DNA sample visible for easier loading**; **bromophenol blue migrates slightly faster than DNA and
xylene cyanol slightly slower**, bracketing the DNA so migration can be monitored; and **glycerol
increases the density of the sample so it sinks to the bottom of the well**. The **DNA ladder
contains fragments of known length, which allows the size of the bands to be determined**.

**I3. What a gel cannot tell you.** It gives **the sizes present and the number of distinct
fragments**, never the sequence. Any mutation that does not change the product's length, meaning any
substitution or a single-base indel inside a long product, leaves the band where it was and is
**invisible on a gel**. Gels run under different conditions are **not directly comparable**, since
percentage, voltage and run time all differ.

> **The reversed-migration trap.** DNA is negative, so it runs **towards the anode**, and **small
> fragments end up furthest from the well**.

## J. Hybridisation finds one sequence among thousands.

**J1. Nucleic acid hybridisation** is **the process by which two complementary, single-stranded
nucleic acid chains base-pair and reform a double-stranded hybrid**. Reaching single strands needs
conditions that disrupt the hydrogen bonds: **heating to about 100 °C, high pH of 13 or above, or
very low salt concentrations**. Renaturation needs **a prolonged period at a lower temperature of
65 °C**. Hybrids can be **DNA/DNA, RNA/RNA or DNA/RNA**, and **the greater the complementarity
between the two single-stranded molecules, the more favourable the hybridisation reaction**.

**J2. Probes** are **single-stranded DNA or RNA ranging from 15 to several thousands of nucleotides
long**, which **carry radioactive, fluorescent or chemical markers to facilitate detection**. They
are **sensitive**, since **complementary sequences present at a concentration as low as 1 molecule
per cell can be detected**, and **selective**, since a probe **hybridises only to nucleic acid
molecules carrying all or part of the complementary sequence**.

**J3. Stringency is set by temperature.** Sequences **do not need to be perfectly complementary to be
able to hybridise**, and **stringency can be controlled by varying the incubation temperature**.
**Higher temperature, 42 °C, is more stringent**, sitting just below the melting point of a perfect
helix so **all imperfect helices are unstable** and only the identical match survives. **Lower
temperature, 35 °C, is less stringent**, so **imperfectly paired double helices can form** and the
probe also finds **related but non-identical sequences**. The temperature chooses the question: is
this exact gene here, or is anything like it here.

**J4. Primer against probe.** **Both are short, single-stranded oligonucleotides which bind to the
DNA via complementary base-pairing.** A **primer anneals to a specific sequence on the DNA template
and provides a free 3'-OH group for DNA polymerase to add nucleotides**, so it belongs to
replication; a **probe allows the detection of target DNA from a sample** and is **structurally
modified, that is labelled**, so it can be seen.

**J5. Southern blotting, four steps.**

1. **Agarose gel electrophoresis:** the DNA is **separated on the basis of size**.
2. **Blotting:** the gel sits on a **paper wick absorbing buffer from a reservoir**, with a
   **nitrocellulose or nylon membrane** over it and blotting paper stacked on top, so **capillary
   action draws buffer through the gel and membrane**, transferring the DNA as **a replica of the
   bands**. The omitted step lives here: **the double-stranded DNA must be denatured to its
   single-stranded form before hybridisation with the probe, either prior to or during blotting, by
   exposing the gel to alkaline denaturing conditions.**
3. **Incubation with the labelled probe**, sealed in a bag with buffered salt solution, where the
   **labelled single-stranded DNA or RNA probe hybridises with the DNA of interest**.
4. **Detection:** the membrane is **washed to remove unbound probe**, then read by
   **autoradiography** or chemical or fluorescent detection. **The size of the DNA that hybridises
   with the probe is determined by reference to the molecular weight markers electrophoresed
   alongside.**

Southern blotting **uses a DNA sample and allows the experimenter to detect the presence of a
specific gene or a specific DNA sequence**.

**J6. Why the blot shows fewer bands than the gel.** Only bands that **contain a sequence
complementary to the probe** hybridise, so only those are visualised. Every other fragment is still
on the membrane, simply unlabelled.

**J7. The three techniques are one workflow.** Amplify it with PCR, sort it by size on a gel,
identify the fragment you want by blotting and probing.

# PART 5. BIOETHICS OF MATERNAL GENETIC SCREENING (LO 2m)

## L. One outcome: four principles, one framework, one application.

**L1. What screening is.** **Genetic screening is the analysis of a person's DNA to check for alleles
associated with disease**, applied to **people who are free of the symptoms**. Maternal screening
tests **provide information about the health of a foetus** but **do not diagnose. They determine
whether the foetus is at an increased or decreased risk** of an abnormality. A **diagnostic test is
only performed when a screening test is abnormal**, and diagnostic tests are **more invasive and may
pose a higher risk to the pregnant woman and the foetus**. Screening is non-invasive: carrier
screening, cell-free foetal DNA screening (NIPT), serum protein markers. Diagnosis is invasive:
**amniocentesis** and **chorionic villus sampling**. That two-step structure carries most of the
ethics, because a screen gives a probability and turning it into an answer costs risk.

**L2. The four principles.** Name the one you are applying; the mark comes from a specific mechanism
attached to a named party, not from a moral label.

- **Respect for persons**, built on **autonomy**, the **right to self-determination**, the source of
  **informed consent, privacy and confidentiality**. Counselling and refusal without coercion.
- **Maximising benefits and minimising harms**: **beneficence**, which **requires positive action**,
  and **non-maleficence**, doing no harm. Preparation and early intervention against false positives,
  distress and procedural risk.
- **Justice**, **giving to each that which is his due**, so **resources, risks and costs should be
  distributed equitably**. Unequal access is a justice argument.
- **Care**, **the maintenance of healthy, caring relationships between individuals and within a
  community**, which unlocks the argument that screening marginalises people living with the
  condition.

**L2b. The eight considerations your notes list, each with the specific point that earns the mark.**
A principle named alone scores nothing. These are the mechanisms you attach to it.

| Issue | The specific point |
|---|---|
| **Autonomy and informed consent** | **Pre-test counselling** must cover purpose, process, implications and what follows a positive result; she must be able to **accept or reject without coercion** |
| **Clinical** | Screening is **not 100% accurate**. A **false positive** causes psychological distress and pushes her toward **more invasive, riskier confirmatory tests**; a **false negative** gives false reassurance |
| **Degree of severity** | Down syndrome impairments **vary greatly**, and screening **cannot predict the extent** of disability or the specific challenges the child will face |
| **Termination of pregnancy** | Abortion is a likely outcome, raising the **moral status of the foetus** against the **rights of the parents**; a falling Down syndrome population raises **eugenics** concerns |
| **Non-maleficence** | Negative psychological impact, sharpened when the condition has **no cure or effective treatment**; **amniocentesis and CVS carry real risk** to foetus and mother |
| **Beneficence** | An informed decision, and the chance to **prepare for early intervention** and resources, potentially improving the child's outcome |
| **Justice and equity** | Screening may not be **available to all socioeconomic groups**, and unequal access **worsens existing health disparities** |
| **Social implications** | **Disability advocates** argue screening **devalues** lives lived with the condition and **marginalises** those already living with it |

**L3. The framework:** **Ethical Question → Facts → Stakeholders → Ethical Principles → Alternate
Options and Rebuttals → Decision and Justification.** LO 2(m) says *discuss*, so a list of
considerations with no judgement caps however long the list.

**L4. What separates a top answer.** Name the stakeholders separately: the **pregnant woman**, her
**partner**, the **foetus**, the **healthcare system**, and **people already living with the
condition**. Take the strongest version of each side: for screening, beneficence through preparation;
against it, that a probabilistic result forces a decision under uncertainty while the confirmatory
test that resolves it risks the pregnancy. Then land. Screening should be **offered rather than
routine**, conditional on **pre-test counselling**, **consent without coercion** and **equitable
access**, since the test that serves autonomy when freely chosen undermines it as a default.

## N. Gaps and cautions. Read before drilling.

**N1. The bank has no maternal-screening bioethics question at all.** Fifteen years of prelims
contain nothing on amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling, NIPT, informed consent or the trisomy-21
debate. The nearest is preimplantation diagnosis of IVF embryos, a different decision: discarding a
blastocyst, not terminating a pregnancy, and no miscarriage risk. Build the answer from §L yourself.

**N2. Inversion is untested in the entire bank.** Deletion, duplication and translocation all have
real questions; inversion appears only inside mark-scheme lists for other questions. Expect it cold.

**N3. Two errors in the corpus you will meet.** One prelim stem calls sickle cell anaemia a somatic
mutation. It is inherited, so the mutation is **germline and present in every cell from the zygote
onwards**; the question after it is sound, the premise is not. And one school paper calls printed
sequences the **non-template** strand in the question and the **template** strand in the answer
booklet, so check which strand you have before transcribing.

**N4. Meiosis and cancer are both Book 3.** Non-disjunction is taught here because LO 2(k) needs it,
but the stages themselves are LOs 2(n) and 2(s), and p53, ras and proto-oncogenes belong to LOs 2(p)
to 2(r). Both turn up in mark schemes here because those essays were set to candidates who had
already covered them.

**N5. Where the lexicon is strictest.** *Trisomy 21*, not an extra chromosome. *Taq polymerase*, not
a heat-stable polymerase. *Aneuploidy*, not Down syndrome, as the consequence of non-disjunction.
*Non-homologous* in the translocation definition. *Glutamate to valine*, with **hydrophilic** and
**hydrophobic** attached. *Anode*, and *constant charge density*.
