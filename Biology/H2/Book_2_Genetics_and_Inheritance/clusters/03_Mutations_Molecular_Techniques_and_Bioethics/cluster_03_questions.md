# Mark Schemes

### Question 1

**(a)** *Which of a single-base substitution and a single-base insertion is more likely to leave a
working protein.* **[12]**

**1.** The **substitution** is the more likely of the two to leave a working protein, because a
substitution **replaces one nucleotide pair with another** and so **changes at most one codon and at
most one amino acid**, whereas an insertion of one base changes **every codon after it**.

*The four outcomes of the substitution, max 5:*

**2.** **Silent** — the **codon is changed, but the same amino acid is inserted into the protein
because of the degeneracy of the genetic code**, so the amino acid sequence is **unchanged**, there
is **no change in conformation** and the **function is not altered**. There are **64 codons for 20
amino acids** and the redundancy sits **mostly at the third base of the codon**, so a change at the
third position very often gives a synonymous codon. [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 2, and note that
**methionine (AUG) and tryptophan (UGG) have one codon each**, so at those two positions there is no
silent option]

**3.** **Neutral** — the substitution **changes the mRNA codon and the amino acid translated, however
the resulting amino acid substitution produces no detectable change in the function of the protein**.
Two named routes: **substitution of the original amino acid with an amino acid of similar physical
and chemical properties**, or **substitution of an amino acid residue that is non-essential to that
protein's structure and function**.

**4.** **Missense** — a **different amino acid is translated**, so the **amino acid sequence of the
polypeptide is changed, resulting in a change in the specific 3-dimensional conformation of the
protein**, and hence the **function of the protein is altered**.

**5.** **Nonsense** — a **codon for an amino acid is changed into a stop codon, leading to the
premature termination of translation**. The resulting polypeptide is **shorter (truncated)** than
normal, and **nearly all nonsense mutations result in non-functional proteins**.

**6.** So **three of the four possible outcomes of a substitution leave a working protein** and only
one of them reliably does not. That is the whole basis of the prediction.

*The insertion, max 6:*

**7.** **One base is not a multiple of three**, and the resulting mRNA is **read as a series of
non-overlapping codons**, so an insertion **not in multiples of three results in a frameshift
mutation**. The code is **triplet and non-overlapping** and the **reading frame is set by the start
codon AUG**. [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 2]

**8.** **All the nucleotides downstream of the insertion site are improperly grouped into codons,
resulting in extensive missense** — the amino acid sequence from that point on is completely altered,
not altered at one residue.

**9.** The frameshift **may also cause a new, premature stop codon to be generated in the reading
frame**, or a **read-through of the normal stop codon, resulting in polypeptides of altered
lengths**. **In any case, a frameshift usually results in a non-functional protein.**

**10.** **Nothing upstream of the site changes**, so position decides how much is lost. The change
sits at **codon 12 of 400**, so roughly **97% of the polypeptide lies downstream** and is rewritten.
The same insertion at codon 395 would cost far less.

**11.** The chain to loss of function has to be written out, and it is the same chain either way:
**primary structure changed → R groups in different positions → different hydrogen bonds, ionic
bonds, hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds → changed tertiary conformation → active site or
binding site no longer complementary in shape to the substrate → non-functional protein.**
[CROSS-BOOK — Book 1]

**12.** Judgement, and it must be conditional rather than absolute: the substitution is **more likely
but not certain** to leave function, because it can still be **missense at a catalytic or binding
residue, or nonsense**; the insertion is **very unlikely** to leave function at this position.

R: calling a substitution a frameshift — **no base has been added or removed, so the frame cannot
shift**. R: treating every insertion or deletion as catastrophic without applying the multiple-of-
three test. R: "the gene is faulty so the protein doesn't work", which skips every marking point.

**(b)** *What follows from a mutation present in a tumour but not in blood or sperm.* **[4]**

**1.** The change is present only in a **somatic cell** and its descendants, so it is a **somatic
mutation**, not a germline one.

**2.** Somatic mutations occur **in somatic cells** and are **not inherited by the progeny**, so
**none of his children can inherit it** and it is **lost on the death of the organism**.

**3.** It affects **only the individual, and only the clone of cells descended from the mutated
cell** — which is what the tumour is.

**4.** Contrast, for the second mark: a **germline mutation occurs in germline cells (germ cells and
gametes)** and **may be transmitted to the offspring and to successive generations**; **when a
mutated gamete fuses with another gamete the resulting zygote contains the mutation**, and **mitosis
then transmits it to every cell of the new individual**. If adverse, the mutant condition is a
**genetic disorder / hereditary disease**.

chain from changed R groups through 3D configuration to a non-complementary active site, verbatim
from the key, which runs it on DNA polymerase III); School Prelim 2023 P2 Q4b and HCI Prelim 2023 P2
Q4b, reprinted as Topical 2019 STQ Q4d(ii) from EJC Prelim 2020 P2 Q1(d)(ii) (a — the eight-point
frameshift-to-non-functional-enzyme key and the shorter EJC version, verbatim); Topical 2019 STQ
Q4d(i), from EJC Prelim 2020 P2 Q1(d)(i) [2] (a — missense against nonsense, verbatim, including
*"Reject references to degeneracy of genetic code"* as a *distinguishing* feature); TPE 2024 P3
Q1a(ii) and TPE 2025 P3 Q1a(ii) [2] (a — why a substitution leaves the downstream amino acids alone,
verbatim); SAJC Prelim 2021 P1 Q7, key B (a — the minimum-and-maximum reasoning that a base change

**Archetype:** mechanism explain-chain, run as a prediction between two named alternatives.
**Trap:** the symmetrical pair. Claiming a single substitution shifts the reading frame — it cannot,
because no base is added or removed. And treating every indel as catastrophic, when a 3-base deletion
does not frameshift at all. Second trap: offering **degeneracy** as the difference between missense
and nonsense, which is an explicit reject; degeneracy is why a *silent* mutation exists.
**LOs:** 2(k) gene mutation, with substitution, addition and deletion including frameshift.
Reach-back 2(c) translation and the genetic code, Core Idea 1 protein structure.
**Concepts fused:** (1) substitution defined as replacement of one nucleotide pair; (2) the four
substitution outcomes discriminated by two questions, did the amino acid change and did the function
change; (3) degeneracy of the genetic code and where the redundancy sits; (4) the two amino acids
with a single codon; (5) the two named routes to a neutral mutation; (6) premature stop codons and
truncation; (7) the multiple-of-three test as the thing that decides everything about an indel;
(8) triplet, non-overlapping code and the frame set by AUG; (9) extensive missense downstream against
a single changed residue; (10) read-through of a normal stop codon as the other frameshift outcome;
(11) position within the gene as a quantitative determinant of damage; (12) primary to tertiary to
active site as the loss-of-function chain, reached back to Book 1; (13) germline against somatic and
what each means for inheritance.

### Question 2

**(a)** *Account for the difference between the two cells in Fig. 2.1, and what has not changed.*
**[12]**

*The chain, in order, max 9:*

**1.** The gene involved is the **β-globin gene, which encodes one of the polypeptide subunits that
make up haemoglobin (Hb)**. The answer must start here — a scheme in this Book states *"Reject
marking points 1 and 2 if the answer just states 'at the 12th amino acid position' without the idea
that it occurred in the DNA / gene."*

**2.** There is a **substitution of a thymine for an adenine at one position in the Hb gene (template
strand)**, which results in a **missense mutation**. Quote one of the strings exactly: DNA triplet
**CTT → CAT**, or mRNA codon **GAG → GUG**.

**3.** The **sixth amino acid residue in the polypeptide is changed from a glutamate (hydrophilic) to
a valine (hydrophobic)**. Both halves of that are needed; *glutamate to valine* on its own is half a
point.

**4.** The **specific three-dimensional conformation and function of the Hb protein is altered**,
because the **primary structure changed**, so the **R groups form different interactions** — where
glutamate's charged R group made **ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds**, valine's non-polar R group makes
**hydrophobic interactions**. [CROSS-BOOK — Book 1]

**5.** The substitution **creates a hydrophobic spot on the outside of the Hb protein that sticks to
the hydrophobic region of an adjacent Hb protein's beta chain**.

**6.** The mutant Hb subunits **stick to one another when the oxygen concentration is low,
particularly when the red blood cells are in capillaries and veins**, so **HbS is less soluble at low
oxygen concentrations** and the molecules **polymerise / crystallise**. The **aggregated proteins
form fibre-like structures within red blood cells**. It is **reversible** — at high oxygen
concentration the haemoglobin **resumes globular structure**.

**7.** The **fibre-like structures cause the red blood cells to lose their normal morphology and
become sickle-shaped**, which is the difference visible in Fig. 2.1: the unaffected cell is a
**smooth biconcave disc**, the affected one a **rigid crescent with pointed ends**.

**8.** Two separate physiological consequences, both wanted: **sickled cells are less able to move
through capillaries and can block blood flow, resulting in severe pain and cell death of the
surrounding tissue due to shortage in oxygen**; and the **sickled red blood cells are also fragile
and easily destroyed, further decreasing the oxygen carrying capacity of blood**.

**9.** The mutation is **inherited, so it is germline and present in every cell from the zygote
onwards** — it is not something the red cell acquired. Note that one prelim stem in the papers opens
"Sickle cell anaemia is caused by a somatic mutation"; that premise is wrong.

*What has not changed, max 3:*

**10.** The **number and type of subunits is unchanged**. Haemoglobin is still the Book 1 worked
example of **quaternary structure**: **four polypeptide subunits, two α and two β**, each associated
with a **haem prosthetic group** holding an Fe²⁺ ion. [CROSS-BOOK — Book 1]

**11.** The **molecular size is unchanged** — HbA and HbS have the **same molecular mass**, and only
**one amino acid in one of the four subunits** differs, so the **primary structure is 99.8% the
same**.

**12.** What changed is the **surface chemistry of the β subunit**, which is exactly why HbS
molecules **stick to each other** rather than falling apart. A whole-molecule change would destroy
haemoglobin; a surface change makes it aggregate.

R: "the gene is faulty so the haemoglobin doesn't work." R: starting the chain at the amino acid.
R: saying valine, or the red blood cell, or the polypeptide chain, polymerises — it is the **HbS
molecules** that polymerise.

**(b)** *Why the allele is common where malaria is endemic, and whether it is harmful or
advantageous.* **[6]**

**1.** In malarial regions **HbAHbS heterozygotes have a higher reproductive success than either
homozygote**, so the allele is **selected for** and persists at high frequency despite being lethal
in the homozygote.

**2.** **HbSHbS homozygotes die of sickle cell anaemia**, because **sickle-shaped red blood cells
obstruct blood vessels and deprive multiple organs of oxygen, resulting in organ damage**, and
because they are **more rigid and fragile, haemolysing readily**.

**3.** **HbAHbA homozygotes die of malaria**, because **the malarial parasite (Plasmodium) can only
infect normal red blood cells and not sickle-shaped ones**.

**4.** The heterozygote **makes both haemoglobins**, so it has **enough normal HbA for adequate oxygen
transport** and **enough HbS for the parasite to be at a disadvantage**, and it therefore **survives
and reproduces where both homozygotes do not**.

**5.** The judgement has to name the level. At the level of the **individual homozygote** the mutation
is **harmful** — it produces a painful, life-shortening disease. At the level of the **population
under a malarial selection pressure** it is **advantageous**, because it raises mean fitness and
**changes allele frequency over time**.

**6.** So "harmful or advantageous" is not answerable without saying **which level and which selection
pressure**. Remove malaria from the environment and the same allele is purely deleterious — which is
why its frequency tracks the distribution of malaria and not the distribution of people.

form different interactions with other amino acids"*); MI Prelim 2023 P2 Q4a [4] (a — verbatim,

verbatim, with the reject on valine or the red blood cell polymerising); TMJC Prelim 2025 P2 Q6a [3]

NYJC Prelim 2025 P3 Q4a essay key (a and b). The figure is the school's own micrograph pair, 2.5

**Archetype:** mechanism explain-chain on the one disease the syllabus names, closed with a

**Trap:** starting the chain at the protein. The KRAS scheme in this Book rejects the first two

**(a)** *How PCR replaces the cell's replication proteins, and what it cannot escape.* **[10]**

*What replaces what, max 6:*

**1.** **Heat replaces helicase and the initiator proteins.** The amplification is carried out **in
vitro, i.e. heat is used to separate the 2 DNA strands**: at **90–100 °C (95 °C for about 30 s)** the
**hydrogen bonds holding together the two strands of the DNA template are broken**, so the template
is **denatured to single-stranded DNA**. In the cell that work is done by **helicase using ATP**, and
the origin is opened by **initiator proteins**. [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 1]

**2.** **The annealing temperature replaces the single-strand DNA-binding proteins.** Cooling to
**50–65 °C** is enough to let primers **anneal specifically to complementary sequences at the 3' end
of the single-stranded templates via hydrogen bonds**, but the temperature is **kept high enough that
the two long template strands do not re-anneal to each other**. In the cell, re-annealing is
prevented by **single-strand binding proteins**, which also protect the unstable ssDNA.

**3.** **Chemically synthesised DNA primers replace primase.** PCR uses **two sets of short,
single-stranded DNA primers, oligonucleotides about 20 bases long**, made artificially, whereas the
cell uses **RNA primers made by primase**. The distinction is a real examiner's point: **RNA primers
are only found in DNA replication; as synthetic primers are used during PCR there is no need to use
RNA primers, which would then have to be replaced with DNA**.

**4.** **Nothing replaces DNA polymerase I or ligase, because neither is needed.** In the cell, DNA
polymerase I **removes the RNA primer with 5'→3' exonuclease activity and replaces the
ribonucleotides with DNA nucleotides**, and **ligase seals the phosphodiester bond between Okazaki
fragments**. In PCR the primers are **DNA already** and are **kept in the product**, and each new
strand is **synthesised continuously in one piece from a single primer**, so **there are no Okazaki
fragments and no nicks to seal**.

**5.** **Nothing replaces topoisomerase, because the problem does not arise.** In the cell,
supercoiling builds ahead of a moving fork in a long, anchored, circular or packed molecule; in a
tube the template is **short, linear and already fully separated before synthesis begins**, so there
is **no fork travelling into unopened duplex** and no torque to relieve.

**6.** **Taq polymerase replaces the cell's DNA polymerase III**, and the buffer supplies **Mg²⁺ ions
as the cofactor required for DNA polymerase activity**. Taq is **thermostable and is not denatured by
repeated heat treatments in the PCR cycle**, so it need not be replaced each cycle.

*What cannot be escaped, max 4:*

**7.** **DNA synthesis cannot occur de novo.** **None of the DNA polymerases can initiate the
synthesis of a DNA strand on its own**, because the **shape of the active site is complementary to
the 5' phosphate of the incoming nucleotide and the 3'-OH group of the last nucleotide of the growing
strand**. [CROSS-BOOK — Book 1, active-site complementarity]

**8.** So a **primer is still required, purely to provide a free 3'-OH end that the polymerase can
extend**. That requirement is the one thing PCR keeps: it changes what the primer is made of and who
makes it, but it cannot do without one.

**9.** The other two constraints survive as well. **Elongation is still 5' to 3' only**, with the
**template read 3' to 5'**, and the **primer sequences flank the sequence of interest and are
complementary to the 3' end of both template strands**, which is why **two** primers are needed and
why they set the boundaries of the product.

**10.** Synthesis is still by **complementary base pairing**, with **phosphodiester bonds catalysed
between the free 3'-OH of the growing strand and the 5' phosphate of the incoming dNTP**, the
**incoming dNTP losing a pyrophosphate group whose bond breakage supplies the energy**.

R: describing transformation of a host cell, or any step involving a plasmid — PCR is **cell-free**.
R: "RNA primers" in a PCR answer.

**(b)** *Ranking the limitations of PCR against a 30 000-year-old bone.* **[6]**

**1.** The property that makes the experiment possible at all is **sensitivity together with
robustness**: PCR **can amplify sequences from trace amounts of DNA**, down to a single cell, and
**permits amplification of specific sequences from material in which the DNA is badly degraded or
embedded in a medium from which conventional DNA isolation is difficult**. A few hours of cycling
turns an unusable sample into enough DNA to sequence, because **the number of copies doubles every
cycle, giving 2ⁿ copies after n cycles**.

**2.** **First and worst, contamination.** **Due to the extreme sensitivity of PCR, any contamination
of the reaction by non-template nucleic acids in the environment could be easily amplified.** Here
the contaminant is **modern human DNA** — from the excavator, the museum, the laboratory — which is
**very similar in sequence to the target**, so it **anneals to the same primers** and the result may
be an amplified modern human sequence reported as Neanderthal. This is the single fact that is
**advantage 1 and limitation 1 at the same time**.

**3.** **Second, infidelity.** **Taq polymerase lacks 3' to 5' exonuclease activity**, so there is
**no proofreading**, and the error rate is about **1 in 10 000 bases**. It matters more than usual
here because an error made in an **early cycle is copied into every descendant molecule**, and
because the whole point of the experiment is to find the **small number of positions at which
Neanderthal differs from modern human** — a genuine variant and a polymerase error look identical in
the product. [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 1: DNA polymerase III **proofreads**, reversing by one base
pair and excising the mismatch, which is exactly the activity Taq is missing]

**4.** **Third, prior sequence information is required to design the primers**, and the only sequence
available is the **modern human** one. If the Neanderthal sequence differs **within a primer binding
site**, the primers are **no longer complementary, cannot anneal, and nothing is amplified** — a
false negative rather than a wrong answer. A difference *between* the two primer sites would still be
amplified.

**5.** **Fourth and least threatening, the limited product size.** PCR is efficient only over roughly
**0.1–5 kb**, but **ancient DNA is already fragmented into short pieces**, so short amplicons are
what the experiment wants anyway. The limitation and the sample happen to agree.

**6.** **Irrelevant here: PCR applies only to nucleic acids and not to proteins.** True, and it costs
this experiment nothing, since the target is mitochondrial DNA. Say so rather than listing it as a
threat — the ranking is the mark.

Also creditable: Taq is **stable at high temperatures and not denatured by repeated heat
treatments**, with a **half-life of about 40 minutes at 95 °C** against a **denaturation step lasting
about one minute**, so many cycles fit inside one half-life. [CROSS-BOOK — Book 1: thermostability is
a property of the bonds maintaining the tertiary structure, and **72 °C is Taq's optimum temperature**,
not merely a temperature it tolerates]

### Question 4

**(a)** *Account for the pattern in Fig. 4.1, and state what the gel cannot establish.* **[10]**

*Why the DNA moves, and why it separates, max 4:*

**1.** **The phosphate groups of the sugar-phosphate backbone in DNA are negatively-charged, so DNA
moves towards the positive electrode (anode)** once a **direct current** is applied. The arrow in
Fig. 4.1 points **down the gel**, so the **wells are at the top, at the negatively-charged cathode**,
and the anode is at the bottom. [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 1: the phosphates project outward from the
helix because they are hydrophilic, which is why the whole molecule carries a uniform surface charge]

**2.** **DNA molecules essentially have a constant charge density, i.e. charge per unit of mass**, so
**charge cancels out of the comparison between fragments** and they separate **primarily on the basis
of size**. This is the whole answer to "why size and not charge", and an answer without it is
incomplete.

**3.** **The complex network of pores in the gel matrix acts as a molecular sieve** that impedes
movement and separates the fragments **by length**. **Shorter DNA fragments are less impeded by the
pores than longer ones and move through the gel more quickly**, so fragments of different lengths
**migrate as distinct bands**.

**4.** The PCR products are **linear**, which they must be — *"DNA molecules to be separated by
electrophoresis should be linear"* — and **agarose** is the appropriate gel for products of this size,
separating over roughly **0.5–20 kb**.

*Reading the three lanes, max 4:*

**5.** The mutant allele carries a **deletion**, so **the amplified product of the normal allele is
longer than the amplified product of the mutant allele**. The **upper band is therefore the normal
allele** and the **lower band the mutant allele**, because **DNA fragments from the normal gene
travel a shorter distance**.

**6.** **Deer mouse 1** shows the upper band only, so it carries **two copies of the normal allele**
and is **homozygous normal**.

**7.** **Deer mouse 2** shows the lower band only, so it carries **two copies of the mutated allele**
and is **homozygous mutant**. **Deer mouse 3** shows both bands, so it carries **one copy of each**
and is **heterozygous**.

**8.** Consequence, if asked to go on: **deer mouse 2 and deer mouse 3 will probably die of a severe
viral infection**, because they have **two copies and one copy of the mutated allele respectively**,
so **insufficient antiviral proteins are produced**.

*What the gel cannot establish, max 3:*

**9.** **No sizes.** There is **no molecular weight marker / DNA ladder lane** on this gel. A ladder
**contains DNA fragments of known length and allows the size determination of the bands**; without
one, the gel gives **relative positions only**, so nothing can be said about how many base pairs
either allele is, or how large the deletion is. Gels run under different conditions are also **not
directly comparable**.

**10.** **No sequence, and therefore no substitutions.** A gel reports **the sizes present and the
number of distinct fragments**, and nothing about base order. A mutation that **does not change the
length of the amplified product** — a substitution, or a single-base indel inside a long product —
**leaves the band in the same place** and would be **invisible**: the two products **would not appear
as separate bands and would travel the same distance**. Nor can the gel say **which allele is
dominant**, whether either mouse is actually ill, or whether the deletion is inside a coding region.

R: reversing the migration — DNA runs **towards the anode** and **small fragments end up furthest
from the well**. R: calling fragment size "inversely proportional" to distance migrated; the
relationship is logarithmic.

**(b)** *Predict and account for the three haemoglobin lanes at alkaline pH.* **[6]**

**1.** **At alkaline pH, HbA and HbS are negatively charged and migrate towards the positive
electrode**, so this separation, like the DNA one, is driven by an applied field acting on a net
negative charge.

**2.** There the resemblance stops. **Glutamate has a charged, hydrophilic R group** and **valine a
non-polar one**, so replacing glutamate with valine at position 6 removes **one negatively charged
amino acid** from **each β chain**, and haemoglobin has **two β chains per molecule**. [CROSS-BOOK —
Book 1 R-group chemistry and quaternary structure]

**3.** Therefore **one less negatively charged amino acid (glutamic acid) in HbS results in slower
migration across the gel** — HbS is **less strongly attracted to the anode** and travels **a shorter
distance from the well** in the same time.

**4.** **Reject size.** **HbA and HbS are of the same molecular size**, so the sieving argument that
carried the whole of part (a) earns nothing here. Proteins of the same mass separate **by charge**;
DNA fragments of different length separate **by size**, precisely because their charge per unit mass
is constant. Importing one explanation into the other is the mark this question exists to take.

**5.** The three lanes: the **unaffected person gives a single band at the HbA position**, furthest
migrated; the person with **sickle cell anaemia gives a single band at the HbS position**, less far
migrated; the **carrier gives two bands, one at each position**.

**6.** **Thickness is a mark of its own.** The carrier's **two bands are each about half the thickness
of the homozygotes' single bands**, because the carrier makes **roughly half as much of each
haemoglobin**. Reference lanes of **known HbA and HbS** are run alongside so that band positions can
be matched to a known genotype.

### Question 5

**(a)** *The difference between the gel and the autoradiogram, and the procedure between them.*
**[10]**

*Why the two images differ, max 3:*

**1.** Lane 2 is **whole genomic DNA cut into fragments**, which produces **hundreds of thousands of
fragments of every possible length**. They **overlap continuously all the way down the lane**, so
they cannot resolve into discrete bands and appear as a **smear**. Lane 1 resolves cleanly only
because it is **one small DNA cut into a handful of fragments of known length**, 23.1 kb down to
2.0 kb.

**2.** The autoradiogram shows **a small number of discrete dark bands** because **only those
fragments that contain a sequence complementary to the probe hybridise with it**, so **only those
carry a label and only those are visualised**.

**3.** Every other fragment **is still on the membrane** — it has simply **not been labelled**. This
is the entire point of the technique: it turns *"here are all the fragments"* into *"here is the one
I care about"*, which is why a smear on the gel can still yield a readable result.

*The procedure, one mark per step with its purpose, max 7:*

**4.** **Cut.** The DNA is **digested into fragments by restriction enzymes**, which is what makes a
length difference between alleles visible at all. *(Naming this step is required; nothing about the
enzymes themselves is.)*

**5.** **Separate.** The fragments are **separated on the basis of size by agarose gel
electrophoresis**, with **molecular weight markers electrophoresed alongside** in lane 1.

**6.** **Denature.** The **double-stranded DNA molecules must be denatured to their single-stranded
form before hybridisation with the probe**, and this is done **either prior to or during the blotting
process by exposing the gel to alkaline denaturing conditions / NaOH**. Without this there is nothing
for a single-stranded probe to base-pair with. [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 1: what is being broken is
the **hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs**]

**7.** **Transfer.** The gel is **supported on a wick or sponge in a bath of buffer**, a
**nitrocellulose or nylon membrane is laid over it**, and a **stack of blotting paper is placed on
top**; **capillary action draws the buffer up through the gel and the membrane**, carrying the DNA
with it and transferring it as **a replica of the band positions on the gel**. The membrane is then
**baked at about 80 °C, or exposed to UV, to fix the DNA permanently onto it**.

**8.** **Hybridise.** The membrane is **sealed in a bag with a buffered salt solution containing a
labelled single-stranded DNA or RNA probe**, which **hybridises with the DNA of interest by
complementary base pairing**. The probe is **labelled, i.e. carries a radioactive, fluorescent or
chemical marker to facilitate detection**, and is **complementary in nucleotide sequence to the
specific target sequence it binds**.

**9.** **Wash and detect.** The membrane is **washed to remove unbound probe**, then subjected to
**autoradiography — an X-ray film is placed over the membrane** — so that **dark bands appear exactly
where the probe has base-paired**.

**10.** **Read.** **The size of the DNA that hybridises with the probe can be determined by reference
to the molecular weight markers that were electrophoresed alongside**, which is why lane 1 is in the
picture at all.

R: reciting the steps without their purposes. R: giving a probe that is double-stranded, or
unlabelled, or complementary to only one of the two alleles when both need detecting.

**(b)** *The two temperatures in Fig. 5.2, the choice for a related gene, and probe against primer.*
**[6]**

**1.** **Hybridisation is the process by which two complementary, single-stranded nucleic acid chains
base-pair and reform a double-stranded hybrid.** Crucially, **nucleic acid sequences do not need to
be perfectly complementary to be able to hybridise**, and **the stringency of hybridisation can be
controlled by varying the incubation temperature**.

**2.** **At 42 °C the conditions are more stringent.** The temperature sits **just below the melting
point of a perfectly base-paired helix**, so **all imperfect helices are unstable** and dissociate.
**Only A**, the identical match, **forms a stable double helix**.

**3.** **At 35 °C the conditions are less stringent.** **Imperfectly paired double helices can form
and remain stable**, so **A, C and E all form stable double helices** — Fig. 5.2 marks the bulges as
**imperfect base-pairing**. C and E are **related but non-identical** sequences.

**4.** So the choice of temperature is a choice of question. To **search another species for a related
gene**, use the **lower, less stringent temperature**, because a homologous gene in a different
species will be **similar but not identical** and would be missed at high stringency. The cost is
**specificity**: sequences that merely resemble the target are also picked up, so the result needs
confirming. [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 1: a **G–C pair is held by 3 hydrogen bonds and an A–T pair by
2**, so a GC-rich duplex melts at a higher temperature, which is also why a **primer low in GC content
needs a lower annealing temperature**]

**5.** **Probe against primer.** **Both are single-stranded oligonucleotides which bind to the DNA via
complementary base-pairing.** The difference is what they are for: a **primer anneals to a specific
sequence on the DNA template and provides a free 3'-OH group for DNA polymerase to add nucleotides
to**, whereas a **probe allows the detection of target DNA in a sample** and is therefore
**structurally modified, i.e. labelled**, to carry a radioactive, fluorescent or chemical marker.

**6.** So the judgement is **no, not reliably**. A probe **ranges from 15 to several thousand
nucleotides long**, where a primer is **about 20 bases**; PCR needs **two** primers **flanking** the
target, not one; and the **mismatched binding that Fig. 5.2 shows a probe tolerating at 35 °C would
destroy the specificity PCR depends on** — the whole point of a primer is that it **does not attach
elsewhere**.

### Question 6

**(a)** *The two products of one unequal crossing over, and why one is worse.* **[8]**

**1.** A single **unequal crossing over between synapsed chromosomes during meiosis** produces, in the
same event, **a duplication on one chromatid and a deletion on the other** — one chromatid gains a
segment and its partner loses the same segment.

*Deletion, max 4:*

**2.** **Deletion** — a chromosome **breaks in one or more places and a portion of it is lost**. The
loss can occur **near one end or from the interior**, giving a **terminal or an intercalary**
deletion.

**3.** The effects are **usually profound**, because the **genotype is altered due to the absence of
certain gene loci** — the information is simply gone and **no copy remains on that chromosome**.

**4.** If the deletion affects **the same gene loci on both homologous chromosomes, the effect is
usually lethal**, since **no functional copy of those loci exists anywhere in the cell**.

**5.** If **only one of a homologous pair is affected**, the effect on the phenotype is that **alleles
on the non-deficient homologue will be expressed, even if recessive**. This is the discriminating
mark and almost nobody writes it: a deletion can **unmask a recessive allele** that would otherwise
have been hidden, so a heterozygous carrier can become an affected individual without any new gene
mutation at all.

*Duplication, max 3:*

**6.** **Duplication** — **any part of the genetic material, be it a single locus or a large piece of
chromosome, is present more than once in the genome**. It can also arise from **a replication error
prior to meiosis**.

**7.** Nothing has been lost, so the immediate consequences are milder: the effects are **gene
redundancy** and **phenotypic variation**, and duplication is **an important source of genetic
variation during evolution**, because a **spare copy is free to accumulate mutations while the
original continues to do its job**.

**8.** Judgement: **losing information is worse than holding it twice.** A missing locus **cannot be
compensated for**, and it additionally **exposes whatever allele survives on the homologue**, whereas
an extra copy usually costs only **altered gene dosage**. The asymmetry is why one event that
produces both is remembered for the deletion.

**(b)** *Why a balanced translocation still causes harm, the 46-chromosome Down syndrome, and the
inversion comparison.* **[10]**

**1.** A **reciprocal translocation involves the exchange of segments between two non-homologous
chromosomes**. **Non-homologous** is the load-bearing word — exchange between homologues is **crossing
over**, which is normal. **Genetic information is not lost or gained, there is only a rearrangement of
genetic material**, and hence the **presence of a translocation does not directly alter the viability
of individuals bearing it**.

*Why a carrier can still be affected, max 3:*

**2.** **Position effect.** A translocation **may realign certain genes in relation to other genes**,
so a gene carried to a new neighbourhood is **transcribed at a different rate**. Cluster 2 supplies
the mechanism: **enhancers and silencers are DNA sequences that act over hundreds or thousands of base
pairs, work independently of their orientation, and may lie inside an intron**, and **activators bind
enhancers to raise the transcription rate while repressors bind silencers to lower it**. Move a gene
next to a different set of control elements and its expression changes even though its own sequence
is untouched. [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 2]

**3.** **A break point inside a gene.** The two breaks have to fall somewhere, and if one falls
**within a coding sequence** it **destroys that gene**, or **fuses it to part of another**, producing
a protein that does not exist in a normal cell.

**4.** So "no material lost or gained" describes **the chromosome complement, not the phenotype**. The
inventory is balanced; the arrangement is not.

*Why the children can be affected, max 3:*

**5.** **A rearranged chromosome cannot pair normally at meiosis** with its unrearranged partner, so
segregation is disorderly and **some gametes receive a duplication of a segment while others receive
a deletion of it** — **unbalanced gametes**.

**6.** **The unbalanced gamete carries a duplication or deletion of genetic material and may give rise
to an abnormal embryo** on fertilisation by a normal gamete. The parent is unaffected precisely
because the parent's own complement is balanced.

**7.** **Down syndrome without 47 chromosomes.** In a **Robertsonian translocation the long arms of
two chromosomes fuse**, most often **chromosome 21 onto chromosome 14**, and the two short arms are
lost with no effect. Such a **carrier has 45 chromosomes**, a balanced complement and a normal
phenotype. Their gamete carrying **both the 14/21 fusion chromosome and a free chromosome 21**
delivers **three
copies of the chromosome-21 loci** while the **count reads 46**, so the karyotype looks normal and the
gene dosage is not. **Non-disjunction accounts for 95% of Down syndrome; the other 5% are caused by
translocation, mosaicism or partial trisomy.**

*The inversion comparison, max 3:*

**8.** **Inversion** — **a segment of a chromosome is turned around 180° within a chromosome**. It
**requires two breaks along the length of the chromosome and subsequent reinsertion of the inverted
segment**, and it **does not involve a loss of genetic information but simply rearranges the linear
sequence**.

**9.** So it **shares two of the three risks**: it **may cause position effects**, for the same reason
— genes have been moved relative to control elements — and it has **two break points that can fall
inside a gene**. It also produces **aberrant gametes in heterozygotes**, because an inverted segment
cannot pair with its uninverted partner without looping.

**10.** The difference is the third route. An inversion keeps every locus **on its own chromosome**,
so it **cannot deliver an extra copy of a whole chromosome arm to a gamete** and **cannot produce a
trisomy-equivalent child**. A carrier of an inversion risks **unbalanced gametes**; a carrier of a
Robertsonian translocation risks **a specific, named, recurrent aneuploidy**.

### Question 7

**(a)** *Deduce which parent and which division, using Fig. 7.1.* **[10]**

*The mechanism, max 5:*

**1.** **Non-disjunction is the failure of chromosomes or chromatids to disjoin and move to opposite
poles during division.** When it occurs in meiosis, **the normal distribution of chromosomes into
gametes is disrupted**.

**2.** It happens because the **kinetochore fails to capture, or fails to hold, the spindle
microtubules**. The **centromere is the site at which the kinetochore assembles**, and if **spindle
fibres cannot attach**, the pair is **not pulled apart**. [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 1: the centromere
nucleates the kinetochore, which is the protein complex that attaches to the microtubules of the
spindle, and without attachment no poleward force acts]

**3.** **Fig. 7.1(a), meiosis I: the homologous chromosomes fail to separate.** Both members of the
pair travel to the same pole, so **all four gametes are abnormal — n+1, n+1, n−1, n−1** — and the
n+1 gamete carries **both homologues**, therefore **both of that parent's alleles at the locus**.

**4.** **Fig. 7.1(b), meiosis II: the sister chromatids fail to separate.** Meiosis I has already
separated the homologues correctly, so only one of the two secondary cells is affected and the
gametes are **n+1, n−1, n, n — two abnormal and two normal**. The n+1 gamete carries **two copies of
one chromatid**, therefore **the same allele twice**.

**5.** That contrast is the whole deduction: **meiosis I gives you two different alleles from one
parent, meiosis II gives you the same allele twice.**

*The deduction, max 5:*

**6.** The child is **P1 P2 P3**. **The mother is P1 P2 and the father is P3 P4**, so **P1 and P2 both
came from the mother** and **P3 came from the father**.

**7.** **Since the child has P1 P2 P3, and his mother has P1 and P2, non-disjunction must have
occurred in the mother** — a normal ovum could only have carried one of her two alleles.

**8.** **Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes, and would have separated P1 and P2 during normal
formation of the ovum in the mother. Since the child has both P1 and P2, non-disjunction must have
occurred during meiosis I, where the pair of homologous chromosome 21 fail to separate, pulling the
pair of homologous chromosomes to one pole.**

**9.** The alternative is excluded explicitly. **If non-disjunction occurred during meiosis II, the
ovum would contain either P1 P1 or P2 P2, and the child would have the genotype P1 P1 P3 or
P2 P2 P3** — not the genotype observed. The father contributed a **normal n gamete carrying P3**.

**10.** So: **the mother, at meiosis I**. That **(n+1) ovum fused with a normal (n) sperm**, giving a
zygote of **2n + 1 = 47 chromosomes**, with **an additional chromosome for the 21st pair**.

**(b)** *When and in what division the mosaic form arose, and the correct name for the consequence.*
**[6]**

**1.** In the mosaic form the aberration **affects only a proportion of the cells** of the individual,
while the rest carry the usual two copies.

**2.** A **meiotic error affects the gamete and therefore every cell of the individual**, because the
zygote itself would carry it and **mitosis would transmit it to all cells**. That is the **classic
form**, and it cannot produce a mosaic.

**3.** Therefore the **zygote must have started with the normal chromosome number** and the error must
have happened **after fertilisation**, during **a mitotic division in the developing embryo or
foetus**.

**4.** **Non-disjunction of chromosome 21 at that mitosis** gives **one daughter cell with three
copies of chromosome 21 and one with a single copy**; the trisomic cell **divides on to give a clone
of trisomic cells alongside the normal cells**, which is exactly what mosaicism is. The **earlier in
development it happens, the larger the affected clone**.

**5.** **Naming.** The consequence of non-disjunction is **aneuploidy** — **the general condition in
which an organism loses or gains one or more chromosomes, but not a complete set**. **Monosomy** is
**the loss of a single chromosome from an otherwise diploid genome** (2n−1) and **trisomy** is **the
gain of one chromosome** (2n+1).

**6.** Two refusals, both taken from examiners' comments. **"Polyploidy" is wrong**, because
**non-disjunction leads to an unequal distribution of the chromosomes, not the creation of an entire
set of chromosomes**; polyploidy means **multiples of the haploid chromosome set** and **occurs mainly
in plants, not animals**. **"Down syndrome" is refused as the name of the general consequence**,
because **the preamble does not give sufficient information that the outcome will definitely be Down
syndrome, and aneuploidy is a more accurate representation.**

### Question 8

**(a)** *Discuss whether screening every pregnant woman is defensible.* **[12]**

*Read the data first, max 2:*

**1.** The risk is **essentially flat at about 0.08% from age 20 to 28**, lifts slightly to about
**0.13% at 31**, reaches about **0.32% at 36**, passes **0.5% at 38**, then rises steeply to about
**3.6% at 45** — an **accelerating, roughly exponential** rise, about a **45-fold increase** across
the range. **Age over 35** is one of the recognised high-risk criteria, along with **a family or
personal history of genetic conditions, a history of miscarriage or stillbirth, and lifestyle factors**
such as exposure to cigarette smoke or radiation.

**2.** But the risk **never reaches zero**, and **most births happen to women under 35**, so **most
affected pregnancies occur in the low-risk group**. An age threshold would therefore miss the majority
of the cases it exists to find. That is the strongest single argument for the current policy and it
comes straight off the graph.

*The distinction the whole debate rests on, max 1:*

**3.** **Maternal genetic screening tests do not diagnose medical conditions in the foetus. These
screening tests determine if the foetus is at an increased or decreased risk of a particular
abnormality.** **Diagnostic testing is only performed when a screening test is abnormal**, and
**prenatal diagnostic tests are usually more invasive tests that may pose a higher risk to the
pregnant woman and the foetus**. So a screen hands back a **probability**, and converting it into an
answer costs **a risk to the pregnancy itself**.

*For, max 3 — argue the strongest version, not the weakest:*

**4.** **Beneficence.** **Parents are able to make an informed decision on the outcome of the
pregnancy** and can **potentially prepare themselves for having a child with a specific medical
condition**; parents who know **can prepare for early intervention and resources to help their child,
potentially improving the outcome of the child**. The serious case for screening is not "information
is good" — it is that **early intervention measurably improves outcomes**.

**5.** **Respect for persons.** **Autonomy emphasises the right to self-determination and acknowledges
a person's right to make choices based on personal values and beliefs**, and she cannot exercise that
right without the information. Withholding a test because a woman is 29 is a decision taken **for**
her.

**6.** **Justice.** An age threshold makes access depend on **an arbitrary cut-off** rather than on
need, and **denies the test to the group that produces most affected pregnancies**.

*Against, max 4:*

**7.** **Clinical issues.** **Genetic screening tests are not 100% accurate and there might be
instances of false positives or false negatives.** **False positives might result in psychological
distress and might also result in more invasive and riskier tests for a confirmatory diagnosis.**
**False negatives might incorrectly give parents a sense of reassurance while, in reality, the foetus
has a genetic condition.** Testing a whole population, most of it at very low risk, **raises the
absolute number of false positives** even though the test itself has not changed.

**8.** **Non-maleficence.** **Such news might have a negative psychological impact on the parents**,
and **parents might feel particularly stressed and anxious should the condition of the foetus have no
cure or effective treatment**. **Diagnostic tests such as amniocentesis and CVS carry high risks of
harming the foetus and the pregnant woman.**

**9.** **Degree of severity.** **The mental and physical impairments of Down syndrome can vary
greatly. Screening tests cannot accurately predict the extent of the disease nor the specific
challenges a child might face.** A number is being used to decide something the number cannot
describe.

**10.** **Termination of pregnancy.** **Abortion of a foetus with Down syndrome is a likely
outcome**, which **raises ethical issues about the moral status of a foetus and the rights of
parents**, and **a decrease in the Down syndrome population via termination of pregnancy might raise
concerns about eugenics, which is selective breeding for desired traits.**

**11.** **Care, and the stakeholder who is not in the room.** **Disability advocates argue that
screening for medical conditions like Down syndrome contributes to the devaluation of people with
such lives**, which can lead to **the marginalisation of individuals with such medical conditions and
the general negative societal attitude towards them.** Plus **justice again**: **genetic screening
might not be available to all pregnant women from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds**, and
**inequities in access could worsen existing health disparities**, since parents from less fortunate
backgrounds **might not be able to bear the financial burden of the medical condition of the child**.

*The judgement, max 2 — a list with no position caps, however long the list is:*

**12.** Name the **stakeholders separately**: the **pregnant woman**, her **partner**, the **foetus**,
the **healthcare system**, and **people already living with the condition**. Most answers argue only
for the parents, and the disability-advocacy point belongs to a group with no voice in the decision.

**13.** A defensible landing: screening should be **offered rather than routine**, conditional on
**pre-test counselling**, which is **essential to ensure that pregnant women are fully informed about
the purpose, process and implications of genetic screening** and should also **cover the possible
outcomes and the subsequent steps to take**; on **explicit consent**, since **care must be taken to
ensure that the pregnant woman has the right to accept or reject the screening test without
coercion**; and on **equitable access**. State the reason, not just the position: **the same test that
serves autonomy when it is chosen freely undermines autonomy when it becomes the default**, because a
routine test is one a woman must actively refuse rather than actively choose.

**QWC:** the four principles must be **named and attached to a party** — **respect for persons and
autonomy, maximising benefits and minimising harms, justice, and care**. A bioethics mark is earned by
**a specific mechanism attached to a named stakeholder**, never by a moral label. R: "playing God".
R: a list of considerations with no judgement.

**(b)** *Account for the trend in Fig. 8.2, and judge what it establishes.* **[6]**

### Question 9

**(a)** *Deduce what has gone wrong in diseases X and Y, and what the profiles cannot establish.*
**[10]**

*Why the profile measures expression at all, max 3:*

**1.** The starting material is **mRNA, not genomic DNA**, and that choice is the whole method.
**Genomic DNA is present in the same quantity in every cell whether or not the gene is being
expressed**, so amplifying it would give no information about expression; **using mRNA as the starting
material means that only sequences that have actually been transcribed are amplified**.

**2.** mRNA cannot be a PCR template directly, because **RNA is single-stranded**, and **PCR uses a
set of two primers, so two strands are needed for both primers to bind regions flanking the gene of
interest**; with one strand only, **unidirectional extension of one primer means the specific sequence
to be amplified cannot be marked out**. Also **the conformation of the active site of Taq polymerase
is complementary to DNA but not RNA**, and **RNA is unstable and will degrade upon heating**.

**3.** So **reverse transcriptase is used first to make complementary DNA (cDNA)** from the mRNA. The
cDNA is then **separated by size by gel electrophoresis**, and the gene of interest is located using
**probes complementary to the gene of interest**. The **amount of product in a lane therefore reports
the amount of mRNA that was in the cell**.

*The two diseases, max 4:*

**4.** **Disease X.** The band sits at **the same position, 700 bp**, but is **much thicker and
darker**. Same position means **same length**, so the transcript has **not changed size**. The extra
intensity is a **thicker band for the individual suffering from disease X, indicating increased
expression of the gene of interest / an increased amount of gene product**.

**5.** Accept — and this is where Cluster 2 pays — a **gain-of-function mutation in regulatory regions
such as promoters or enhancers**, which would **raise the rate of transcription**, since an
**activator bound to an enhancer increases the transcription rate above basal**. **Reject
"gain-in-function mutation" on its own, and reject a change in the sequence of the coding region**,
because a coding change alters the product and not the amount. [CROSS-CLUSTER — Cluster 2]

**6.** **Disease Y.** There is **no band for the individual suffering from disease Y, indicating no
expression of the gene of interest / no gene product**. Accept **loss of the gene**, or a
**loss-of-function mutation in regulatory regions such as promoters or enhancers** — a **repressor
bound to a silencer lowers the transcription rate**, and a **promoter that no longer binds the general
transcription factors cannot assemble a transcription initiation complex**. **Reject "loss-in-function
mutation" on its own, and reject a change in the sequence of the coding region.**

**7.** Read as a pair, the two panels say that **a disease can be caused by a gene being expressed too
much or not at all, without a single base of its coding sequence being wrong** — which is the whole
argument of LO 2(i).

*What the profiles cannot establish, max 3:*

**8.** **Nothing about sequence, and therefore nothing about most mutations.** The **band position is
unchanged in disease X**, so any change that **does not alter length** — a substitution, or a small
indel in a long transcript — is **invisible**: two such products **would not appear as separate bands
in the gel** and **would travel the same distance**.

**9.** **Nothing downstream of the mRNA.** A cDNA profile **cannot provide information on
post-translational regulation, on the cause or origin of the disease, on how the disease is inherited,
on which cellular processes are affected, or on the effect of environmental or epigenetic factors on
disease progression or severity**. A protein could be made in normal amounts and then **degraded by
the proteasome**, or made and never **activated by phosphorylation**, and this profile would show
nothing at all.

**10.** **Nothing certain about disease Y.** An absent band is also what a **failed RNA extraction, a
failed reverse transcription, a failed amplification or a failed hybridisation** looks like. Without a
**positive internal control**, "no expression" and "the experiment did not work" are the same picture.

**(b)** *What a housekeeping gene adds, and which conclusion it most affects.* **[4]**

**1.** A **housekeeping gene such as GAPDH is expressed commonly in all cells and tissues at
relatively high levels**, regardless of treatment or disease state, so it serves as an **internal
reference or control against which the gene of interest is normalised** — which is why such data is
reported as a ratio.

**2.** It **corrects for differences between samples in the amount of starting RNA, in the efficiency
of reverse transcription and in the efficiency of amplification**, so that **any difference observed
is due to a genuine difference in expression and not to technical or sampling variation**. That
matters for **disease X**, whose whole claim rests on one band being **thicker** than another, and
band intensity is easily changed by how much RNA went into the tube.

**3.** It matters far more for **disease Y**, which is the conclusion it most affects. **An absent
band is the weakest possible evidence**, because it is indistinguishable from a technical failure, so
a **housekeeping band present in that same lane** is what turns "no expression" into a finding. If the
**housekeeping band is also missing, the lane is void and no conclusion may be drawn from it** —
exactly the reasoning that refused to clear an individual whose sample **failed to display a result
for the positive control** on a SARS-CoV-2 gel.

**4.** So the control does two different jobs at once: for **disease X it makes a quantitative claim
safe**, and for **disease Y it makes a negative claim possible at all**. A negative result without a
positive control is not a result.
