### Question 1

**(a)** *How the pattern in Fig. 1.2 is produced, and what lane 2 should show.* **[8]**

The two alleles of the marker differ in the **number of TAGA repeats**, six against ten, so amplifying across the locus **produces DNA fragments of different sizes / lengths**. **[1]**

**Add forward and reverse primers**, **flanking the 3' ends of the genetic marker**, and **amplify using the polymerase chain reaction**. **[1]**

The gel is run with a **direct current / potential difference applied across it**. **[1]**

**The phosphate groups of DNA are negatively charged, so DNA migrates to the anode / positive electrode**, moving **from the cathode to the anode**. **[1]**

Every fragment carries the **same charge per unit length**, a **constant charge density**, so **charge cancels out** and the fragments are **separated on the basis of size alone**. **[1]**

**The gel matrix acts as a molecular sieve**: **shorter fragments are less impeded by the pores and travel faster and further from the well**, while **larger fragments move more slowly and stay nearer the well**. **[1]**

Each carrier is **heterozygous** at the marker, so **the carrier should have 2 bands**. **[1]**

The two bands lie **at 24 bp and 40 bp**, the 24 bp band (six repeats, linked to **g**) **further from the well** than the 40 bp band (ten repeats, linked to **G**). **[1]**

**(b)** *Why lane 5's band is thicker.* **[4]** [INFERRED: extension, no official key]

**Band thickness reports the quantity of DNA at that position**, not its length. **[1]**

In lane 3 the individual's **two alleles differ in length**, so the amplified product is **split between two positions** and each band holds **about half the total**. **[1]**

In lane 5 the **two alleles co-migrate**, so **both products pile into one position** and that band holds roughly **twice as much DNA** as either band in lane 3. **[1]**

The two alleles are the same length because of **crossing over of genetic marker segments in one of the carrier parents**, which has changed the repeat number of one allele to match the other. The individual is still a carrier; the gel simply cannot resolve the two alleles. **[1]**

**(c)** *Which of the three questions Fig. 1.2 can answer.* **[4]** [INFERRED: extension, no official key]

**Base sequence: no.** Electrophoresis **separates on the basis of size alone**, so it reports length and never base order. Sequencing would be required. **[1]**

**Length in base pairs: yes, here.** **Lanes 1 and 6 carry a DNA ladder of fragments of known length**, so band position can be compared against known reference bands. **[1]**

Without that ladder the gel would give **relative size only**, which is why the presence of the ladder is the thing that decides this one. **[1]**

**Dominance: no.** The gel reports the **length of a marker linked to the gene**, not the phenotype. **Dominance is established from phenotypes across a family**, and a gel cannot show which allele is expressed. Note also that this marker is **non-coding**, so it is not the disease gene itself. **[1]**

**Gaps closed:** B2-38 (the whole gel mechanism, the largest single gap in the Cluster 3 sitting), B2-43 (what a gel cannot establish, including the ladder nuance), B2-44 (reading a gel by band position and band thickness).

**Concepts fused:** (1) tandem repeat number setting fragment length; (2) primers flanking the target and the amplification step that must precede the gel; (3) negatively charged phosphate groups and migration to the anode; (4) constant charge density, so charge cancels and size alone separates; (5) agarose as a molecular sieve; (6) heterozygosity read as two bands; (7) band intensity as quantity, not length; (8) co-migration of two alleles of equal length; (9) the DNA ladder as the thing that converts relative to absolute size; (10) non-coding marker DNA linked to a gene rather than being the gene; (11) the limits of the technique as a separate category of answer from its mechanism.

### Question 2

**(a)** *Option audit on the PCR advantage and limitation table.* MCQ key: **D**. **[6]**

**Row A fails on its advantage.** **Taq polymerase lacks (3' to 5') proofreading ability**, so PCR carries a **high error rate due to the lack of a built-in proofreading activity in Taq DNA polymerase** and **produces a higher than normal frequency of replication errors**. **[1]**

Row A's limitation is correct: **after several cycles enzymes may get denatured even though primers and nucleotides are added in excess and are still available, so PCR cannot continue**. **[1]**

**Row B fails on its advantage.** After 20 cycles PCR **is able to produce 2²⁰ copies of target DNA**, not 20². Row B's limitation is correct: **prior sequence information is usually necessary to construct the primers**, so an unknown sequence cannot be amplified. **[1]**

**Row C fails on its limitation.** **PCR is relatively faster than traditional methods of using bacteria as it takes only several hours.** Row C's advantage is correct: **robustness, a broad range of nucleic acid sources can be used**. **[1]**

**Row D is correct in both halves.** Advantage: **sensitivity, it only requires a trace amount of DNA**. **[1]**

Limitation: the **size range of the DNA fragments that can be amplified is limited to 0.1 to 5 kb**. **[1]**

**Defect in the school's own key, flagged.** The TMJC scheme's note on row A opens with the words *Taq DNA polymerase is a DNA polymerase, hence it has a proof-reading mechanism*. That clause is wrong, and every other scheme in the corpus contradicts it. Answer with the version above: **Taq lacks 3' to 5' exonuclease activity, therefore no proofreading**.

**(b)** *The counting, and which limitation defeats which laboratory.* **[10]**

Starting from **one double-stranded molecule**, after n cycles the number of copies is **2ⁿ**, because **both new strands act as templates in the next cycle, so the number of copies of the target sequence doubles every cycle**. **[1]**

After 20 cycles there are **2²⁰ = 1 048 576 DNA molecules**. **[1]**

Each molecule is **double-stranded**, so there are **2 × 2²⁰ = 2²¹ = 2 097 152 separate DNA strands**, of which only **two are the original strands**. Molecules and strands are counted differently, and the question decides which. **[1]**

*Examiner's comment on the nearest published equivalent: this question was surprisingly not well done, and many students did not remember the formula 2ⁿ for calculating the number of copies of DNA amplified from n number of PCR cycles.*

**Laboratory J is defeated by the size range.** Its target is **4 kb**, at the top of the **0.1 to 5 kb** window, and long products amplify poorly. **[1]**

J is also exposed to **contamination**, because **PCR is too sensitive: a minute amount of contaminant DNA which contains a sequence complementary to the primers can be amplified, leading to a misleading result**, and a burial-site sample carries modern DNA from everyone who has handled it. **[1]**

**Laboratory K is defeated before it starts, by the primer requirement.** **Prior sequence information is usually necessary to construct the primers**, and K's gene has never been sequenced, so no specific primers can be designed. Sensitivity and fragment size are no obstacle to K at all. **[1]**

**No helicase is needed**, because **heating to about 95 °C breaks the hydrogen bonds between the complementary base pairs**, denaturing the target so the two strands separate. **[1]**

**No single-strand binding proteins are needed**, because the **annealing temperature controls re-annealing** and does their job. **[1]**

**No primase is needed**, because **DNA primers are supplied ready-made**; but synthesis still **cannot occur de novo**, so a **primer supplying a free 3' OH group is still required** and **complementary base pairing still governs** which nucleotide is added. **[1]**

**No DNA ligase is needed, because there is no lagging strand** in PCR and therefore **no Okazaki fragments to join**; each primer is extended continuously to the end of the template. **[1]**

### Question 3

**(a)** *Name and account for the aberration in each of the three patients.* **[12]**

**Patient P: reciprocal translocation.** A **segment of one chromosome breaks off and becomes attached to a non-homologous chromosome**. **[1]**

**No genetic information is lost or gained**, so the **carrier has a normal phenotype**. **[1]**

At meiosis the **rearranged chromosome cannot pair normally with its homologue**, so **segregation produces unbalanced gametes**. **[1]**

An **unbalanced gamete carries a duplication or a deletion of genetic material and may give rise to an abnormal embryo**; most such embryos are lost, which accounts for the miscarriages, and the surviving one accounts for the affected child. **[1]**

**Patient Q: deletion.** A **segment of a chromosome is lost**, so the affected homologue is shorter. **[1]**

Because one homologue has lost the locus, the **allele on the intact homologue is expressed even if it is recessive**, since no functional dominant allele remains to mask it. This is **unmasking**. **[1]**

It explains the inheritance pattern directly: only one parent needed to contribute the recessive allele, because the other parent contributed a chromosome missing that locus altogether. **[1]**

If the **same locus were deleted on both homologues it would be lethal**, since **no functional copy would remain**. This is why deletion is the most damaging of the four. **[1]**

**Patient R: inversion.** A **segment breaks off, rotates through 180° and rejoins the same chromosome**, so the **gene order in that region is reversed**. **[1]**

**No genetic material is lost or gained**, which is why the quantity of DNA is normal. **[1]**

The **base sequence of the gene itself is unchanged**, so this is not a gene mutation and no altered polypeptide is produced. **[1]**

**Gene activity has changed because the gene's position has changed, not because the DNA sequence changed.** A break point can move a gene next to condensed heterochromatin, or away from the enhancer it needs, so transcription falls or stops. **[1]**

**(b)** *The single error that generates two of them at once.* **[4]**

**Unequal crossing over** between **misaligned homologous chromosomes** at **prophase I**. **[1]**

It produces a **duplication** and a **deletion**. **[1]**

One product **carries the segment twice**; the reciprocal product **has lost it**, so both aberrations arise from one exchange, one in each product. **[1]**

Neither inversion nor translocation can be produced this way, because both require a break and rejoining involving a different orientation or a **non-homologous** chromosome, not a misaligned exchange between homologues. **[1]**

**Source:** IJC Prelim 2018 P2 Q4a(i) [1] (the unequal crossing over stem and the naming of both products, verbatim key: *duplication of chromosome segment T / deletion of chromosome segment T*); School Prelim 2023 P1 Q17 (the unbalanced-gamete consequence, verbatim key including *the unbalanced gamete carries a duplication or deletion of genetic material and may give rise to an abnormal embryo*); YIJC Prelim 2025 P3 Q4a and EJC Prelim 2023 P2 Q4c(i) (the four structural aberrations as the syllabus names them, verbatim); NYJC Prelim 2025 P3 Q4a (the definition set, verbatim). The three patient scenarios are an extension built on those keys, tagged [INFERRED: extension, no official key]; the position-effect wording is HCI Book 2 chapter 2.5 verbatim.

### Question 4

**(a)** *The level each drug acts at, and one mechanism the cell uses there.* **[10]**

**Drug 1 acts at the chromatin level.** Nuclease resistance plus unacetylated histone tails means the DNA over T is **condensed**, and **no pre-mRNA at all** means transcription never began. **[1]**

Mechanism: **histone acetyltransferases add acetyl groups to the lysine residues of the histone tails**. **Acetylation disperses the positive charge**, so the **ionic bonds between the positively charged histones and the negatively charged phosphate backbone are lost** and the **affinity of the histones for DNA falls**. **[1]**

The chromatin becomes **less compact**, moving from a **heterochromatin-like to a euchromatin-like state**, so **transcription factors and RNA polymerase can access the promoter**. **Histone deacetylases reverse this and restore the positive charge**, recompacting the chromatin and silencing the gene. **[1]**

**Drug 2 acts at the post-transcriptional level.** Pre-mRNA is present at the normal amount, so transcription is untouched; no mature mRNA reaches the cytoplasm, so **processing or export has been blocked**. **[1]**

Mechanism: the primary transcript is **capped at the 5' end, spliced by the spliceosome, and polyadenylated**, then **exported through the nuclear pore**. **Alternative splicing** at this level also decides **which** protein is made from one gene. **[1]**

**Drug 3 acts at the translational level.** Mature mRNA is present at the normal amount with a **full-length poly(A) tail**, so it is neither missing nor being degraded early, yet no polypeptide appears. **[1]**

Mechanism: **eukaryotic initiation factors bind the 5' cap and the poly(A) tail**, **recruit the small ribosomal subunit** and **scan for the start codon AUG**, after which the large subunit binds and the translation initiation complex forms. **Translational repressor proteins bound to the untranslated regions block this step.** **[1]**

**Drug 4 acts at the post-translational level.** A **full-length polypeptide** is present but the **active product is absent**, so the block is after translation. **[1]**

Mechanism: **proteolytic cleavage of an inactive precursor polypeptide at specific basic residues by tissue-specific proteases**, which is how one polypeptide can yield different active products in different tissues. **[1]**

Also at this level: **reversible phosphorylation by kinases and phosphatases**, **glycosylation**, and **ubiquitination marking a protein for degradation by the proteasome**. Of these, **only phosphorylation is reversible**. **[1]**

**(b)** *Ranking the four drugs by resources wasted.* **[4]** [INFERRED: extension, no official key]

The ranking is **ordinal, by how early in the pipeline the block falls**, and not by how much machinery each mechanism itself consumes. **[1]**

**Drug 1 wastes least.** **If the gene is blocked at the chromatin level, transcription is prevented, so nothing downstream is synthesised at all**: no ribonucleotides, no ATP for transcription, no amino acids, no GTP for translation. **[1]**

**Drug 2 next**, since one **pre-mRNA** has been paid for; then **drug 3**, since a **complete mature mRNA** has been paid for, including capping, splicing and polyadenylation. **[1]**

**Drug 4 wastes most**, because a **full-length polypeptide** has been synthesised, at the cost of amino acids and roughly four high-energy phosphate bonds per peptide bond, and it must now be degraded. Note that speed runs the opposite way: post-translational control acts fastest because the product is already made and needs only modifying. **[1]**

**(c)** *What promoter methylation changes, and how you would show it.* **[2]**

**What changes: how much protein is made and in which cells.** **DNA methyltransferases methylate the cytosines of CpG dinucleotides in the CpG island of the promoter**, which **prevents transcription factors from binding** and **recruits MeCP, which in turn recruits histone deacetylase**, condensing the chromatin further, so **the rate of transcription of T falls** in liver cells alone. **[1]**

**What does not change: what protein is made.** The **base sequence of T is unaltered**, so any T polypeptide still produced has the **identical amino acid sequence** and is not truncated. ### Question 7

**(a)** *The fate of each engineered transcript, and why RNA needs protecting.* **[9]**

**Molecule A, no 5' cap.** The cap **distinguishes mRNA from other RNA types in the cell**. **[1]**

It also **protects the transcript from degradation by exonucleases**, and it **defines the 5' end, which is where the small ribosomal subunit is recruited**. **[1]**

So molecule A is **degraded rapidly** and, while it survives, **initiation factors cannot bind and the small ribosomal subunit is not recruited**, so **little or no protein is made**. **[1]**

**Molecule B, no poly(A) tail.** The tail **protects the mRNA from degradation by exonucleases**, **facilitates export from the nucleus**, and makes the mRNA **a more stable template for translation**. **[1]**

So molecule B has a **short half-life**. It can still be translated, because the cap is present, but **fewer rounds of translation occur from the same template**, so the yield of protein is low. **[1]**

**Molecule C, intron 1 retained.** Introns are normally **excised by the spliceosome**, which assembles from **snRNPs that recognise and bind the 5' and 3' splice sites flanking the intron by complementary base pairing**, after which the **flanking exons are ligated**. **[1]**

With the intron retained, it is **read as coding sequence**; because **intron length is usually not a multiple of three**, the **reading frame shifts** for everything downstream, **every codon after the intron is misread**, and a **premature stop codon** is usually reached, giving a **truncated, non-functional polypeptide**. **[1]**

Two splicing rules worth stating here: **only introns are removed, without changing the sequential order of exons**, and **the first exon cannot be spliced out**, so no variant lacking exon 1 can exist. **[1]**

Why RNA needs the protection and DNA does not: **DNA carries a hydrogen at the 2' carbon of deoxyribose where RNA carries a hydroxyl group**. That **2' OH carries a partial negative charge which repels the negatively charged phosphate backbone**, so **RNA cannot coil as tightly as the DNA double helix** and is **more susceptible to chemical degradation**. Being **single-stranded** rather than a stable antiparallel duplex compounds it. **[1]**

**(b)** *The covalent bonds from gene to folded protein, and what makes collagen strong.* **[4]**

**Phosphodiester bonds** join the nucleotides of the DNA and then of the mRNA, formed by **condensation between the 3' OH of one nucleotide and the 5' phosphate of the next**. **[1]**

**Ester bond** joining the amino acid to the **CCA stem at the 3' end of the tRNA**, formed by the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase using ATP; then **peptide bonds** between successive amino acid residues, formed by **condensation catalysed by peptidyl transferase, an rRNA activity of the large ribosomal subunit**. **[1]**

Formed **after the polypeptide leaves the ribosome**: **disulfide bonds** between cysteine residues, fixing tertiary and quaternary structure, and the post-translational modifications **glycosylation**, **phosphorylation** and **ubiquitination**, of which **only phosphorylation is reversible**. **[1]**

Collagen: the strength comes from **covalent cross-links between adjacent tropocollagen molecules**, not from the peptide bonds within any one chain. These are **interchain covalent bonds**, and they sit in the same category as the disulfide bonds above, formed after translation and between separate polypeptides. A single chain has no such links, which is exactly why the fibre is far stronger than its parts. **[1]**

**(c)** *Reading Fig. 7.1, and how a 3' UTR protein raises output.* **[3]**

**When length of poly(A) tail is <50 nucleotides long, no protein was produced**, because **with a short poly(A) tail the ribosome is unable to bind to the RNA** and the **RNA is degraded by exonucleases**. **[1]**

**When length of poly(A) tail increases from 50 to 250 nucleotides, concentration of protein increases**, because a **longer poly(A) tail increases mRNA stability and half-life**, it **facilitates ribosome binding for translation**, and the **increased stability allows for repeated translation**. **[1]**

The **3' UTR carries stabilising and destabilising sequences**, which are **binding sites for specific proteins that increase or decrease the rate of poly(A) tail shortening**. A protein bound at a stabilising sequence **slows the shortening**, so the **half-life rises** and **more rounds of translation occur from the same template**, raising output **without a second round of transcription**. **[1]**

**(b)** *From the double helix to a metaphase chromatid, plus the spindle organiser and two reasons for condensing.* **[8]**

**First level.** **DNA is wound around a histone octamer to form a nucleosome**, **stabilised by histone H1**, and **nucleosomes are linked by linker DNA to form the beads on a string structure**, about **10 nm** across. **[1]**

The DNA is held there by **ionic bonds between the positively charged amino acids of the histones and the negatively charged phosphate backbone of DNA**. **[1]**

**Second level.** **Interactions between the histone tails of neighbouring nucleosomes, the linker DNA and the H1 histone coil the string into the 30 nm chromatin fibre, the solenoid.** Beads on a string comes first and the solenoid second; the order is a marking point in itself. **[1]**

**Third level.** **The solenoid attaches to a scaffold of non-histone proteins to form looped domains of about 300 nm.** **[1]**

**The looped domains coil and fold further, compacting all the chromatin to produce the metaphase chromosome, comprising two sister chromatids joined at the centromere.** **[1]**

**The spindle organiser is the centriole.** It is **made of protein**, specifically microtubules, has **no membrane**, and it **organises microtubules into spindle fibres** rather than generating them. **[1]**

**Region X, the centromere, is DNA**: a **short, AT-rich, tandemly repeated non-coding sequence** carrying **binding sites complementary to kinetochore proteins**. Centromeres and centrioles have nothing in common beyond both being needed for division, and a centromere is not derived from a centriole. **[1]**

Two reasons for condensing: **the DNA does not get entangled and break during anaphase, and segregation into the daughter nuclei is facilitated**; and **condensation regulates gene accessibility**, since a condensed region cannot be reached by the transcription machinery. The **antiparallel double helix is itself what allows DNA to coil tightly** enough for any of this. **[1]**

**(c)** *Why the circularised chromosome loses nothing.* **[4]**

Replication is **semi-conservative** and **every new strand is built 5' to 3' only**, so one of the two new strands at each fork must be made **discontinuously, as a series of short fragments known as Okazaki fragments**, each begun by its **own RNA primer**. **[1]**

**DNA polymerase I removes each RNA primer and replaces it with DNA**, and **DNA ligase seals the nick**; **DNA polymerase III carries out the bulk of synthesis and the 3' to 5' proofreading**. **[1]**

At the extreme end of a **linear** molecule, when the **final RNA primer is removed there is no upstream strand onto which DNA polymerase can build**, so **no free 3' OH group is available** and **the gap cannot be filled**. A **circular** molecule has no ends, so every primer removed has an upstream strand available and every gap is filled and sealed, which is also why a bacterium never faces the problem. **[1]**

The loss falls at **both ends of the linear chromosome, not one**. The **two replication forks travel away from the origin in opposite directions**, so **each daughter strand is leading at one end and lagging at the other**; each daughter molecule therefore ends up short at one end and its partner short at the other, and across the pair sequence is lost from both ends. The sequence lost is the **telomere**, which also **prevents the cell's DNA repair machinery from recognising the chromosome end as a double-strand break**.  **[1]**

### Question 9

**(a)** *Non-disjunction defined, the two divisions, and aneuploidy against polyploidy.* **[5]**

**Non-disjunction is the failure of chromosomes or chromatids to separate and move to opposite poles** during nuclear division. **[1]**

**In meiosis I, homologous chromosomes fail to separate**, so the gamete receives **two different homologues from that parent**, carrying **two different alleles**. **[1]**

**In meiosis II, sister chromatids fail to separate**, so the gamete receives **two copies of the same chromatid**, carrying **the same allele twice**. That is the discriminator: two different alleles from one parent means meiosis I, the same allele twice means meiosis II. **[1]**

Fertilisation of such a gamete by a normal one gives a zygote with **three copies of chromosome 21 and 47 chromosomes in total**. **[1]**

**Aneuploidy is having one extra chromosome or lacking a chromosome; polyploidy is having more than two complete sets.** Only one chromosome is in excess here, so the condition is **aneuploidy**, and **trisomy-21** names it precisely. **[1]**

**(b)** *Screening against diagnosis, and which test to offer universally.* **[5]**

**Screening tests do not diagnose.** It **determines whether the foetus is at an increased or decreased risk of a particular abnormality**, from **markers in the pregnant woman's blood or from ultrasound**, and it carries **no risk to the pregnancy**. **[1]**

**Diagnostic tests are performed only when a screening test is abnormal.** It samples fetal material directly, by **amniocentesis** (a needle through the abdomen into the uterus for amniotic fluid) or **chorionic villus sampling** (cells from the placenta), and it **confirms the genetic condition of the foetus**. It is **more invasive and poses a higher risk to the pregnant woman and the foetus**. **[1]**

Reading Table 9.1: **maternal age alone detects only 30% at a 5% false positive rate**, so it misses about seven affected pregnancies in ten. **[1]**

**Adding foetal nuchal translucency at 11 to 14 weeks raises detection to 75% at the same 5%**; adding **serum beta-hCG and PAPP-A raises it to 90%**; adding the **nasal bone raises it to 97%**, still at **5%**. Detection rises fourfold while the false positive rate does not move. **[1]**

Judgement: offer the **combined first-trimester test at 11 to 14 weeks** using maternal age, nuchal translucency, nasal bone, beta-hCG and PAPP-A. It gives the **highest detection rate for no increase in false positives**, and it is **early enough for a diagnostic test to follow** and for decisions to be made before late pregnancy. **Ultrasound markers at 16 to 23 weeks are worse on both axes, 75% detection at a 10 to 15% false positive rate**, and later. **[1]**

**(c)** *Discuss the bioethics of offering screening to every pregnant woman.* **[8]**

Structure the answer the way the school's framework does: **state the ethical question, set out the facts, name the stakeholders and how each is affected, apply the ethical principles, give the alternative and the rebuttal, then decide and justify**. **[1]**

**Stakeholders:** the pregnant woman, the foetus, her partner and family, clinicians and the health service that funds the programme, and people living with Down syndrome. A bioethics mark is earned by naming a party who is harmed or benefited and the mechanism; **generalised statements such as playing God are explicitly rejected**. **[1]**

**For, autonomy and informed consent.** Screening supplies information the woman can act on. **Pre-test counselling is essential to ensure that pregnant women are fully informed about the purpose, process and implications of genetic screening**, and **care must be taken to ensure that the pregnant woman has the right to accept or reject the screening test without coercion**. **[1]**

**For, beneficence.** **Parents are able to make an informed decision on the outcome of the pregnancy and can prepare themselves for having a child with a specific medical condition**; **parents who know that their child has Down syndrome can prepare for early intervention and resources, potentially improving the outcome of the child**. It **can avoid having offspring with serious genetic disease, to prevent suffering of the child and parents**, and it **allows couples to have children who would otherwise choose not to**. **[1]**

**For, reach.** Universal offer **detects trisomy-21 in mothers who did not have increased maternal age and would not otherwise have been offered testing based on maternal age alone**, which, from Table 9.1, is where the great majority of cases sit. **[1]**

**Against, non-maleficence.** **Genetic screening tests are not 100% accurate.** **False positives result in psychological distress for the pregnant woman and her partner and lead to more invasive and riskier tests for a confirmatory diagnosis**; **false negatives incorrectly give parents a sense of reassurance while the foetus has a genetic condition**. **Diagnostic tests such as amniocentesis and CVS carry risks of harming the foetus and the pregnant woman.** And the **mental and physical impairments of Down syndrome vary greatly, so screening cannot predict the extent of the disease or the specific challenges a child might face**. **[1]**

**Against, the decision that follows.** **Abortion of a foetus with Down syndrome is a likely outcome**, which raises **the moral status of a foetus and the rights of parents**, on which individuals differ, and **a decrease in the Down syndrome population via termination might raise concerns about eugenics**. **Disability advocates argue that screening for conditions like Down syndrome contributes to the devaluation of people with such lives** and to their **marginalisation**. **[1]**

**Against, justice and equity, then the judgement.** **Screening might not be available to all pregnant women from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, and inequities in access could worsen existing health disparities.** Weighing these: offering the test to every pregnant woman **is defensible, but only as an offer and never as an expectation**. That requires **counselling before and after the test**, an **explicit right of refusal without penalty**, **public funding so access does not depend on income**, and **clear communication that a positive screen is a probability and not a diagnosis**. The principle that decides it is **respect for persons and autonomy**: the programme's job is to inform a decision, not to make one. **[1]**
