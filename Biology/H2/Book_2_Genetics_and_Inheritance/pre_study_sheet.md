## 2.1 DNA Structure and Replication

1. Any hereditary material has to do four things: **store a lot of information**, stay **chemically stable**, **replicate accurately**, and still allow **variation**. Every structural feature of DNA below exists to deliver one of those four.
2. A **nucleotide** is a **pentose sugar** + a **nitrogenous base** + a **phosphate group**. DNA uses **deoxyribose**, RNA uses **ribose**, and the extra **2' OH** on ribose is what makes RNA the less stable of the two.
3. Bases split into **purines** (**adenine**, **guanine**, double ring) and **pyrimidines** (**cytosine**, **thymine**, **uracil**, single ring).
4. Nucleotides join by **condensation** into **phosphodiester bonds** between the **3' OH** of one sugar and the **5' phosphate** of the next, building a **sugar-phosphate backbone** that runs **5' to 3'**. That directionality governs everything downstream.
5. The **double helix** is **two antiparallel strands**, backbone outside and bases inside, held by **hydrogen bonds** between **complementary base pairs**: **A-T with 2 bonds, G-C with 3**.
6. A **purine always pairs with a pyrimidine**, which is why the helix keeps a **constant width** along its whole length.
7. Replication is **semi-conservative**: each daughter molecule keeps one parental strand. **Meselson and Stahl** proved it with **¹⁵N/¹⁴N labelling** and **density-gradient centrifugation**.
8. Replication runs in four stages: unwinding at **origins of replication** by **helicase** (with **single-strand binding proteins** and **topoisomerase**), then **RNA primers** laid by **primase**, then extension by **DNA polymerase**, then joining by **DNA ligase**.
9. DNA polymerase has **two limitations that shape the whole mechanism**: it cannot start a chain from nothing (hence primers), and it only synthesises **5' to 3'** (hence a continuous **leading strand** and a discontinuous **lagging strand** of **Okazaki fragments**).
10. Accuracy comes from **proofreading** by DNA polymerase's **3' to 5' exonuclease** activity.
11. Because the lagging strand cannot be primed right at the end, linear chromosomes shorten every round. This is the **end replication problem**, and chapter 2.3 picks it up again.

## 2.2 Eukaryotic Gene Expression

1. The **central dogma** runs **DNA to RNA to polypeptide**, one way. DNA itself is never used directly, because it has to stay intact as the permanent store.
2. **RNA** differs from DNA on four things that matter: **ribose** not deoxyribose, **uracil** not thymine, **single-stranded**, and much shorter.
3. Five RNA types do five jobs: **mRNA** carries the message, **tRNA** adapts codon to amino acid, **rRNA** builds and catalyses in the ribosome, **snRNA** splices, and **siRNA/miRNA** regulate.
4. In **transcription**, **RNA polymerase** reads the **template strand 3' to 5'** and synthesises **5' to 3'**. **General transcription factors** position it at the **promoter**, which contains the **TATA box**.
5. Transcription runs **initiation, elongation, termination**, and produces **pre-mRNA**.
6. Eukaryotes then run **post-transcriptional modification**, which prokaryotes do not: a **5' cap**, **splicing** of **introns** out by the **spliceosome** leaving **exons** joined, and a **3' poly(A) tail**.
7. The **genetic code** is **triplet, universal, non-overlapping and continuous, and degenerate but unambiguous**. **64 codons**, of which **61 code** and **3 are stop**.
8. **tRNA** is the physical adaptor: an **anticodon** at one end, the amino acid attached at the **CCA end**, and **aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase** doing the charging using **ATP**.
9. **Translation** happens on the ribosome's **A, P and E sites**: **initiation** at the first **AUG**, **elongation** where **peptidyl transferase** forms each **peptide bond**, and **termination** when a **release factor** reads a stop codon.
10. **Post-translational modification** finishes the job: **glycosylation**, **phosphorylation**, **disulfide bonds**, **proteolytic cleavage** (proinsulin becoming insulin), and **ubiquitin** tagging for destruction.

## 2.3 Organisation of the Eukaryotic Genome

1. The **genome** is all the genetic material of an organism. A complete eukaryotic genome is **nuclear plus mitochondrial**, plus **chloroplast** in photosynthetic organisms.
2. **Genome size does not correlate with biological complexity**, and it is **not proportional to gene number**. This is the counter-intuitive claim the chapter is built around.
3. **Prokaryotes have far higher gene density.** Eukaryotic genomes are mostly **non-coding intergenic DNA**, which is where the size goes.
4. DNA is packed in **three levels of condensation**: a **histone octamer** wrapped by **146 bp** makes a **nucleosome** and the **10-nm fibre**, **histone H1** and **linker DNA** give the **30-nm fibre**, and **scaffold proteins** with **looped domains** give the **300-nm fibre** and finally the **metaphase chromosome**.
5. Condensation exists to **prevent entanglement** and to allow **segregation** during division.
6. A **eukaryotic gene** is a **transcription unit** of **exons and introns**, plus **regulatory sequences** (**promoter**, **proximal** and **distal control elements**), plus the **5' and 3' UTRs**.
7. **Telomeres** are **TTAGGG repeats** with a **3' overhang** tucked into a **t-loop**. They **protect the chromosome end**, **prevent loss of genes**, and **limit replicative lifespan** (the **Hayflick limit**).
8. **Telomerase** rebuilds telomeres. It is active in **germ, stem and cancer cells** but not in most **somatic** cells, and that single fact is why cancer cells are effectively immortal.
9. The **centromere** is **heterochromatin** where the **kinetochore** assembles, holding sister chromatids together and driving **correct segregation**.

## 2.4 Control of Eukaryotic Gene Expression

1. Eukaryotic control differs from prokaryotic control for three structural reasons: DNA is wrapped in **nucleosomes**, genes are **not organised into operons**, and transcription and translation are **separated by the nuclear envelope**.
2. Control happens at **five levels**, and naming the level correctly is usually the first mark: **chromatin, transcriptional, post-transcriptional, translational, post-translational**.
3. At the **chromatin level**, **euchromatin** is accessible and **heterochromatin** is not. **Histone acetylation** by **HATs** removes positive charge, weakens the histone's grip on DNA and opens the chromatin; **HDACs** reverse it.
4. **DNA methylation** at **CpG islands** in promoters represses transcription, both directly and by recruiting HDACs.
5. At the **transcriptional level**, **activators** bind **enhancers**, the DNA **loops** back, and **mediator proteins** bridge the activator to the **transcription initiation complex**, pushing the rate above **basal**. **Repressors** binding **silencers** do the opposite.
6. **Tissue specificity comes from the combination** of control elements present in the gene and activators present in that cell, not from a single on/off switch.
7. At the **post-transcriptional level**, **alternative splicing** is the headline: one gene, several different proteins.
8. At the **translational level**, control runs through **mRNA half-life**, availability of **initiation factors**, **translational repressors**, and **RNA interference** by **miRNA** processed by **Dicer** and loaded into **RISC**.
9. At the **post-translational level**, proteins are switched on by modification and switched off by **proteasomal degradation**.

## 2.5 Gene and Chromosomal Mutations

1. A **mutation** is a change in the **base sequence** of DNA. **Germline** mutations are inherited; **somatic** mutations are not.
2. A **substitution** produces one of four outcomes: **missense**, **nonsense**, **silent** or **neutral**.
3. **Insertions and deletions** split on one test only: **a multiple of three** adds or removes whole amino acids with no frameshift, while **not a multiple of three** causes a **frameshift** and therefore either extensive missense or a **premature stop codon**.
4. Every mutation answer follows the same chain, and **it must start at the gene**: **base sequence → amino acid sequence → 3D conformation → protein function**. Starting at the amino acid loses the mark.
5. **Sickle cell anaemia** is the named case: a substitution in **β-globin** swapping **glutamate for valine**, creating a **hydrophobic patch** that makes haemoglobin aggregate at **low oxygen concentration**.
6. Causes divide into **spontaneous** (replication errors, **DNA slippage**) and **induced** by **mutagens**, which are either **physical** (**X-rays**, **UV**) or **chemical** (**base analogues**, **base-modifying agents**, **intercalating agents**).
7. **Chromosomal aberrations of structure** are **deletion, duplication, inversion and translocation**.
8. **Chromosomal aberrations of number** come from **non-disjunction**, giving **aneuploidy** (**monosomy** as in Turner, **trisomy** as in Down and Klinefelter), or whole extra sets in **polyploidy**.
9. **Screening** identifies risk in a population; **testing** diagnoses an individual. The named methods are **cell-free foetal DNA (NIPT)**, **amniocentesis** and **chorionic villus sampling**.

## 2.6 Molecular Techniques

1. **PCR** amplifies one specific DNA sequence **exponentially**, doubling it every cycle.
2. It needs five components: **DNA template**, **primers**, **dNTPs**, a **thermostable DNA polymerase**, and **buffer with Mg²⁺**.
3. Each cycle runs three steps at three temperatures: **denaturation**, **annealing**, **extension**.
4. **Specificity comes entirely from the primers.** Its **advantages** are sensitivity, speed and robustness; its **limitations** are contamination, replication infidelity, a ceiling on product size, the need to know flanking sequence, and that it works on nucleic acids only. The syllabus asks for these by name.
5. **Gel electrophoresis** separates fragments by **size**: DNA is **negatively charged** so it migrates to the **anode**, and **smaller fragments travel further** through the gel's pores.
6. A **DNA ladder** is run alongside because gels are **not directly comparable** to each other.
7. **Nucleic acid hybridisation** works because complementary single strands anneal. A **probe** is a short labelled single strand, and **stringency is controlled by temperature**.
8. **Southern blotting** chains the three together: separate by electrophoresis, **denature**, **transfer** to a membrane, **hybridise** with a labelled probe, then detect.

## 2.7 Bioethics

This chapter is three pages long and carries **one examinable learning outcome**. Read it once, do not cluster it.

1. **Values** are what you hold worthwhile, **morals** are those values expressed as conduct, and **ethics** is reasoning about what one should do and why.
2. The **four principles** are **respect for persons** (including **autonomy** and **informed consent**), **maximising benefits and minimising harms**, **justice**, and **care**.
3. The **decision framework** runs: ethical question → facts → stakeholders → principles → options and rebuttals → **decision with justification**. The justification is where the marks are.
4. The only application the syllabus examines is the **bioethics of genetic maternal screening for mutations, including trisomy-21**. Rehearse that one answer with both sides and a judgement.
