## PART A: DNA STRUCTURE AND REPLICATION

### 1. Every structural feature of DNA exists to deliver one of four requirements of hereditary material

- 1.1 The four: **store large amounts of information**, stay **chemically stable**, **replicate accurately**, still permit **variation**.
- 1.2 A **nucleotide** is a **pentose sugar**, a **base** and a **phosphate**. DNA uses **deoxyribose**, RNA **ribose**, whose **2' OH** makes RNA less stable.
- 1.3 Bases split into double-ring **purines** (**A, G**) and single-ring **pyrimidines** (**C, T, U**).
- 1.4 **Phosphodiester bonds** form by condensation between the **3' OH** of one sugar and the **5' phosphate** of the next, so the backbone runs **5' to 3'**. That directionality governs everything downstream.
- 1.5 The **double helix** is **two antiparallel strands**, backbone outside, bases inside, held by **hydrogen bonds**: **A-T 2, G-C 3**. **Purine always pairs with pyrimidine**, so width stays constant.
- 1.6 Sort features by the property each delivers: **hydrogen bonds, base stacking, backbone outside, histone packing** give **stability**; **complementary base pairing** gives **fidelity**.

### 2. Replication is semi-conservative, and two polymerase limitations dictate the whole mechanism

- 2.1 **Semi-conservative**: each daughter keeps one parental strand, proved by **Meselson and Stahl** with **¹⁵N/¹⁴N labelling** and **density-gradient centrifugation**.
- 2.2 Four stages: unwinding at **origins** by **helicase** (plus **SSB proteins**, **topoisomerase**), priming by **primase**, extension by **DNA polymerase**, sealing by **DNA ligase**.
- 2.3 Limitation one: polymerase **cannot start a chain from nothing**, which is why primers exist.
- 2.4 Limitation two: polymerase **only synthesises 5' to 3'**, which with antiparallelism forces a continuous **leading strand** and a discontinuous **lagging strand** of **Okazaki fragments**.
- 2.5 Fidelity comes from **proofreading** by polymerase's **3' to 5' exonuclease** activity.

### 3. The end replication problem falls straight out of that mechanism

- 3.1 The last **RNA primer** on the lagging strand is removed, and **no upstream strand offers a free 3' OH** for polymerase to extend.
- 3.2 So a linear chromosome **shortens every round**, which Part C picks up as telomeres.

## PART B: EUKARYOTIC GENE EXPRESSION

### 1. The central dogma runs one way, and RNA is the working copy that keeps DNA intact

- 1.1 **DNA to RNA to polypeptide**, one way. DNA is never used directly, because it must stay intact as the permanent store.
- 1.2 **RNA** differs from DNA on four counts: **ribose**, **uracil** for thymine, **single-stranded**, much shorter.
- 1.3 Three RNAs carry the roles: **mRNA** the message, **tRNA** the codon-to-amino-acid adaptor, **rRNA** the ribosome's structure and catalysis.
- 1.4 In **transcription**, **RNA polymerase** reads the **template strand 3' to 5'** and builds **5' to 3'**, placed at the **promoter** and **TATA box** by **general transcription factors**.
- 1.5 It runs **initiation, elongation, termination**, giving **pre-mRNA**.

### 2. Eukaryotes modify the transcript before it leaves the nucleus, and prokaryotes do not

- 2.1 A **5' cap** protects from nucleases, recruits the small ribosomal subunit and marks the transcript as mRNA.
- 2.2 **Splicing** by the **spliceosome** removes **introns**, joining the **exons**.
- 2.3 A **3' poly(A) tail** protects from nucleases, stabilises the template and enables export.

### 3. Translation is decoding, and the genetic code plus tRNA is how the decoding works

- 3.1 The code is **triplet, universal, non-overlapping and continuous, degenerate but unambiguous**: **64 codons, 61 coding, 3 stop**.
- 3.2 **tRNA** is the adaptor: an **anticodon** at one end, the amino acid at the **CCA end**, charged by **aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase** using **ATP**.
- 3.3 Translation uses the ribosome's **A, P and E sites**: **initiation** at the first **AUG**, **elongation** with **peptidyl transferase**, **termination** when a **release factor** reads a stop codon.
- 3.4 **Post-translational modification** finishes it: **glycosylation**, **phosphorylation**, **disulfide bonds**, **proteolytic cleavage** (proinsulin to insulin), **ubiquitin** tagging.

## PART C: ORGANISATION OF THE EUKARYOTIC GENOME

### 1. Genomes are compared on one fixed set of axes, and eukaryotic genomes are mostly non-coding

- 1.1 The **genome** is all the genetic material of an organism, and viral, prokaryotic and eukaryotic genomes compare on fixed axes: **DNA or RNA**, **single- or double-stranded**, **nucleotide number**, **linear or circular**, **introns or none**, **packing**.
- 1.2 **Prokaryotes have far higher gene density**; eukaryotic genomes are mostly **non-coding DNA**, and **genome size tracks neither complexity nor gene number**.

### 2. DNA is packed in three levels of condensation, so chromosomes can segregate without entangling

- 2.1 Level one: a **histone octamer** wrapped by **146 bp** gives a **nucleosome** and the **10-nm fibre**, held by **ionic bonds** to the negative **backbone**.
- 2.2 Level two: **histone H1** and **linker DNA** coil that into the **30-nm fibre**. Level three: **scaffold proteins** loop it into the **300-nm fibre**, then the chromosome.
- 2.3 The point of it is **segregation at anaphase without entanglement or breakage**.

### 3. Non-coding DNA is a list of six structures, each needing a structure and a function

- 3.1 A **eukaryotic gene** is a transcription unit of **exons and introns**, plus **promoter**, **enhancers**, **silencers** and the **UTRs**.
- 3.2 **Telomeres** are **TTAGGG repeats** with a **3' overhang** in a **t-loop**: they **protect the end**, **prevent gene loss** and **limit replicative lifespan** (**Hayflick limit**).
- 3.3 **Telomerase** does not prevent the end replication problem, it **restores length afterwards**. Active in **germ, stem and cancer cells**, not most **somatic** cells, hence cancer cell immortality.
- 3.4 The **centromere** is **heterochromatin** where the **kinetochore** assembles; without one, chromatids **segregate at random** and daughter cells gain or lose chromosomes.

## PART D: CONTROL OF EUKARYOTIC GENE EXPRESSION

### 1. Control happens at five levels, and naming the level correctly is usually the first mark

- 1.1 The five, in order: **chromatin, transcriptional, post-transcriptional, translational, post-translational**.
- 1.2 Three structural reasons it differs from prokaryotic control: DNA wrapped in **nucleosomes**, genes **not sharing one promoter**, and a **nuclear envelope** separating transcription from translation.

### 2. At the chromatin and transcriptional levels, control is about physical access to the promoter

- 2.1 **Euchromatin** is accessible, **heterochromatin** is not.
- 2.2 **Histone acetylation** by **HATs** neutralises the positive charge on **lysine**, weakening the histone's grip, opening chromatin, raising transcription. **HDACs** reverse every link.
- 2.3 **DNA methylation** at **CpG islands** in promoters represses transcription directly and by recruiting **HDACs**, connecting the two chromatin mechanisms.
- 2.4 **Activators** bind **enhancers**, DNA **loops** back, **mediator proteins** bridge to the **transcription initiation complex**, lifting rate above **basal**. **Repressors** on **silencers** reverse it. Activators and repressors are **proteins**; enhancers and silencers are **DNA**.
- 2.5 **Tissue specificity comes from the combination** of control elements in the gene and activators in that cell, not one on/off switch.

### 3. The last three levels control the transcript and then the finished protein

- 3.1 **Post-transcriptional**: **alternative splicing** lets one gene make several proteins, and capping and polyadenylation are themselves regulated.
- 3.2 **Translational**: **mRNA half-life**, set by **poly(A) tail length** and **3' UTR** sequences, plus availability of **initiation factors** and **translational repressors**.
- 3.3 **Post-translational**: proteins switched on by **biochemical modification**, switched off by **proteasomal degradation**.

## PART E: GENE AND CHROMOSOMAL MUTATIONS

### 1. Every mutation answer runs the same chain, and it must start at the gene

- 1.1 The chain: **base sequence, then amino acid sequence, then 3D conformation, then protein function**. Starting at the amino acid loses the mark.
- 1.2 A **mutation** is a change in **base sequence**; **germline** mutations are inherited, **somatic** ones are not.
- 1.3 A **substitution** gives one of four outcomes: **missense**, **nonsense**, **silent**, **neutral**.
- 1.4 **Additions and deletions** split on one test: **a multiple of three** adds or removes whole amino acids, anything else **frameshifts**, giving extensive missense or a **premature stop codon**.

### 2. Sickle cell anaemia is the named disease, and it is that chain run end to end

- 2.1 A substitution in **β-globin** swaps **glutamate for valine** at residue six, turning a **hydrophilic** residue **hydrophobic**.
- 2.2 That leaves a **hydrophobic patch**, so haemoglobin **aggregates into fibres at low oxygen** and the cell sickles.
- 2.3 Sickled cells **block capillaries** and are **fragile**, cutting oxygen-carrying capacity. Aggregation **reverses at high oxygen**, so symptoms are episodic.

### 3. Chromosomal aberrations split into structure and number, and each has its own consequence

- 3.1 **Structural**: **deletion** loses information and unmasks recessives; **duplication** creates redundancy; **inversion** and **translocation** lose nothing but cause position effects and aberrant gametes.
- 3.2 **Numerical**: **non-disjunction** in meiosis I or II gives **aneuploidy**, the named case being **trisomy 21 (Down syndrome)**, **95%** of it from non-disjunction.
- 3.3 **Screening** identifies risk across a population, **testing** diagnoses an individual: **cell-free foetal DNA (NIPT)** screens, **amniocentesis** and **chorionic villus sampling** diagnose.

## PART F: MOLECULAR TECHNIQUES

### 1. PCR amplifies one sequence exponentially, and the primers alone make it specific

- 1.1 Five components with reasons: **template**; **primers**, since polymerase cannot start de novo; **dNTPs**; **thermostable polymerase**, surviving repeated 95 °C; **buffer with Mg²⁺**, the cofactor.
- 1.2 Three steps per cycle: **denaturation at 95 °C**, **annealing at about 54 °C**, **extension at 72 °C**. Product **doubles every cycle**.
- 1.3 The syllabus asks for both by name. **Advantages**: sensitivity, speed, robustness. **Limitations**: contamination, replication infidelity, a size ceiling, needing the flanking sequence, nucleic acids only.

### 2. Gel electrophoresis separates by size, and the reasoning is a four-link chain

- 2.1 **Phosphate groups are negatively charged**, so DNA migrates to the **anode**.
- 2.2 **Charge density is constant**, so charge is not a variable and size is the only one left.
- 2.3 The gel is a **molecular sieve**, so **smaller fragments travel further**, and a **DNA ladder** runs alongside because gels are **not comparable**.

### 3. Hybridisation is complementary base pairing used as a search tool, and Southern blotting chains it to the gel

- 3.1 **Nucleic acid hybridisation** is the annealing of complementary single strands, and a **probe** is a short labelled single strand.
- 3.2 **Stringency is controlled by temperature**: high keeps only perfect matches, lower also finds related but non-identical sequences.
- 3.3 **Southern blotting** chains them: separate on a gel, **denature to single strands**, **transfer** to a membrane, **hybridise** with a labelled probe, detect. Candidates omit the denaturation.

## One-line mental map

**DNA and replication:** four requirements of hereditary material shape every structural feature, and two polymerase limitations force primers, a lagging strand and the end replication problem.

**Gene expression:** DNA to RNA to polypeptide one way, capping, splicing and polyadenylation finish the transcript, and tRNA plus the ribosome decode triplets into peptide bonds.

**Genome organisation:** genomes compare on fixed axes, three levels of condensation let chromosomes segregate cleanly, and non-coding DNA carries the structural and regulatory work.

**Control:** five levels from chromatin to post-translational, promoter access is the theme, and combinations of activators and control elements give tissue specificity.

**Mutations:** base sequence to amino acid to conformation to function is the only chain, and aberrations scale that logic up to whole chromosomes.

**Techniques:** primers alone make PCR specific, gels separate by size because charge density is constant, and a labelled probe turns that gel into Southern blotting.
