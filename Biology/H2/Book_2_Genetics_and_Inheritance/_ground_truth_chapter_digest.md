### 2. Content node map

- **Role of DNA as the hereditary material** (p.4)
  - Properties any hereditary material must have: high storage capacity and chemical stability, accurate replication, capacity for variation
  - Seven lines of evidence that DNA is the hereditary material (chromosome analysis, metabolic stability, constancy within a cell, mutagen correlation, Griffith 1928, Avery 1944, Hershey-Chase 1952)
- **Structure of DNA** (p.5–19)
  - Nucleotides as the building blocks: pentose sugar, nitrogenous base, phosphate group
  - Pentose sugar: ribose vs deoxyribose, and the consequence of the 2' OH for RNA stability
  - Nitrogenous bases: purines vs pyrimidines, the four bases in DNA vs RNA
  - Nucleoside then nucleotide: glycosidic bond, then phosphoester bond; mono/di/triphosphates
  - Dinucleotide and polynucleotide formation via phosphodiester bonds; the sugar-phosphate backbone
  - Polarity and directionality: 5' phosphate end and 3' hydroxyl end
  - Road to discovery: X-ray diffraction (Wilkins, Franklin) and Chargaff's rules
  - The double helix, its eight main features (two antiparallel strands, 2 nm diameter, backbone outside, bases inside, hydrogen-bonded base pairs, 0.34 nm stacking, 3.4 nm per turn, major and minor grooves)
  - Why complementary base pairs form: steric restriction and hydrogen-bond geometry
  - Packing of DNA in eukaryotic chromosomes (preview only, five levels)
  - Stable, invariant storage: which structural features stabilise the helix and which keep the sequence invariant
- **Replication of DNA** (p.20–35)
  - DNA strands as templates; Watson and Crick's copying insight
  - Three competing models: semi-conservative, conservative, dispersive
  - The Meselson-Stahl experiment: 15N/14N labelling, CsCl density-gradient centrifugation, generation 0, 1 and 2 band patterns, and how each result eliminates a model
  - Mechanism, four stages: location of origins of replication, separation of parental strands, synthesis of RNA primers, synthesis of daughter strands
    - Origins: A-T rich sequences, initiator proteins, replication bubble and forks, single origin in prokaryotes vs multiple in eukaryotes and why
    - Separation: helicase, single-strand DNA binding proteins, topoisomerase
    - Priming: limitation 1 of DNA polymerase (no de novo synthesis), primase, the free 3' OH
    - Synthesis: complementary base pairing, proofreading via 3'→5' exonuclease, bond formation and the pyrophosphate energy coupling, limitation 2 (5'→3' only), leading vs lagging strand, Okazaki fragments, DNA ligase
  - The end replication problem: why linear chromosomes shorten each round

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 The chapter cannot decide whether the bond DNA polymerase makes is a phosphodiester bond or a phosphoester bond.** This one matters because a mark scheme will accept only one of them.

- Page 9: "Two nucleotides join to form a dinucleotide by condensation between the 5'-phosphate group of one nucleotide and the 3'-hydroxyl group of the other to form a **phosphodiester** bond."
- Page 11, Concept Check 1: nucleotides to nucleic acids, "Condensation reaction / Forms **phosphodiester** bonds."
- Page 33: "The addition of nucleotide (i.e. dNTP) to the growing daughter DNA strand requires the formation of a **phosphoester** bond between the free 3' hydroxyl group of the last nucleotide in the growing strand and the free 5' phosphate group of the incoming dNTP."
- Page 34: "DNA ligase - a linking enzyme - catalyses the formation of a **phosphoester** bond between the 3' end of each new Okazaki fragment and the 5' end of the growing daughter DNA strand."

Same chemical linkage, two names, in one chapter. Chapter 2.2 then uses "phosphodiester" for the equivalent RNA bond throughout (pages 12 and 15). The correct term for the internucleotide linkage is phosphodiester. Ethan should write phosphodiester every time and reserve phosphoester for the single sugar-to-phosphate ester inside one nucleotide.

**5.2 "10 bases per turn" versus "10 base pairs per turn."** These differ by a factor of two.

- Page 12 (conclusions from X-ray diffraction): DNA "has 10 bases to each complete turn of the helix."
- Page 16, feature 7: "The double-helix makes a complete turn every 10 base pairs, so each turn is 3.4 nm (34 Å)."
- Page 3 concept map: "-10 nucleotides per turn of helix (3.4nm)"

Two of the three say bases or nucleotides, which would mean 5 base pairs per turn. The correct figure, and the one consistent with 3.4 nm per turn at 0.34 nm per rise, is 10 base pairs, i.e. 20 nucleotides. Only page 16 is right.

**5.3 Duplicate figure number: Fig. 19 is used twice.** Page 17 has "Fig. 19: Base pairing in DNA." Page 35's end-replication-problem graphic carries the internal label "Fig. 19: Illustration and explanation of the end-replication problem" while the caption underneath the same graphic reads "Fig. 37: Illustration and explanation of the end replication problem." One figure, two numbers, and one of those numbers already belongs to a different figure eighteen pages earlier.

**5.4 The five-level DNA packing scheme has six rows.** Figure 20 on page 18 lists Level 1 (2 nm), Level 2 (10 nm), Level 3 (30 nm), Level 4 (300 nm), then an unlabelled row for "Condensed section of metaphase chromosome, 700nm", then Level 5 (1400 nm). The page 3 concept map gives a clean five-level version and omits 700 nm from the numbering: "Level 4: Looped domains (300nm) / Level 5: Fully condensed chromosomes (1400nm)". If Ethan is asked to name the five levels he should use the concept map's list, not Fig. 20's six rows.

**5.5 Garbled sentence in the page 3 concept map.** "NB: Only fully condensed chromosomes exist only during metaphase, and hence are always found as pairs of sister chromatids." The word "only" appears twice and the sentence does not parse. `[INFERRED]` The intended claim is that fully condensed chromosomes exist only during metaphase.

**5.6 Missing verb, page 27.** "At each end of a replication bubble Y-shaped structure called a replication fork, where the new strands of DNA are synthesised." A verb is missing. As printed this is not a usable definition of a replication fork, so Ethan should not memorise this sentence as written.

**5.7 Replication timing stated two different ways.** Page 25 says human replication "takes the cell just a few hours to copy all these DNA." Page 29 says "Replication takes approximately eight hours in human cells with multiple origins of replication." Not a hard contradiction, but if a question asks for a figure, use eight hours, since that is the one attached to the multiple-origins argument.

**5.8 Oversimplification of DNA repair, page 19.** "if the base sequence in one of the two strands is accidentally altered, the cell discards the damaged strand. It then makes a perfectly good strand by using the remaining intact strand as a template." Repair excises the damaged region, not the whole strand. Harmless for the redundancy argument, but do not write "the cell discards the damaged strand" in an answer about excision repair.

**5.9 Minor spelling and typography.** Page 3 concept map: "Messelson-Stahl" (double s) against "Meselson-Stahl" everywhere in the body; "DNA polymerase cannot synthesis a new DNA strand de novo" (should be synthesise). Page 1 has a stray footnote marker: "the Meselson-Stahl experiment21 by Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl." Page 12 renders the Chargaff base-pair notation as "(A=T, GC)" where the triple-bond symbol for G≡C has dropped out.

Everything else in this chapter checks out. The Chargaff table, the Meselson-Stahl predicted-versus-observed logic, the helicase/SSB/topoisomerase division of labour, the two DNA polymerase limitations, and the end-replication-problem mechanism are all internally consistent and factually sound.

### 6. Load-bearing vs skippable

**Load-bearing (this is where the marks are):**

- The eight main features of the double helix on pages 15 to 16. This is a stock structured-question answer and the numbers (2 nm diameter, 0.34 nm stacking, 3.4 nm per turn, 10 base pairs per turn, 2 hydrogen bonds A-T, 3 hydrogen bonds G-C) are examinable as recall.
- The structure-to-function chain in section 2.2.5, page 19: which features make DNA *stable* (hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic stacking, backbone outside, bases inside, histone packing in eukaryotes only) versus which make the sequence *invariant* (complementary base pairing, so information is redundant). Examiners love this split because it separates candidates who memorised a list from candidates who understood it.
- The full replication mechanism, pages 27 to 34, named enzyme by named enzyme. Helicase, SSB proteins, topoisomerase, primase, DNA polymerase, DNA ligase, each with its function and, where stated, its energy requirement.
- The two DNA polymerase limitations, and the fact that leading versus lagging strand synthesis is a *consequence* of limitation 2 plus antiparallelism. This causal chain is the answer to "explain why the lagging strand is synthesised discontinuously."
- The end replication problem, page 35. It has its own line in the syllabus, outcome 2(b), and the mark-bearing detail is precise: the final RNA primer at the end of the lagging strand is removed, and there is no upstream strand with a free 3' OH for DNA polymerase to extend from.
- Meselson-Stahl, pages 22 to 23. Not just the result but the reasoning: generation 1 all hybrid eliminates conservative, generation 2 showing light plus hybrid eliminates dispersive. Concept Check 2 on page 24 is a real exam question shape (sketch the bands for generations 2 and 3).
- Prokaryote versus eukaryote origins of replication, page 28 to 29, including the *reason* for multiple origins (speed, given chromosome length).

- Section 2.2.4, DNA packing, page 18. The chapter says outright: "You will be formally taught DNA packing in Organisation of Eukaryotic Genome." Drill it in 2.3, not here.

### 2. Content node map

- **Introduction to gene expression** (p.5–6)
  - Why DNA is not used directly: it must stay intact as the permanent store
  - The Central Dogma: unidirectional DNA to RNA to polypeptide
  - Gene expression as a two-stage process, transcription then translation
- **Gene expression through RNA** (p.6–9)
  - Similarities between RNA and DNA (five points)
  - Differences between RNA and DNA (eleven-row comparison table)
  - Roles of the five RNA types: mRNA, tRNA, rRNA, snRNA, siRNA/miRNA
- **Transcription** (p.9–16)
  - Components of the transcription machinery: gene, RNA polymerase, general transcription factors, ribonucleotides
    - Gene anatomy: promoter (TATA box, transcription start site), coding region (template vs non-template strand, transcription bubble), termination sequence
    - RNA polymerase: substrate, direction of reading, direction of synthesis, the three eukaryotic RNA polymerases
    - General transcription factors: two stated jobs, positioning and release
  - The three stages: initiation (transcription initiation complex, unwinding, first phosphodiester bond), elongation (bubble movement, base pairing, bond formation, re-annealing and proofreading), termination (terminator transcribed, cleavage 10 to 35 nucleotides downstream of AAUAAA)
- **Post-transcriptional modification of mRNA** (p.17–19)
  - 5' cap: what it is and three functions
  - RNA splicing: exons and introns, spliceosome, snRNPs and snRNAs
  - 3' poly(A) tail: endonuclease cleavage, poly(A) polymerase, roughly 200 A nucleotides, two functions
- **The genetic code** (p.20–23)
  - Why a triplet code (the 4 vs 16 vs 64 argument)
  - General features: 64 codons, 61 coding, 3 stop
  - Key features, the "TUND" set: triplet, universal, non-overlapping and continuous, degenerate but unambiguous; plus wobble; plus punctuation codons
- **Translation** (p.24–38)
  - Components of the translation machinery
    - Mature mRNA: coding region and the two UTRs
    - tRNA: cloverleaf secondary structure, L-shaped tertiary structure, anticodon, CCA stem, adaptor role
    - Amino acid activation: aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, ester linkage, ATP hydrolysis, 20 synthetases
    - rRNA: synthesis and processing, structural and catalytic roles
    - Ribosome: subunit sizes, mRNA binding site, A, P and E sites
    - Translation factors: initiation, elongation and release factors, GTP
  - The three stages: initiation (eIFs, 5' cap recognition, scanning for AUG, initiator tRNA into P site, LSU joins), elongation and translocation (codon recognition, peptide bond formation by peptidyl transferase, translocation by one codon), termination (release factor, water added, polypeptide released, complex dissociates)
  - Polyribosomes and their advantage
  - Free vs bound ribosomes: cytosolic proteins vs targeted proteins, signal peptide and SRP
- **Post-translational modification of polypeptides** (p.39–40)
  - Attaching biochemical functional groups (glycosylation, reversible phosphorylation)
  - Structural changes (disulfide linkages)
  - Removing amino acid sequences (proteolytic cleavage, proinsulin to insulin)
  - Attaching ubiquitin for proteasomal degradation

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 Which polymerase makes 5S rRNA? The chapter answers twice, differently.**

- Page 12: "RNA pol I transcribes the genes encoding rRNA. It is found in the nucleolus." and "RNA pol III transcribes the genes encoding tRNA."
- Page 29: "The fourth, 5S rRNA is synthesized from a separate cluster of genes (by a different polymerase, RNA pol III), and does not require chemical modification."

Page 12 says all rRNA genes go to pol I. Page 29 says one of the four rRNAs goes to pol III. Page 29 is the correct one. If a question asks which polymerase transcribes rRNA genes, the safe answer is pol I for 18S, 5.8S and 28S, and pol III for 5S.

**5.2 Exons are defined as protein-coding, then the chapter says part of them is not translated.**

- Page 18: "Exons, which are protein-coding sequences in the gene"
- Page 25: mature mRNA contains "Untranslated regions (UTRs): Additional sequence at the 5' end, preceding the start codon... and following the stop codon"

Mature mRNA is built only from exons, so if every exon were protein-coding there could be no UTRs. The precise definition is that exons are the sequences retained in the mature mRNA, of which only the region between start and stop codons is translated. Ethan should be aware that the school's exon definition is the loose one and that a Cambridge answer wanting precision would say "sequences that are retained in the mature mRNA."

**5.3 Cross-reference errors around the poly(A) figure.** The poly(A) tail figure is Fig. 17 on page 19, but three separate cross-references point at Fig. 16, which is the splicing figure.

- Page 11: "it codes for a polyadenylation signal sequence (AAUAAA) in the pre-mRNA (KIV: Post-transcriptional modification in **Fig. 16**)"
- Page 16: "codes for a polyadenylation signal sequence (AAUAAA) (KIV: Post-transcriptional modification in **Fig. 16**)"
- Page 19: "catalysed by the enzyme poly (A) - polymerase (**Fig. 16**)"

And in the other direction, page 18 says "Both intron and exon sequences are transcribed into pre-mRNA (Fig. 15 & **Fig. 17**)" where Fig. 17 is the poly(A) figure and Fig. 16 is the splicing one. Every one of these is off by one.

**5.4 Duplicate figure number: Fig. 31 appears twice**, on page 34 ("Elongation of translation") and again on page 35 ("Steps involved in elongation and translocation"). Same problem as Fig. 19 in chapter 2.1.

**5.5 Fig. 37 on page 40 has no caption.** The line reads "Fig. 37:" and stops.

**5.6 Probable factual error about the initiator tRNA, page 33.** "The initiator tRNA has a unique anti-codon loop that is distinct from that of the tRNA that normally carries methionine." The Fig. 30 caption on the same page says the initiator tRNA carries "the anticodon UAC", which is the same anticodon the elongator methionine tRNA carries. Initiator and elongator Met-tRNAs differ in other structural features and in which factors recognise them, not in the anticodon. As printed, page 33 contradicts its own figure caption. Do not reproduce the "unique anticodon loop" claim in an answer.

**5.7 Loose wording in peptide bond formation, page 34.** Two consecutive bullets say "The methionine is thus transferred to the 2nd amino acid carried by the aminoacyl-tRNA at the A site" and then "The ester bond between the initial methionine and the tRNA is broken to release the initial methionine (in the P site)." Read literally, the second bullet says methionine is released, which would contradict the first. What actually happens is that the ester bond to the P-site tRNA breaks *because* the methionine has been transferred. Ethan should write "transferred", never "released", for this step.

**5.8 Wobble example does not state anticodon polarity, page 23.** "phenylalanine tRNA with the anticodon AAG recognises not only the complementary mRNA codon UUC but also UUU." This only works if the anticodon is read 3'-AAG-5'. Written 5'-AAG-3' it would pair with a leucine codon. The chapter never states the convention. If Ethan is asked to write an anticodon he must label the ends, because an unlabelled anticodon is read 5' to 3' by default and would be marked wrong.

**5.9 Ambiguous phrasing in the similarities list, page 6.** "Both RNA and DNA make use of 3 nitrogenous bases: adenine (A) and guanine (G), which are purines, and cytosine (C), which is a pyrimidine." Read as written this says each nucleic acid uses three bases, which is wrong; each uses four. The intended claim is that they share three bases in common. Also on that page, "A strand of RNA and a strand of DNA strand has a sugar-phosphate backbone" has a stray duplicated word.

**5.10 Timing of splicing, page 18.** "RNA splicing occurs after the release of pre-mRNA from RNA polymerase." Page 18 also says capping happens "As soon as a length of the pre-mRNA has been transcribed", so the chapter treats capping as co-transcriptional and splicing as post-transcriptional. Splicing is in fact largely co-transcriptional too. This is a simplification rather than a contradiction, and for A-level purposes the school's version is safe to reproduce.

**5.11 Simplification, page 30 and Fig. 28.** "The large subunit (60S) has 3 binding sites for tRNA." The A, P and E sites actually span the interface between both subunits, which the chapter half-acknowledges on page 30 when it says rRNA is "the main constituent of the A and P sites and of the interface between the large and small ribosomal subunits." The school teaches the sites as belonging to the large subunit and its own figure labels them that way, so reproduce the school version, but do not be thrown if a source says otherwise. `[INFERRED]` this is a deliberate teaching simplification, not a typo.

**5.12 Minor spelling inconsistency.** Page 4 writes "proteosome"; page 40 writes "proteasome". The second is correct.

Beyond these, the chapter is solid. The transcription and translation step-by-step tables, the genetic code features, the tRNA structure section and the post-translational modification list are all internally consistent.

> **Learning Outcomes**

### 2. Content node map

- **Introduction and the genome concept** (p.5–7)
  - What a genome is, and that viruses may have DNA or RNA genomes
  - Genes carried on chromosomes at fixed loci; diploid vs haploid
  - What a complete eukaryotic genome comprises: nuclear, mitochondrial, and (in photosynthetic organisms) chloroplast
  - The human nuclear genome: 3 x 10^9 bp, 22 autosomes plus one sex chromosome
- **Complexity of the eukaryotic genome** (p.8–10)
  - More complex organisms tend to have larger genomes (holds across the prokaryote-to-eukaryote span, breaks down among higher eukaryotes)
  - No correlation between biological complexity and gene number; genome size is not proportional to gene number
  - Prokaryotic genomes have much higher gene density than eukaryotic ones
  - Higher eukaryotes have lower gene density, because of the large proportion of non-coding intergenic DNA
- **Overview of DNA in the eukaryotic genome** (p.11–14)
  - The composition breakdown: gene and gene-related DNA vs intergenic DNA vs repetitive DNA
  - Packing of DNA, three levels of condensation
    - First level: histone octamer, 146 bp, nucleosome core, beads on a string, 10-nm fibre
    - Second level: histone H1 and linker DNA, 30-nm fibre or solenoid
    - Third level: scaffold proteins, looped domains, 300-nm fibre, metaphase chromosome, 700-nm chromatid
  - Role of condensation: preventing entanglement and enabling segregation
- **Organisation of the eukaryotic gene** (p.15–18)
  - Transcription unit: exons and introns
  - Non-coding regulatory sequences: promoter, proximal control elements, distal control elements (enhancers with activators, silencers with repressors)
  - Untranslated regions: 5' UTR and 3' UTR, defined at the mRNA level
- **Organisation of intergenic DNA** (p.19–20)
  - Repetitive DNA and tandem arrays
  - Satellite DNA and its three length classes, and where it sits (heterochromatin, centromeres, telomeres)
  - Centromere vs telomere comparison table
- **Telomere** (p.21–27)
  - Structure: TTAGGG repeats, G-rich strand, 3' overhang, t-loop
  - Four functions: protection, stability, preventing gene loss, regulating replicative senescence
  - The end-replication problem restated
  - Replicative cell senescence and the Hayflick limit
  - Telomerase: what it is, what it does and does not do, its two components, its five-step mechanism
  - Telomerase activity in somatic vs germ vs stem vs cancer cells, and cancer immortality
- **Centromere** (p.28–30)
  - Structure: position, alpha satellite DNA, heterochromatin, centromere-specific nucleosomes, kinetochore assembly
  - Three functions: sister chromatid adhesion, kinetochore formation, proper chromosome segregation

**5.1 The chapter claims learning outcome 2(d) and then only delivers one third of it.** Outcome 2(d) on page 1 requires him to "describe the structure and organisation of viral, prokaryotic and eukaryotic genomes (including DNA/RNA, single-/double-stranded, number of nucleotides, packing of DNA, linearity/circularity and presence/absence of introns)." - Page 17: "Prokaryote genomes are arranged in operons where multiple genes are clustered together under the control of a single promoter and regulatory region. There are no introns present and post-transcriptional modifications are not necessary prior to translation."
- Page 20: "Prokaryote genomes are circular in nature. Hence, they will not contain telomeres at the ends of their chromosomes nor centromeres within their chromosome."

**5.2 Satellite DNA is taught in detail even though the chapter's own learning outcome says it is not required.** Page 1 quotes 2(h) with the exclusion "(knowledge of transposons, satellite DNA, pseudo-genes and duplication of segments is not required)". Then section 4.2.1 on page 19 gives satellite DNA a full treatment with its three length classes, and the page 4 overview map labels the satellite DNA branch "examples in syllabus". Ethan should know the phrase "tandem repetitive, non-coding satellite DNA" because it describes centromeres and telomeres, but should not spend time on the regular / mini / micro satellite classification.

**5.3 Control elements are placed inside the gene by one figure and outside the gene by another.**

- Page 11, Fig. 8: "Regulatory sequences: Distal control elements (Enhancer & Silencer), Proximal control elements" sit under the branch headed "**Intergenic DNA (non-coding)**".

Do not write "control elements are intergenic DNA" in an answer, because the chapter's own Fig. 15 draws them flanking the gene as part of it.

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 The chapter's definition of "mutation" contradicts the chapter's own content, twice, within four lines.** Page 4: "Such a change is called a mutation, **an alteration to an organism's characteristics that is inherited**, due to a change in the genetic material of a cell."

- It says a mutation alters an organism's characteristics. Three pages later the chapter teaches **silent mutations** ("the function of the protein is not altered") and **neutral mutations** ("no change in the overall three-dimensional conformation of the protein and hence the function of the protein is not altered"). Neither alters characteristics.
- It says a mutation is inherited. Four lines below, on the same page: "**Somatic mutation**... These mutations are **not inherited** by the progeny and hence not passed on to the next generation."

A mutation is a change in the DNA base sequence. Whether it changes the phenotype, and whether it is heritable, are separate questions. This definition should not be memorised as written. `[INFERRED]` A safe replacement that stays close to the chapter's vocabulary: "a permanent change in the DNA base sequence of a cell."

**5.2 In-frame insertions and deletions are wrongly labelled missense and nonsense mutations.** This is the biggest conceptual error in the chapter and it appears in three places.

- Page 5: "Addition or deletion of deoxynucleotides in multiples of 3 results in: **missense mutation**, i.e. an mRNA codon was added or deleted and the resulting polypeptide has an amino acid added or deleted respectively. OR **nonsense mutation**, i.e. a stop codon was added."
- Page 12, Table 1, row "Nucleotide Addition (or Deletion) in multiples of three": "**Missense** - changes one amino acid" and "**Nonsense** - addition of a stop codon."
- Page 3, the summary concept map, carries the same structure.

The chapter's own definition on page 4 says a missense mutation is "**a nucleotide substitution** in a DNA sequence" that "results in the translation of a different amino acid", and a nonsense mutation is "**a nucleotide substitution**" that changes a codon into a stop codon. An in-frame insertion of three nucleotides does not substitute anything and does not change one amino acid into a different one. It **adds** an amino acid. Calling that missense contradicts the definition on the facing page.

Notice that Fig. 6(C) on page 10, which shows the same event, describes it correctly and never uses the word missense: "2 additional glutamines (Gln) are found in the polypeptide sequence." The figure is right and the prose and table are wrong.

For an answer, describe an in-frame indel by what it does (one or more amino acids inserted or removed, reading frame preserved, downstream sequence unchanged) rather than by the label the notes attach to it.

**5.3 The sickle-cell substitution is stated backwards relative to the strand it names.** Page 13: "Substitution of a **thymine for an adenine** at one position in the Hb gene (**template strand**)".

The facts: the sixth codon of the beta-globin mRNA changes from GAG (glutamate) to GUG (valine). On the coding, non-template strand that is GAG to GTG, so A is replaced by T. On the **template** strand it is CTC to CAC, so **T is replaced by A**. Under the standard reading of "substitution of X for Y" (X replaces Y), the sentence describes A being replaced by T, which is the coding-strand change, not the template-strand change the sentence claims to describe.

`[INFERRED]` Either the strand label or the base order is wrong. The safe answer is to state the codon change directly: GAG to GUG in the mRNA, glutamate to valine at the sixth residue of the beta-globin chain, and to name the strand only if the question supplies it.

**5.4 The polymerase error rate contradicts chapter 2.1 by four orders of magnitude.**

- Chapter 2.1, page 25: "The mutation rate, approximately **1 nucleotide change per 10^9 nucleotides** each time the DNA is replicated."
- Chapter 2.5, page 15: "DNA polymerase make mistakes at a rate of about **1 in every 100 000 nucleotides**. With our 6 billion base pairs in each diploid human cell, that would be about 120 000 mistakes every time a cell divides."

`[INFERRED]` These are almost certainly measuring different things. 1 in 10^5 is the raw insertion error rate of the polymerase before correction, and 1 in 10^9 is the final mutation rate after proofreading and mismatch repair. Chapter 2.5 does go on to describe both correction systems, so its internal logic works. Neither chapter states which figure is which, so if a question asks for "the mutation rate" the 10^9 figure is the one to give, and the 10^5 figure should be described as the polymerase's uncorrected error rate. The arithmetic in the 2.5 passage checks out: 6 x 10^9 base pairs is 1.2 x 10^10 nucleotides, and one error per 10^5 gives 120,000.

**5.5 Two different definitions of genetic screening on the same page.** Page 24 opens with "Genetic screening is the analysis of a person's DNA to check for presence of one or more alleles that are associated with disease", which would include testing a symptomatic patient. Section 6.1 on the same page then says "When people who are **free of the symptoms** of a disease are tested like this, it is known as genetic screening", which excludes symptomatic patients. Since outcome 2(m) is specifically about screening, the discriminating definition matters. `[INFERRED]` The second is the one that does the work: screening tests people without symptoms, testing confirms a suspected diagnosis.

**5.6 Table 1's neutral mutation row loses half the definition.** Page 8 gives two routes to a neutral mutation: "the substitution of the original amino acid with an amino acid of similar physical and chemical properties" **or** "the substitution of an amino acid residue that is non-essential to that protein's structure and function". Table 1 on page 12 records only the first: "Neutral – changes to one amino acid with similar physical and chemical properties." A student revising from the table alone would lose the second route.

**5.7 The frameshift read-through outcome is only mentioned once.** Page 8 says a frameshift may "result in a **read-through of the normal stop codon**, resulting in polypeptides of altered lengths." Neither the summary map on page 3 nor Table 1 on page 12 mentions read-through. Both list only extensive missense and premature nonsense. Read-through gives a longer protein, which is a distinct and testable outcome.

**5.8 The Fig. 10 DNA slippage labels are confusing.** The two outcome labels read "Insertion of repeats in daughter strand" and "Deletion of repeats in parental strand". Slippage in the parental strand causes a **deletion in the daughter strand**, not a deletion in the parental strand. `[INFERRED]` The labels appear to describe which strand slipped rather than which strand ends up altered, but as printed they read as the latter.

**5.9 Dated and clinically loaded description of Down syndrome.** Page 27: "Patients with Down Syndrome often have decreased intelligence, **increased unselfconsciousness, openness and affection**". Page 22: "they have **low intelligence level**." This is the kind of characterisation that current Cambridge mark schemes avoid, and it sits awkwardly beside the chapter's own bioethics table on page 28, which warns that screening "contributes to the devaluation of people with such lives." `[INFERRED]` Use neutral clinical language (intellectual disability, characteristic facial features, congenital heart defects) rather than the booklet's wording.

**5.10 The Singapore data is 27 years old.** Fig. 23 on page 27 plots Down syndrome live births versus abortions "from 1993 to 1998" and the text says screening "has significantly reduced the rate of Down Syndrome live births **over the years** in Singapore". For a 2025 to 2026 booklet that is a very long extrapolation from a six-year window that ended before NIPT existed. Not an error, but do not quote it as current.

Page 27: "The screening tests for Down Syndrome **was** offered to all **pregnancy** mothers."
