## Structural fact to absorb before anything else

**Book 9 is not a Core Idea. It is Extension Topic A, and it has its own alphabet.** The learning outcomes are lettered (a) to (k) and belong to no Core Idea. That is a change of habit from Books 1 to 3, where every outcome carried a Core Idea number. There is no "2(n)" style reference anywhere in this Book. `[OFFICIAL]`

**The school prints the outcome block four separate times**, at the front of 9.0 and again at the head of chapters 9.5, 9.6 and 9.7. Those four printings are identical to each other. `[SCHOOL]`

**But 9.0 also prints a fifth, different block**, headed "LEARNING OUTCOMES FOR SECTION 1 TO SECTION 4", whose (a) and (d) do not match the current syllabus. ## 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 The Section 1 to 4 outcome block is an outdated version of outcomes (a) and (d), and it is printed inside the current Book.** `[OFFICIAL]` on the syllabus wording, `[SCHOOL]` on the printing.

| | Cover block, and the 9477 syllabus | "Section 1 to Section 4" block, printed p.1 |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | "describe the specific (adaptive) immune system, including **active, passive, naturally acquired and artificially acquired** immunity, and the non-specific (innate) immune system" | "describe the specific (adaptive) and non-specific (innate) immune systems including **active and passive, natural and acquired** immunity" |
| (d) | "explain how **somatic recombination, hyper-mutation and class switching** result in millions of different antibody molecules" | "explain how **genetic recombination during development** results in millions of different antibody molecules (including somatic recombination, hyper-mutation and class switching)" |

Two things follow, and both change what to learn. First, the current (a) names **four** things, and they combine as a two-by-two grid: naturally acquired active, naturally acquired passive, artificially acquired active, artificially acquired passive. The old block's "active and passive, natural and acquired" reads as if "natural" and "acquired" were the opposed pair, which they are not; acquired immunity is the umbrella term for the whole adaptive system in this Book's own §7.1. Second, the current (d) puts the **three named mechanisms in the outcome itself**, not in a bracket. Specimen Paper 3 Q3(c) asked for all three by name for three marks. `[OFFICIAL]` The old wording foregrounds "genetic recombination during development", which covers only one of the three, and which is also the phrase the Content list uses.

**Use the cover block. Ignore the Section 1 to 4 block.**

**5.2 Small wording drifts from the syllabus, in the cover block itself.** All four printings carry the same three:

- School: "the eradication of **small pox**". Syllabus: "the eradication of **smallpox**". `[OFFICIAL]`
- School LO (h): "explain the **mode** of transmission and infection". Syllabus: "explain the **modes** of transmission and infection". `[OFFICIAL]` The plural matters, because chapter 9.6 teaches three distinct modes of transmission and four distinct virulence-factor classes under infection.
- School Content bullet: "**Mode** of viral and bacterial infections". Syllabus: "**Modes** of viral and bacterial infections". `[OFFICIAL]`

None of these changes any content. They are evidence that the outcome block was retyped rather than pasted, which is the same signal Book 3 gave, and it is a reason to check any wording that does matter against the syllabus rather than against the notes.

**5.3 The contents outline covers Sections 1 to 4 only.** Printed page 2 lists sections 1 to 4 with page numbers and stops at page 78. There is no master contents page for sections 5, 6 and 7. Each of those chapters carries its own local outline instead. Not an error, but it means there is no single place in the Book where all seven sections are listed.

**5.4 No section anywhere corresponds to outcomes (j) or (k).** The contents outline runs 1.1 to 4.7, and the three later chapters print their own outlines: 5.1 to 5.3, 6.1 to 6.8, and 7.1 to 7.5. Read them together and there is no epidemiology section. See the cross-chapter findings.

**Skippable:** the thirteen textbook references on printed page 1. The cover artwork.

## 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 The verification finding: chapter 9.1 does NOT define outbreak, epidemic or pandemic.** `[SCHOOL]` The chapter is titled "Definitions and Overview" and its §1.2 is headed "KEY TERMINOLOGIES", so this is where those three terms should live. They are not there. The §1.2 table defines twelve terms and none of them is an epidemiological one. A literal search of the whole 139-page Book returns:

- **outbreak**: appears three times, all in passing, never defined. "epidemic outbreak of influenza" (9.5, printed p.84); "A community in which no one is immunised and an outbreak occurs" (9.7 Fig. 7.3 caption, printed p.125); "caused a limited outbreak" (9.7, printed p.127). Plus "measles outbreaks in the UK and pertussis outbreaks in the US" (9.7, printed p.126).
- **epidemic**: appears twice, never defined. "High attack rates are necessary to result in an epidemic outbreak of influenza" (9.5, printed p.84); "epidemics can be prevented, i.e. herd immunity" (9.7, printed p.129).
- **pandemic**: appears once, never defined. "HIV/AIDS pandemic" listed as a factor raising TB incidence (9.6, printed p.100).

**This confirms and extends the Book 2 finding.** Book 2's digest recorded that outbreak, epidemic and pandemic are defined nowhere in the corpus. That remains true after Book 9, which is the one Book that carries the outcome requiring them. LO (k) is uncovered by the school's notes.

**5.2 The chapter's own antigen definition contains a duplicated clause.** Printed page 4: "It is a molecule that binds to an antibody or a T cell antigen receptor (TCR). ... **It binds to an antibody or a T cell receptor (TCR) which elicits a B or T cell response respectively.**" The same statement is made twice within one bullet, once with "T cell antigen receptor" and once with "T cell receptor". Not a content error, but if the definition is being memorised, memorise the second sentence, because it carries the "elicits a B or T cell response respectively" clause that the first lacks, and it is the version chapter 9.4 repeats on printed page 51.

**5.3 The word "acquired" is used in two incompatible senses inside the same Book, and the confusion starts here.** Printed page 6, Fig. 1.2 caption: "the individual has **acquired immunity** to the infectious agent", meaning immunity gained after infection. Chapter 9.3 printed page 26: "Adaptive immunity is a specific immune response (**also called acquired immunity**)", meaning the adaptive system as a whole. Chapter 9.7 printed page 117: "**Adaptive/Acquired immunity** can be acquired either by active or passive immunisation." So "acquired" is a synonym for adaptive, and separately a descriptor of how immunity was obtained. `[OFFICIAL]` LO (a) uses it in the second sense only, and precisely: "**naturally acquired** and **artificially acquired** immunity". Write the outcome's two-word phrases and the ambiguity disappears.

**5.4 The immune system's "three major functions" list opens on cancer, which belongs to Book 3.** Printed page 5, function 1: "It tries to recognize and remove abnormal 'self' cells created when normal cell growth and development go wrong. For example, the diseases we call cancer..." This is true immunology and it links to Book 3's LO 2(p), which names **loss of immunity** as a causative factor for cancer. It is not an Extension Topic A outcome. Read it once as the bridge to Book 3 and do not build an answer around it.

**5.5 Type 1 diabetes appears as the worked example of autoimmunity.** Printed page 5. "Diabetes" returns zero hits in the 9477 syllabus. `[OFFICIAL]` This is the same scope trap Book 3 carried in its chapter 3.1 §7, arriving from a different direction. Context only.

**5.6 Minor wording.** Printed page 4: the immunity definition reads "It means having to escape from infection / illness / sickness", where "having to escape" should be "having escaped" or "the ability to escape". Printed page 8: "All the cellular elements of the blood, including leukocytes are the monocytes, the dendritic cells, and the neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils" is a broken sentence; the intended statement is that the leukocytes include those cell types. The chapter uses American spellings throughout ("recognize", "defense" alongside "defence"), which is inherited from its US textbook sources and is not a marking issue.

Beyond these, chapter 9.1 is factually sound. The definition block on printed pages 4 and 5 is the most reliable page in the Book.

# Chapter 9.2: Innate Immunity, the Early Defence Against Infections (15 PDF pages, printed 11–25)

## 1. Stated learning outcomes

**None printed in this chapter.** 9.2 opens directly on "SECTION 2 / INNATE IMMUNITY: THE EARLY DEFENCE AGAINST INFECTIONS". The governing outcome is (a), which requires "the non-specific (innate) immune system". `[OFFICIAL]`

## 2. Content node map

- **2.1 Three key lines of defence** (p.11–12)
  - First line: non-specific external barriers, physical and chemical, "Operate regardless of the exact nature of the invader"
  - Second line: innate immune response, likewise invader-independent
  - Third line: adaptive immune response, selective destruction plus memory
  - The three-phase timeline: innate phase, early induced innate response, adaptive immune response
- **2.2 First line of defence, non-specific external barriers** (p.13–15)
  - I. Physical and mechanical barriers: epidermis and keratinocytes, mucous-membrane epithelium of gastrointestinal, respiratory and urogenital tracts, cilia
  - Mucus as a trap, ciliated epithelium sweeping it upward, washing action of saliva and tears
  - II. Chemical and environmental barriers
    - A. pH: skin, stomach (pH 1.0 to 3.0), vagina
    - B. Microcidal secreted molecules: antimicrobial peptides, lysozyme in sweat hydrolysing peptidoglycan, RNases and DNases
  - III. Biologic barriers: commensal microbes, territory defence, opportunistic disease in the immunocompromised
- **2.3 Second line of defence, innate immunity** (p.15–16)
  - Five stated features (a) to (e): always present, immediate, instructs the adaptive system, non-specific and non-lasting, phylogenetically older
  - Summary table of key cells and signalling molecules
- **2.3.1 Components: white blood cells** (p.17–20)
  - Phagocytes as a class, lysosomal contents named
  - Neutrophils: ~60% of peripheral blood leukocytes, polymorphonuclear, half-life ~7 hours, first recruited, pus
  - Monocytes and macrophages: two stages of one lineage, the mononuclear phagocyte system
  - Dendritic cells: portals of entry, antigen presentation, the bridge between innate and adaptive
  - Natural killer cells: large granular non-phagocytic lymphocytes, 5 to 10% of circulating lymphocytes, kill infected and malignant host cells
- **2.3.2 Components: defensive proteins** (p.20–21)
  - I. Cytokines, with interferons as the named example, produced by macrophages and NK cells
  - II. Complement: at least 20 proteins, inactive until activated, three effects (destruction by pore formation, enhancement of phagocytosis, stimulation of inflammation)
- **2.3.3 Inflammatory response, phagocytosis and fever** (p.22–25)
  - Virulence factors introduced
  - I. Inflammation: histamine from mast cells and macrophages, increased blood flow, exudation of plasma proteins, redness, warmth, swelling and pain, neutrophil accumulation
  - II. Phagocytosis: histamine attracts neutrophils, antibody-coated pathogens, receptor recognition of antibody, engulfment into a phagocytic vacuole, digestive enzymes
  - III. Fever: raises phagocyte activity, slows bacterial reproduction, iron deficiency, faster adaptive proliferation, interferon production and NK stimulation

## 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 Dendritic cells are called "differentiated macrophages", which is wrong, and the same page also calls them a separate lineage.** Printed page 19, two statements about the same cell:

- "A third group of phagocytic cells **with both myeloid and lymphoid origins** is collectively known as dendritic cells"
- "They are bone marrow-derived **differentiated macrophages** that act as antigen-presenting cells (APCs)"

A dendritic cell is not a macrophage that has differentiated further. `[INFERRED]` They are separate cell types arising from separate progenitors, which the first statement gets right. Write **dendritic cell** and **macrophage** as two named APCs, never one as a form of the other. The distinction is directly examinable, because LO (b) names antigen-presenting cells as a class and a mark scheme may want more than one named example.

**5.2 The chapter says NK cells detect virus-infected cells by MHC display, which inverts the actual mechanism, and it drags dendritic cells in with it.** Printed page 20: "Similar to dendritic cells, NK cells distinguish virus-infected cells which should be killed, from healthy cells **by MHC proteins being displayed on their cell membranes**." Two problems. `[INFERRED]` First, NK cells detect the **loss** of MHC class I from a stressed or infected cell, not its display; a healthy cell displaying normal MHC class I is the one an NK cell spares. Second, dendritic cells do not distinguish infected cells from healthy cells at all; they ingest antigen and present it. The comparison is not a real one. The safe statement is that NK cells kill infected and abnormal host cells without needing antigen-specific receptors, which is what the same page says correctly two bullets earlier.

**5.3 "Non-specific" and "specifically recognize" appear four lines apart, and both are on the same page.** Printed page 15: "Innate immune components are present from birth and comprises **non-specific** components available before the onset of infection." Printed page 16, feature (d): "does not lead to lasting immunity and **is not specific** for any individual pathogen." Then printed page 16, immediately after: "**All these mechanisms of innate immunity specifically recognize and react against microbes.**" Read literally the third sentence contradicts the first two. `[SCHOOL]` The source textbook means that innate receptors recognise specific shared microbial structures, as distinct from recognising an individual pathogen, and chapter 9.3 printed page 26 states that resolution correctly: "the mechanisms of innate immunity recognize structures shared by classes of microbes". Use the 9.3 wording. Do not write "innate immunity is specific" in any answer, because LO (a) itself calls it "the **non-specific** (innate) immune system". `[OFFICIAL]`

**5.4 Neutrophil maturation time is given twice, differently.** Printed page 18 body text: "With a half-life of approximately 7 hours". Printed page 18 Fig. 2.10 caption: "They require about **2 weeks** to mature to become mature-segmented neutrophils." These are not the same quantity, half-life in circulation versus development time in marrow, so this is not strictly a contradiction, but the two numbers sit two centimetres apart with no signposting and are easy to conflate. Neither has an outcome behind it.

**5.5 The phagocytosis account on printed page 24 assumes antibodies are already present, inside the innate chapter.** "The neutrophils move towards the pathogens, which may be clustered together and **covered in antibodies**. The antibodies further stimulate the neutrophils to attack the pathogens." Antibodies are adaptive. The chapter never says so, and a student reading in order meets antibody-dependent opsonisation before antibodies have been introduced. `[SCHOOL]` The biology is right; the ordering is not. Chapter 9.3 printed page 47 gives the same content properly, under the heading Opsonization.

**5.6 Section II is numbered as "a third".** Printed page 20: the heading is "II. Natural Killer (NK) Cells" but the first sentence reads "**A third** lymphoid lineage cell distinct from B and T cells". The "third" counts B, T and NK within the lymphoid lineage, while the "II" counts the two categories of innate white blood cell. Both are defensible and together they are confusing.

**5.7 Figure order does not match reading order on printed page 24.** The body text of §2.3.3 II references Fig. 2.20 (inflammation) before Fig. 2.19 (phagocytosis), and Fig. 2.20's caption repeats content already given by Figs. 2.17 and 2.18. Reading-order hazard rather than an error.

**5.8 Minor.** Printed page 13, Fig. 2.4 caption: "confine them a local region" is missing a word. Printed page 17: "Found in tissues and fluids throughout the body phagocytes are usually motile" is missing a comma. Printed page 21 lists complement as "a group of at least 20 proteins", which is the low end of the usual textbook figure of about 30; no outcome depends on the number.

Beyond these, chapter 9.2 is factually sound. The barrier inventory, the complement effects, the inflammation account and the fever mechanisms are all internally consistent.

## 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 Clonal selection is defined two incompatible ways inside one chapter, and this is the most examinable contradiction in the Book.** `[SCHOOL]`

- Printed page 34, Fig. 3.11 commentary: "the small numbers of B cells with receptors complementary to the antigen **are stimulated to divide by mitosis. This stage is known as clonal selection.**" Then separately: "The small clone of cells divides repeatedly by mitosis **in the clonal expansion stage**."
- Printed page 40, Fig. 3.19 caption and the clonal selection hypothesis: "Each antigen ... **selects a pre-existing clone** of specific lymphocytes and stimulates the proliferation and differentiation of that clone", and "clones of lymphocytes specific for different antigens **develop before an encounter with these antigens**".

The second is correct and it is the version the phrase means. **Clonal selection is the antigen binding and picking out the one pre-existing clone whose receptor fits. Clonal expansion is the proliferation that follows.** `[INFERRED]` The page 34 version folds the mitosis into selection and then has to invent a second round of mitosis for expansion, which is not what happens. A candidate who learns page 34 will describe selection as a division event and lose the distinction. Both terms are in the corpus mark schemes as separate marks.

**5.2 Cytotoxic T cells are said to kill with hydrogen peroxide, and the same chapter later gives the correct mechanism.** `[SCHOOL]`

- Printed page 35: "TC cells recognise the antigens, attach themselves to the surface of infected cells, and **secrete toxic substances such as hydrogen peroxide**, killing the body cells and the pathogens inside"
- Printed page 49: "An effector TC cell releases cytotoxic pore-forming molecules called **perforins** along with **granzymes**. When granzymes enter the target cell through perforin channels, they activate an enzyme cascade that induces the cell to undergo **apoptosis**."

Hydrogen peroxide is a phagocyte killing agent, listed on printed page 17 among lysosomal contents. It is not how a cytotoxic T cell kills. `[INFERRED]` Write perforin and granzyme, and name apoptosis as the outcome. This is the exact wording LO (g) needs for the influenza answer, because 9.5 printed page 83 says infected epithelial cells "die via apoptosis as a result of the host immune response mediated by activated cytotoxic T cells".

**5.3 The primary response timing is given three different ways in one chapter.** `[SCHOOL]`

- Printed page 41: "The primary response **peaks about 10 - 17 days** after the initial exposure."
- Printed page 41, Fig. 3.20 caption: "The time after immunization may be **1-3 weeks** for a primary response and **2-7 days** for a secondary response"
- Printed page 49, Step 7: "the concentration does not peak until **1 to 2 weeks** after the initial exposure"

All three overlap but none matches another. `[INFERRED]` Do not quote a number unless the question supplies one. Answer with the comparison the outcome actually wants: the secondary response is **faster in onset, higher in peak, and longer lasting** than the primary. That comparison is what Fig. 3.20 shows and what LO (b) tests.

**5.4 Step 1 says the antigen lacks a self MHC marker, which is a category error.** Printed page 44: "The adaptive immune response begins when a molecule or organism (an antigen) **lacking the self (MHC) marker** manages to evade the first two lines of defence". An antigen is a molecule, not a cell, so it neither carries nor lacks MHC. `[INFERRED]` What is meant is that the antigen is not recognised as self. The same page's Fig. 3.24 caption then says the antigen "is attached to a self (MHC) marker", so the antigen both lacks and carries MHC within one page.

**5.5 Step 6b makes cytotoxic T cell activation require an APC encounter, which conflicts with the same paragraph.** Printed page 49: "The cytotoxic T cells (TC cells) are effector T cells that attack and destroy cells that display **MHC class I**-antigen complexes", followed by "A TC cell becomes activated to destroy a target cell when two events occur simultaneously: 1. The TC cell must **encounter an APC such as a macrophage**. 2. A TH cell must release a chemical to activate the TC cell." `[INFERRED]` A naive cytotoxic T cell does need to be activated by an APC presenting on MHC class I plus helper T cell cytokines. An **effector** cytotoxic T cell then kills any infected body cell displaying the antigen on MHC class I, and that target is not an APC. The paragraph merges activation and killing into one condition list. Chapter 9.4 printed page 53 states the correct version: "Tc cells, are activated by antigens presented on **any cell** in the context of MHC class I protein".

**5.6 The chapter never states which MHC class goes with which T cell, until 9.4.** Printed pages 30 to 39 discuss MHC nine times without once distinguishing class I from class II. The distinction first appears on printed page 49 (MHC class I) and is only made properly in chapter 9.4 printed page 53, in a figure caption. `[SCHOOL]` For an immunology topic this is a real gap in the notes, because the class I versus class II split is the single most-tested MHC fact in the corpus.

**5.7 B lymphocytes are said to reside in the bone marrow.** Printed page 33: "Lymphocytes that remain and mature in the bone marrow develop as B cells. **They reside in the bone marrow.**" The same chapter's printed page 29 says the opposite: "Once they have completed maturation in the bone marrow, both types of lymphocytes **enter the bloodstream** as mature naive lymphocytes. They circulate through the **peripheral lymphoid tissues**". `[SCHOOL]` Mature naive B cells circulate and meet antigen in lymph nodes and spleen. Long-lived plasma cells may return to the marrow, which printed page 36 states correctly. Page 33's sentence is wrong as written.

**5.8 The mature B cell count is given as roughly 10 million, which does not sit with the rest of the Book.** Printed page 33: "Many different types of B cell develop in each of us, **perhaps as many as 10 million.**" Chapter 9.1 printed page 5 says "there are **millions** of different antibodies", chapter 9.4 printed page 49 says antibodies "are produced in **billions** of forms", chapter 9.4 printed page 65 puts the antibody repertoire "at least **10^11**", and chapter 9.4 printed page 68 says the mechanisms "in theory have the capability of recognizing approximately **10^14** different epitopes". Five numbers spanning seven orders of magnitude. `[SCHOOL]` LO (d) says only "**millions** of different antibody molecules". `[OFFICIAL]` Use the outcome's word and quote a number only if the question supplies one. Note the reconciliation, because it is the actual biology: the theoretical repertoire is enormous, the repertoire an individual carries at one time is limited by how many B cells they have.

**5.9 The chapter says antigen presentation and clonal selection happen because the macrophage "collides" with the right T cell.** Printed page 45: "The macrophage wanders through the body until it literally **collides into** an appropriate TH cell." Textbook lymph nodes exist precisely so that this is not random, and the same paragraph says so in the next sentence. Colourful writing rather than an error, but do not reproduce "collides" in an answer.

**5.10 Terminology drift begins here and gets worse later.** This chapter uses TH and TC, helper T and cytotoxic T, effector CD4+ and effector CD8+, all for the same two cells. Chapter 9.7 then switches to "killer T cell". `[SCHOOL]` Fix on **helper T lymphocyte** and **cytotoxic T lymphocyte**, because those are the phrases LO (b) implies and the corpus mark schemes use.

**5.11 Minor.** Printed page 47: the acronym is introduced as "**NOA**" for Neutralization, Opsonization and Complement, where the third letter does not match its own word. Printed page 27 and page 47 both spell opsonisation two ways in the same Book. Printed page 44's Step 5 cross-references "Section 3.5" for clonal selection, which is where it is defined a second time, not where Fig. 3.25 sits. Printed page 26 header numbering repeats the printed page 25 of chapter 9.2.

## 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 Somatic hypermutation is twice said to happen to the plasma cell, which is wrong and matters for LO (d).** `[SCHOOL]`

- Printed page 71: "Somatic hypermutation allows for affinity maturation **after the plasma cell has encountered antigen**."
- Printed page 73, Key Points: "Somatic hypermutation allows affinity maturation **after the plasma cell has encountered antigen** and undergone associated stimulation."

The chapter's own definition box on the same page 71 gets it right: somatic hypermutation occurs "in immunoglobulin heavy and light chains of **activated B cells**". `[INFERRED]` A plasma cell is a terminally differentiated effector that has stopped dividing; hypermutation and selection happen in dividing B cells in the germinal centre, and only then do the winners become plasma cells. The chapter's own printed page 71 says so: "Differentiation into plasma cells usually occurs in **germinal centres** ... During differentiation, two processes can occur." Write **activated B cell** and the error disappears. This is directly examinable, because Specimen Paper 3 Q3(c)(iii) asked candidates to name precisely the process occurring in "**activated B lymphocytes**, to give sub-clones of plasma cells that vary slightly in the ability of their antibodies to bind antigen X". `[OFFICIAL]` The specimen's own wording puts the process in the activated B lymphocyte.

**5.2 V(D)J recombination is called a set of "somatic mutations".** Printed page 68: "**These somatic mutations assemble the genes** that produce the primary repertoire of immunoglobulins". Somatic recombination is a rearrangement of DNA segments, not a mutation, and the chapter defines the two distinctly six pages earlier and again six lines later. `[INFERRED]` LO (d) names the three mechanisms separately, so collapsing them is the exact thing the outcome tests against.

**5.3 The number of J gene segments is given as five and as six, twelve lines apart.** Printed page 65: "there are **five different J gene segments** that constitute J set." Printed page 68: "50 VH genes x 26 DH genes x **6 JH genes**". `[SCHOOL]` The arithmetic also does not match the claim it supports: 50 × 26 × 6 = 7800, and the text says "more than 6000". True but oddly stated. No outcome depends on any of these numbers. Learn the **mechanism** (random selection of one V, one D and one J), not the counts.

**5.4 Fab is equated with the variable region, which it is not.** Printed page 56: "The variable region, termed **Fab** (fragment, antigen binding), confers antigen recognition. The constant region, termed **Fc**". `[INFERRED]` The Fab fragment is a whole light chain plus the VH and CH1 domains of the heavy chain, so it contains constant regions as well. The chapter's own Fig. 4.20 on printed page 59 draws it correctly. In an LO (c) answer say that the **variable regions form the antigen-binding site** and the **constant region of the heavy chain (the Fc region) determines effector function**, and avoid the equation "Fab = variable region".

**5.5 The Key Points box says "five subclasses of heavy chains" where it means classes.** Printed page 62: "**Five subclasses** of heavy chains (γ, α, μ, δ, ε) correspond to distinct heavy chains (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, and IgE)." Five **classes**. The chapter uses "subclass" correctly two lines earlier for IgG1 to IgG4 and IgA1 to IgA2. `[SCHOOL]` The distinction is small but real, and this is a topic where a mark scheme will police the noun.

**5.6 The antibody diversity numbers do not reconcile across the chapter.** Printed page 49: "produced in **billions** of forms". Printed page 65: repertoire "at least **10^11**". Printed page 68: mechanisms give "in theory ... approximately **10^14** different epitopes". Printed page 69's worked example gives only "**4 x 10^5** different antibodies" from 200 light and 2000 heavy chains. `[SCHOOL]` See also 9.3 error 5.8. The reconciliation is that the theoretical combinatorial ceiling is far above what one person carries. LO (d) says "**millions**". `[OFFICIAL]`

**5.7 §4.5.1 says four mechanisms; the Key Points box says six.** Printed page 68: "**Four major mechanisms** for generating antibody diversity in humans occur during B cell development, before antigen exposure." Printed page 73: "There are multiple sources of antibody diversity: (1) multiple V gene segments (2) combinatorial diversity (3) junctional diversity (4) multiple combinations of light and heavy chains (5) **somatic hypermutation** (6) **class switching**." `[SCHOOL]` These are consistent once you notice the qualifier, since 5 and 6 happen **after** antigen exposure. But the qualifier is only in one of the two lists, and LO (d) names exactly three mechanisms, of which only somatic recombination belongs to the first four. **Map the outcome onto the chapter before revising it:** LO (d)'s "somatic recombination" is §4.4.1 plus mechanisms 1 to 4, "hyper-mutation" is §4.5.2(a), "class switching" is §4.5.2(b).

**5.8 §4.2.1(b) is a cross-reference to a page that does not carry the content.** Printed page 53: "MAJOR HISTOCOMPATIBILITY COMPLEX (MHC) PROTEINS. **Refer to: Section 3.4, page 35, on antigen presentation.**" Printed page 35 of chapter 9.3 does cover MHC and HLA, so the reference works, but the reader who follows it finds no class I versus class II split, which is the very thing §4.2.1 needs. The split appears only in the Fig. 4.8 caption on this same page 53. `[SCHOOL]` See 9.3 error 5.6.

**5.9 Repeated figures under new numbers.** Fig. 4.2 (printed p.50) is Fig. 2.7 and Fig. 3.1 again. Fig. 4.19 (printed p.59) is Fig. 4.14 again, printed two pages after it with an identical caption. Fig. 4.33 (printed p.74) repeats Fig. 3.23's title. Fig. 4.34 (printed p.76) repeats Fig. 3.20's content in a simpler form. `[SCHOOL]` Not errors, but the figure count overstates how much distinct material there is.

**5.11 Printed page 65 exists twice.** PDF pages 17 and 18 both carry footer 65, splitting Figs. 4.25 to 4.27 from Fig. 4.28. Navigation hazard.

**5.12 Two small statements sit oddly in §4.7.** Printed page 76 defines passive immunity as receiving "antibodies **or cells** (e.g., lymphocytes, feasible only in genetically identical [inbred] animals)". Cell transfer is not passive immunisation as the syllabus means it, and the parenthesis effectively withdraws the claim. Printed page 77 says of natural passive immunity, "**The only example** is seen in newborns", which is true for humans and is worth keeping as written, because it is exactly the LO (a) case.

## 2. Content node map

- **5.1 Virus transmission and replication** (p.80–81)
  - Table 5.1, routes of transmission: human to human, vertical, human to environment to human, animal to human via a vector, direct animal to human
  - The replication cycle in outline: receptor binding, receptor-mediated endocytosis or envelope fusion, uncoating, takeover of host resources, assembly, release
  - Two release routes: rupture (lysis) or budding with a host membrane envelope
  - Acute, mild or asymptomatic, and persistent (latent) infection
  - Viral pathogenesis defined
- **5.2 How influenza virus causes disease in humans** (p.82–84)
  - Mode of transmission: coughing and sneezing, respiratory droplets, infectious from one day before symptoms to five days after
  - Influenza A, B and C, and their host ranges
  - Influenza A subtypes by haemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA)
  - Step 1 Invasion of first line of defence: entry through oral or nasal cavities, epithelial cells of the respiratory tract as the primary target, the mucociliary layer, NA cleaving sialic acid to escape the mucus
  - Step 2 Infection: cytokine signalling and fever, alveolar macrophages, NK cells, dendritic cells, replication peaking at ~48 hours, depletion of host raw materials, release by budding
  - Step 3 Development of symptoms: budding depleting the epithelial membrane, loss of epithelial lining, excess mucus and swelling, apoptosis of infected cells driven by cytotoxic T cells, and the six symptom-to-cause pairs
  - Step 4 Transmission to other organisms: aerosols, droplet counts for sneeze versus cough, droplet size and depth of penetration, H5N1 from poultry
- **5.3 How HIV causes disease in humans** (p.85–88)
  - HIV as a retrovirus that infects and destroys helper T cells; AIDS as the resulting collection of opportunistic diseases
  - Table 5.2: methods of transmission, incubation period, site of action, clinical features
  - Step 1 Entry: dendritic cells and NK cells first; macrophages as a target; CD4 as the primary receptor with CCR5 or CXCR4 as co-receptor; co-receptor switching later in infection
  - Step 2 Infection: reverse transcriptase, integrase, provirus, latency, invisibility to cytotoxic T cells, budding without lysis
  - Step 3 Symptoms: the three clinical phases
    - Acute phase: 2 to 4 weeks, flu-like symptoms, high viraemia, viral set point, high transmission risk
    - Chronic phase (clinical latency): integration allows evasion, low-level replication, macrophage reservoirs, no prophage-style replication because terminally differentiated helper T cells do not divide, rising viral load
    - Crisis phase (AIDS): helper T count below 200 cells mm^-3, opportunistic infection, ~3 years survival untreated
  - Step 4 Transmission to others from an asymptomatic host

## 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 Figure 5.4 is missing from the sequence.** See the figure table. The chapter jumps from Fig. 5.3 to Fig. 5.5, and nothing in the text refers to a Fig. 5.4. `[SCHOOL]` Either a figure was deleted without renumbering or the numbering skipped. There is no missing content that the text depends on, so the effect is navigational only.

**5.2 The AIDS acronym is expanded wrongly.** Printed page 85: "**AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency)** is not a disease". The S stands for syndrome, and printed page 87 gets it right: "AIDS (**Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome**)". `[SCHOOL]` The full expansion is the safer thing to write, because "syndrome" is precisely what makes the sentence "AIDS is not a disease, it is a collection of opportunistic diseases" true.

**5.3 The chapter uses "HIV virus" and "helper T helper cells".** Printed page 85 heading: "5.3 HOW **HIV VIRUS** CAUSE DISEASE IN HUMANS", with a subject-verb error as well. Printed page 85 body: "can remain latent in **helper T helper cells**". Printed page 86, Fig. 5.7 caption: "the **retroviruse** that causes AIDS". `[SCHOOL]` Write **HIV**, not HIV virus, because the V already stands for virus, and a marker in an immunology topic will notice.

**5.4 Antigenic shift and drift are never mentioned, and this is a scope boundary rather than an error.** The word "antigenic" appears once in this chapter, in "determined by the antigenicity of the surface glycoproteins, haemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA)" (printed p.82). Neither shift nor drift is defined or explained. `[OFFICIAL]` That is because they belong to a different outcome: Core Idea 2 LO 2(f), "describe how variation in viral genomes arises, **including antigenic shift and antigenic drift**". So chapter 9.5 is correct to omit them, and a student who expects them here will think the notes are incomplete when they are not. See the cross-chapter findings for the full Book 4 boundary.

**5.5 The chapter assumes the viral replication cycle without teaching it, for both viruses.** Printed page 80 gives six lines on the generic cycle. Printed page 86 names reverse transcriptase, integrase and provirus in three bullets, and Fig. 5.7 is a full reproductive-cycle diagram with no walkthrough. `[SCHOOL]` The detailed cycles belong to Core Idea 2 LO 2(e), which names "**enveloped viruses, including influenza**" and "**retroviruses, including HIV**" and is taught in Book 4. `[OFFICIAL]` Chapter 9.5 never says so, and the student who has not done Book 4 first will find this chapter under-explained. That is a sequencing problem in the notes, not a content gap.

**5.6 Fever is attributed to interferon in 9.2 and to cytokines in 9.5, describing the same effect.** Chapter 9.2 printed page 25: "fever stimulates virus-infected cells to produce **interferon**, which travels to other cells and increases their resistance to viral attack." Chapter 9.5 printed page 84: "fever stimulates virus-infected cells to produce **cytokines**, which travels to other cells and increases their resistance to viral attack." Interferons are cytokines, so the two are compatible, but the 9.5 version loses the named molecule. `[SCHOOL]` Write **interferon** when the answer is about antiviral resistance, because it is the specific term and 9.2 supplies it.

**5.7 The chapter uses "epidemic outbreak" without defining either word.** Printed page 84: "High attack rates are necessary to result in an **epidemic outbreak** of influenza." This is the only place in the Book where the influenza spread vocabulary of LO (k) is touched, and it is one phrase in a bullet about H5N1 transmission from poultry. `[SCHOOL]` LO (k) explicitly names influenza as its example disease. `[OFFICIAL]` The notes do not deliver it.

**5.8 The influenza damage mechanism is stated in a way that is easy to over-read.** Printed page 83: "**Excessive budding depletes cell membranes** of epithelial cells, which causes epithelial lining of the respiratory tract to be lost." This is the school's own mechanism for LO (g) and should be reproduced, but note it is only half the story: the same page also gives the immune-mediated route, that cytotoxic T cells drive infected epithelial cells into apoptosis. `[INFERRED]` LO (g) asks for disease "through the disruption of host tissue and functions", so a full answer needs both the direct damage and the immune-mediated damage, plus the functional consequence, which is loss of the mucociliary escalator and therefore loss of the first line of defence.

**5.9 Two symptom bullets do not follow from the mechanism given.** Printed page 83: "Sore throat: **loss of epithelial lining**" is consistent. "Shortness of breath: **swelling and destruction of alveoli**" introduces alveolar damage that the chapter has not established, since the stated target is the epithelium lining the respiratory tract. `[INFERRED]` Minor, but do not claim alveolar destruction as the standard influenza mechanism.

**5.10 The latency claim about non-dividing helper T cells needs care.** Printed page 87: "Replication of inserted viral genome does not happen unlike that of prophage as **terminally differentiated helper T cells do not divide**." Resting memory CD4 cells are the main latent reservoir and they are long-lived rather than terminally post-mitotic. `[INFERRED]` The comparison to a prophage is the useful part and it is a genuine Book 4 bridge (LO 2(e)i names the lysogenic cycle). Keep the comparison, drop the absolute claim.

**5.11 Minor.** Table 5.1 on printed page 80 has misaligned rows in the extracted text, with "Blood and blood products / Sexual contact / Abrasions" sharing one examples cell; read it from the page, not from the text layer. Printed page 85 says AIDS incubation is "up to 10 years or more" while printed page 87 says "eight to ten years", which is consistent but stated twice.

## 6. Load-bearing versus skippable

**Load-bearing, and this chapter is short and almost entirely examinable:**

- **The influenza pathogenesis sequence, printed pages 82 to 84, all four steps.** LO (g) names influenza and the epithelial cells of the respiratory tract explicitly, so this is the outcome in the school's own words. The mark-bearing chain is: droplet transmission, entry via nose or mouth, NA cleaves sialic acid so the virus escapes mucus, infection of respiratory epithelial cells, replication and budding, loss of the epithelial lining both directly and through cytotoxic T cell killing, loss of the mucociliary defence, and the symptoms as consequences.
- **The HIV pathogenesis sequence, printed pages 85 to 88, all four steps.** LO (g) names HIV and helper T cells explicitly. The mark-bearing chain is: transmission by direct exchange of body fluids, CD4 binding with a co-receptor, reverse transcriptase and integration as a provirus, latency and invisibility to cytotoxic T cells, progressive destruction of helper T cells, collapse of cell-mediated immunity because helper T cells are the switch for both arms, and opportunistic infection.
- **The one sentence that carries the whole HIV answer, printed page 87:** helper T cell numbers fall below a critical level, cell-mediated immunity is lost, and the body becomes susceptible to opportunistic infections. Link it back to 9.3's helper T cell role, because the reason HIV is so damaging is that the helper T cell activates B cells **and** cytotoxic T cells, so losing it disables humoral and cell-mediated immunity together.
- **Fig. 5.8, printed page 88.** The three-phase graph with CD4 count falling and viral load rising. This is the most likely data stem in the chapter and reading it is a distinct skill from describing the phases.
- **The 200 cells mm^-3 threshold, printed page 88.** A single number that is genuinely worth holding, because it is the operational definition of progression to AIDS.
- **Table 5.1's transmission routes, printed page 80**, at the level of the five route names and one example each.

**Background:**

- The influenza A, B and C host ranges, printed page 82. One line.
- The droplet arithmetic on printed page 84, "up to 20,000 droplets" from a sneeze against "several hundred" from a cough, and the 1 to 4 μm size range. Vivid, not examined.

## 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 The penicillin figure names the wrong enzyme, contradicting its own body text on the facing page.** `[SCHOOL]`

- Printed page 111, body: "penicillin ... binds and blocks **transpeptidases** that form the cross-links between NAM residues"
- Printed page 112, Fig. 6.26(b) caption: "Penicillin blocks the **glycoprotein peptidases** that form the cross-bridges between NAMs"

There is no enzyme called a glycoprotein peptidase in cell wall synthesis. `[INFERRED]` The enzyme is the **transpeptidase**, also known as a penicillin-binding protein. LO (i) names penicillin explicitly and the corpus marks this answer on the enzyme name, so this matters. Write transpeptidase.

**5.2 Fig. 6.24's caption says autolysin breaks "glycolytic bonds".** Printed page 110: "Autolysin breaks **glycolytic** bonds in preexisting peptidoglycan, while transglycosylase synthesizes them". The bonds between NAG and NAM are **glycosidic**, and the chapter's body text on the same page says so correctly: autolysins "hydrolyze the bonds that connect NAG and NAM in the peptidoglycan backbone". `[SCHOOL]` Glycolytic means relating to glycolysis, which is Book 5. A single-letter typo that names a different process.

**5.3 Hepatitis A is filed under direct contact in the text and under foodborne in the table.** Printed page 92: "Diseases transmitted through **direct contact** include **Hepatitis A**, staphylococcal infections and sexually transmitted diseases." Printed page 94, Table 6.1: "Foodborne: e.g. poultry, seafood, meat / Food poisoning (botulism, staphylococcal), **hepatitis A**, listeriosis, tapeworms, toxoplasmosis, typhoid fever." `[INFERRED]` Hepatitis A is a faecal-oral pathogen, so the table is right and the text is wrong. It also appears as a virus in a chapter about bacterial pathogens.

**5.4 Table 6.1 files influenza under three different modes.** Printed page 94: influenza appears under "Indirect contact", under "Airborne", and separately chapter 9.5 printed page 80 files it under "Human to environment to human, Aerosolised virus from respiratory secretions". Chapter 9.6 printed page 92 also names influenza as the example for droplet contact transmission. `[SCHOOL]` Influenza genuinely spreads by more than one route, so this is not false, but a candidate asked to state the mode of transmission of influenza will find four answers in the same Book. The syllabus's own phrasing for LO (h) is "modes", plural, and this is why.

**5.5 The claim that M. tuberculosis survives eight months in fine aerosols is not supportable.** Printed page 100: "Mycobacterium tuberculosis are very resistant and can survive for up to **8 months in fine aerosol particles**." `[INFERRED]` Airborne droplet nuclei carrying M. tuberculosis stay infectious for hours in still indoor air, and the bacterium survives for months in dried sputum, dust and darkness, which is almost certainly where the figure comes from. Eight months as an aerosol is not a real quantity. No outcome needs a survival time, so the safe answer is that the droplet nuclei remain suspended and infectious in the air for a prolonged period, particularly in poorly ventilated and overcrowded spaces.

**5.6 The chapter contains no antibiotic resistance section, despite linking to a WHO antibiotic resistance factsheet.** Printed page 90 lists "WHO Antibiotic resistance Fact Sheet" and a video on "Mechanisms of Resistance to Penicillin" among its internet resources. The body of the chapter mentions resistance only three times in passing: drug-resistant TB strains (printed p.100), β-lactamase (printed p.111), and Fig. 6.22's Antibiotic F (printed p.108). `[SCHOOL]` There is no section on how resistance arises or spreads. **This is correct scope, not a gap.** Extension Topic A has no resistance outcome. Resistance arises from mutation and from transformation, transduction and conjugation, all of which are Core Idea 2 LO 2(g). `[OFFICIAL]` If a question asks how resistance spreads between bacteria, the answer comes from Book 4, not from Book 9.

**5.7 Sections 6.7.1 to 6.7.3 have no learning outcome.** The bacterial growth curve, the bacteriostatic and bacteriocidal and bacteriolytic distinction, minimum inhibitory concentration and the disk-diffusion test occupy printed pages 105 to 108, four pages. `[OFFICIAL]` LO (i) says only "describe the modes of action of antibiotics, including penicillin, on bacteria". Nothing in Extension Topic A names growth phases, MIC or disk diffusion. See the examiner guide for the full ruling; the short version is that this material survives as **practical and data-handling context**, because Paper 4 assesses planning and analysis and a zone-of-inhibition experiment is a standard vehicle for both. Learn it as method, not as recall.

**5.8 Bacteriocidal is spelled that way throughout, and the standard spelling is bactericidal.** Printed pages 106 and Fig. 6.19. `[SCHOOL]` The school inherits this from its Brock source. Both are understood; use **bactericidal** in an answer.

**5.9 "Haemophilus influenza" is missing its species ending.** Printed page 97. The species is *Haemophilus influenzae*. Printed page 129 in chapter 9.7 spells it correctly as "Haemophilus influenzae b". Also note the name is misleading, since it is a bacterium and not the influenza virus, and a candidate who meets it in a stem needs to know that.

**5.10 "Mycobacterium tuberculosisis" is a typo on printed page 100.** The doubled ending appears once, in the first line of §6.4.1.

**5.11 The penicillin lysis mechanism mixes water potential vocabulary.** Printed page 111: "Bacteria live in watery environments and take up water by osmosis. The weakened cell wall cannot withstand the **pressure potential** exerted on it by the cell contents and the cells eventually would burst." `[INFERRED]` The chain is correct in outline and uses the Singapore syllabus's own term for turgor pressure, but as written the wall is said to fail under pressure potential rather than being unable to generate the pressure potential that would stop further water entry. The clean version for an answer is: penicillin blocks transpeptidation, autolysins continue to cut the existing wall, the wall weakens, water enters the bacterium down a water potential gradient by osmosis, the weakened wall cannot resist the resulting pressure, and the cell lyses.

**5.12 Minor.** Printed page 96 lists "kinase" as an invasion enzyme, which is a generic name; the enzyme meant is streptokinase or staphylokinase. Printed page 113: "Chloramphenicol **attach** to 50S subunit" is a subject-verb error, and "Erythromycin inhibits translocation of the **subunit**" should be translocation of the ribosome along the mRNA. Printed page 100's TB statistics are 2014 WHO figures and the Straits Times links are from 2016, so the chapter is dated but not wrong. Fig. 6.29's caption text bleeds into the §6.8.4 heading in the extracted layer.

Beyond these, chapter 9.6 is the most reliable of the three application chapters. The transmission taxonomy, the virulence factor classes, the TB progression and the four antibiotic modes of action are all internally consistent.

`[SCHOOL]` The natural column's explanatory line is the artificial column's line, copied across. Natural active immunity is gained by **infection**, including sub-clinical infection, and specifically not by vaccination. Chapter 9.4 printed page 75 states it correctly: "As this activation occurs **naturally during an infection** it is called natural active immunity." This is directly examinable, because the four-way active-passive natural-artificial grid is the most reliably tested part of LO (a) in the corpus, and the specimen-era prelims ask candidates to place a scenario in the grid.

**5.5 The artificial passive cell is garbled.** Printed page 119: "1. **Antibodies or immunoglobulins immune cells:** receive anti-serum with antibodies from another host". Two noun phrases have collided. The intended content is the injection of antibodies or immunoglobulins, which is what chapter 9.4 printed pages 76 to 77 gives properly with the tetanus antitoxin example.

**5.6 The chapter switches to "killer T cell", which no other chapter in the Book uses.** Printed page 123, step 7: "**Killer T cells** find and destroy the invaded cells. Naïve killer T cells require an APC to display an antigen piece before they are activated." Printed page 124, step 5: "If the vaccination process induced a **killer T cell** response". `[SCHOOL]` Chapters 9.3 and 9.4 use cytotoxic T cell, TC cell and effector CD8+ T cell. Write **cytotoxic T lymphocyte**; killer T cell is textbook shorthand and NK cells are separately called natural **killer** cells four chapters earlier, so the word is genuinely ambiguous inside this Book.

**5.7 Text from Figures 7.2.2 and 7.2.3 bleeds into the body text.** Printed page 124: "Live vaccines were the first to be discovered, and **are4 still** the most effective, such as the live vaccines for smallpox and polio. **3** Live vaccines use organisms that are not virulent." The stray "4" and "3" are call-out numbers from the overlaid figure. Printed page 123's step list and printed page 124's step list are both interleaved with figure call-outs in the text layer. Read those two pages from the page, not from an extraction.

**5.8 LO (e)'s two-clause scope limit is nowhere reproduced or acted on.** The chapter delivers both required clauses, "vaccination stimulates immunity without causing the disease" in §7.2 printed page 120 and "vaccination of a high enough proportion of the population can break the disease transmission cycle" in §7.3 printed page 125. But it also delivers six vaccine types, a dated history, a full eradication case study and an appendix, and it never signals which parts the outcome's "limited to" clause protects. `[OFFICIAL]` The limit is real and it is a substantial time saving. See the examiner guide.

**5.9 The chapter never links vaccination coverage to a threshold or to R0, which is where LO (j) would have connected.** Printed page 126 says the required proportion "varies for each disease" and stops. `[INFERRED]` The reason it varies is that the herd immunity threshold depends on R0, and that link is exactly what LO (j) exists to test. It is the single most natural place in the Book for R0 to appear and it is absent. See the cross-chapter findings.

**5.10 Two figures share the number 7.4.1.** Printed page 126 carries "Figure 7.4.1. Photo of a child infected with smallpox" and "Figure 7.4.1. Progression of Smallpox", both on the same page.

**5.11 Minor.** Printed page 121: "produced by **killed** the disease-causing microbe" should be "by killing"; "still able to **simulate** immune response" should be "stimulate". Printed page 124: "the more antigens of the microbe **retrained** in the vaccine" should be "retained". Printed page 127: "**Somelia**" should be Somalia. Printed page 120: "the body is left with **leaving behind** a population of memory cells" doubles the verb. Printed page 129 gives WHO's figure of "2.5 million lives each year", which is a 2010s figure and now understated by later WHO estimates; no outcome needs a number.

## 6. Load-bearing versus skippable

**Load-bearing:**

- **§7.2's core argument, printed page 120.** This is LO (e) clause one, in the school's own words: the vaccine presents antigens that imitate an infection, triggers clonal expansion of specific B and T cells, leaves memory cells behind, and so converts the next encounter from a slow primary response into a fast secondary one, without causing the disease. That paragraph is the answer, and it must be linked back to 9.3's primary and secondary response curve.
- **§7.2.1's first three vaccine types, printed page 121.** Live attenuated, inactivated or killed, and toxoid, each with its advantage and disadvantage. These three carry the risk-benefit argument for LO (f) and they are the ones the corpus asks about. "Attenuated" is a word the outcome does not use but the corpus does.
- **§7.3 in full, printed pages 125 to 126.** This is LO (e) clause two: herd immunity, why vaccinating enough of a population breaks the transmission cycle, who it protects, and what happens when coverage falls. The outcome's own phrase is "break the disease transmission cycle", so use it.
- **§7.4's two key reasons, printed page 127.** LO (e) names the eradication of smallpox explicitly, so this case study is the outcome's worked example. Ring vaccination plus surveillance, and the three properties that made smallpox eradicable: highly visible rash, short incubation, and no non-human reservoir. Fig. 7.4.3 adds more reasons and is image-only, so extract it.
- **§7.5's benefits and risks table, printed page 129.** LO (f) is a whole outcome for one table. Four benefits and three risks, each in one line. Note that LO (f) says "**discuss**", which means both sides weighed and a judgement reached, not a list.
- **The virus names, printed page 126.** Variola causes smallpox; vaccinia is the cowpox vaccine virus. See error 5.1.

**Background:**

- **§7.1.1, printed page 118, which the school itself marks (FYI).** The whole history from 3000BC to the 1920s. It also contains errors 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3, so reading it costs more than it returns.
- **Vaccine types 4, 5 and 6, printed page 121**, extract, recombinant and DNA vaccines. Three lines each, no advantages or disadvantages given, no outcome, and no corpus presence.
- **§7.2.2 and §7.2.3, printed pages 123 to 124.** The eight-step and six-step walkthroughs restate §3.7 with vaccine antigen substituted for pathogen. Read once as consolidation. They also carry the killer T cell terminology drift and the figure-text bleed.
- **The full list of who herd immunity protects, printed page 125.** Two or three examples is enough.
- **The appendix figures, printed pages 130 to 131.** Useful only as data stems.
- **Vaccine ethics, hesitancy and mandate debates.** These appear nowhere in the chapter and they are not an outcome. LO (f) asks for benefits and risks, which is a scientific and public-health weighing, not an ethical one. Do not import ethics material into this chapter from any other source.

# Cross-chapter findings

## 1. Two of the eleven outcomes are not taught anywhere in the Book

This is the largest finding of the digest and it is verified by literal search of all 139 pages plus a full read.

**LO (j), the basic reproduction number.** `[OFFICIAL]` The outcome reads: "explain the meaning of the basic reproduction number, R0, as an indicator of the transmissibility (contagiousness) of infectious agents, and how R0 can be used to predict the progress of a disease outbreak". The strings **R0**, **R₀**, **basic reproduction number**, **transmissibility** and **contagiousness** return **zero hits** in Book 9. There is no section, no definition, no figure and no worked example.

**LO (k), outbreak, epidemic and pandemic.** `[OFFICIAL]` The outcome reads: "outline the terms (i) outbreak, (ii) epidemic, and (iii) pandemic, that are used to describe the spread of an infectious disease such as influenza". None of the three is defined anywhere. All three appear only in passing, in five places total, catalogued in the 9.1 entry §5.1 above.

**This confirms and extends the Book 2 finding.** Book 2's digest recorded that outbreak, epidemic and pandemic are defined nowhere in the corpus. Book 9 was the place they should have been, and they are not there.

**Why this happened, most likely.** `[INFERRED]` The 9.0 cover page preamble names epidemiology and R0 directly: "students are introduced to concepts in epidemiology ... By further unpacking the significance of the basic reproduction number (R0) in predicting the progress of a disease outbreak, students could also gain some insight to the utility of mathematical models". So the cover promises the content. LOs (j) and (k) are new in 9477 relative to the 9744 syllabus, and the contents outline stops at section 4, which reads like a Book assembled from an older set of chapters with the new cover bolted on and the new sections never written.

**What this means in practice.** These are two of eleven outcomes, and both are cheap to learn, definition-level content. **9477 Specimen Paper 1 Question 30 is an R0 question.** `[OFFICIAL]` The notes cannot supply it. It must be supplied from the syllabus wording and from external sources, and it should be treated as the first gap to close in this Book, not the last.

## 2. Where Book 4 ends and Book 9 begins, resolved outcome by outcome

The two Books overlap on viruses and on bacteria, and the syllabus draws the line cleanly if you read both sets of outcomes together. `[OFFICIAL]`

| Content | Owning outcome | Book |
|---|---|---|
| Reproductive cycle of bacteriophages, lytic and lysogenic | Core Idea 2 LO (e)i | **Book 4** |
| Reproductive cycle of enveloped viruses, **including influenza** | Core Idea 2 LO (e)ii | **Book 4** |
| Reproductive cycle of retroviruses, **including HIV** | Core Idea 2 LO (e)iii | **Book 4** |
| How variation in viral genomes arises, **including antigenic shift and antigenic drift** | Core Idea 2 LO (f) | **Book 4** |
| Binary fission in prokaryotes; transformation, transduction and conjugation | Core Idea 2 LO (g) | **Book 4** |
| Structure and organisation of viral and prokaryotic genomes | Core Idea 2 LO (d) | **Book 4** |
| How influenza and HIV **cause disease in humans through the disruption of host tissue and functions** | Extension Topic A LO (g) | **Book 9** |
| **Modes of transmission and infection** of bacterial pathogens, using *M. tuberculosis* | Extension Topic A LO (h) | **Book 9** |
| Modes of action of antibiotics, including penicillin | Extension Topic A LO (i) | **Book 9** |

**The rule that follows in one line: Book 4 owns what the pathogen does to its own genome, and Book 9 owns what the pathogen does to the host.** Replication mechanism, genome structure and genetic variation are Book 4. Tissue damage, loss of function, transmission between hosts, and treatment are Book 9.

**Two practical consequences.**

First, **chapter 9.5 is under-explained on purpose.** It gives six lines on the generic viral replication cycle and three bullets on reverse transcriptase and integrase, because Book 4 has already done that work. Do not build a Book 9 cluster that re-teaches the replication cycles; build one that assumes them and asks what the replication does to the epithelium or to the helper T cell population.

Second, **antigenic shift and drift do not belong to Book 9 and chapter 9.5 correctly omits them**, but they are the obvious bridge for a cross-Book question, because influenza is named in both LO 2(e)ii and LO (g). Specimen Paper 1 Question 11 is exactly this shape: it lists five features of the HIV reproductive cycle and asks how many also occur in influenza. `[OFFICIAL]` That is a Book 4 question wearing Book 9 clothes.

**Antibiotic resistance sits on the Book 4 side.** Extension Topic A has no resistance outcome, and chapter 9.6 correspondingly has no resistance section, despite linking to WHO resistance material. How resistance arises and spreads is Core Idea 2 LO (g), which names transformation, transduction and conjugation. Penicillin's counter-mechanism, β-lactamase, is taught in 9.6 as part of penicillin's mode of action and belongs here.

## 3. Antibody gene rearrangement belongs to Extension Topic A, not to Control of Gene Expression

Book 2's question bank files antibody gene rearrangement under Control of Gene Expression. The syllabus does not. `[OFFICIAL]`

A literal search of the whole 9477 syllabus returns **somatic recombination** once, **hyper-mutation** once and **class switching** once, and all three hits are in the same place: **Extension Topic A LO (d)**. Core Idea 2 LO 2(i), which is the control of gene expression outcome, lists five regulation levels (chromatin, transcriptional, post-transcriptional, translational, post-translational) and mentions none of the three. There is no other outcome in the syllabus that carries them.

**So LO (d) owns antibody diversity outright, and the corpus filing is an artefact of how the bank was built, not a syllabus judgement.** The confusion is understandable, because class switching is a DNA-level change that alters which protein a cell makes, which sounds like gene regulation. It is not regulated expression of a fixed gene; it is irreversible deletion of DNA between switch regions, which the school states plainly on printed page 72 of chapter 9.4.

Two things follow. First, when drilling Book 2's Control of Gene Expression bank, filter out the antibody records and drill them here instead. Second, **Specimen Paper 3 Question 3(c) is the proof**: three marks, one each, for naming precisely the genetic process in B lymphocyte progenitors, in activated B lymphocytes producing IgG instead of membrane IgM, and in activated B lymphocytes producing sub-clones that differ in binding ability. `[OFFICIAL]` That is somatic recombination, class switching and somatic hypermutation, which is LO (d) tested word for word.

## 7. Error tally and the six that would actually cost marks
