## A. Adaptive immunity is specific, slow to start, and it remembers

**A1. The definition, and the two features that follow from it.** Adaptive immunity is a **specific
immune response**, also called **acquired immunity**, resting on receptors on the surface of **B and T
lymphocytes**. From that come **specificity**, the ability to **distinguish between many different
antigens**, and **immunological memory**, which confers **lifelong protective immunity to reinfection
with the same pathogen**. Innate receptors recognise **structures shared by classes of microbes**;
adaptive receptors recognise a much wider variety of **individual molecules**. Of the school's six
characteristics only three carry an outcome: **specificity and diversity**, **clonal expansion and
selection**, and **immunological memory**. Specialisation, contraction and tolerance are background.

**A2. The two arms divide by where the microbe is.**

| | **Humoral immunity** | **Cell-mediated immunity** |
|---|---|---|
| Mediated by | **antibodies, produced by B lymphocytes** | **T lymphocytes** |
| Defends against | **extracellular microbes and their toxins** | **intracellular microbes** |
| Hard limit | **antibodies cannot gain access to microbes that live and divide inside infected cells** | reaches inside, by **killing the host cell** that harbours the microbe |

The limit in row three is **the reason cell-mediated immunity exists at all**. An antibody works in
solution, so it can neutralise a virus in the blood but never one already inside a cell.

**A3. Where the cells come from.** **Both B and T lymphocytes originate in the bone marrow**, but **only
B lymphocytes mature there**; the **precursor T lymphocytes migrate to the thymus** and mature there.
Mature cells that have not yet met antigen are **naive cells**, which express receptors for antigens but
**do not perform the functions required to eliminate antigens**, and which **die by apoptosis if not
activated**. The lymphatic anatomy tour has no outcome; the bone marrow and thymus line does.

## B. The cells LO (b) names, each as a one-line job description

The outcome asks for **roles**, not descriptions, so learn each cell by what it does.

| Cell | Role |
|---|---|
| **B lymphocyte** | carries **membrane antibody as its receptor**, **one specificity per cell**; on activation it differentiates into **plasma cells and memory B cells** |
| **Plasma cell** | the **effector cell of the B lineage**; **secretes antibodies** at up to several thousand a second, and **does not live long** |
| **Helper T lymphocyte** | recognises antigen on **MHC class II**; **releases cytokines** that make B cells **divide, become plasma cells and secrete antibodies**, make macrophages **phagocytose more vigorously**, and **activate cytotoxic T cells** |
| **Cytotoxic T lymphocyte** | recognises antigen on **MHC class I** on **any infected body cell**; releases **perforins and granzymes**, and the target **dies by apoptosis** |
| **Antigen-presenting cell** | **dendritic cell, macrophage and B cell**; captures antigen, **breaks it into fragments** and **presents them in the groove of an MHC protein** |
| **Memory cell** | **survives long periods in the absence of antigen**, **functionally inactive until stimulated**, then **initiates the secondary response** |

**B1. The helper T lymphocyte is the switch for both arms.** It activates **B cells** on the humoral side
and **cytotoxic T cells** on the cell-mediated side, so **losing it disables both arms together**. That
is why LO (g) makes it HIV's target in Cluster 3.

**B2. A memory B cell does not secrete antibody, and this is a named misconception.** An examiner's
report records that candidates did not know memory B cells **first need to differentiate into plasma
cells**, which in turn secrete antibodies. The chain is **memory B cell → activation → plasma cell →
antibody**, and skipping the middle step loses a mark.

**B3. Only B cells and their plasma cell progeny make antibody**, so they are the cells that **mediate
humoral immunity**. A T cell receptor is structurally similar to an antibody but is **present only on the
cell surface and never secreted**.

## C. Antigen recognition: what each receptor actually sees

**C1. An epitope is the part that binds.** It is the **small, accessible portion of an antigen that binds
an antigen receptor**, also called the **antigenic determinant**. A single antigen usually has **several
epitopes**, but all receptors on one lymphocyte are identical, so they **bind the same epitope**. That
asymmetry, many epitopes per antigen against **one specificity per cell**, is what makes clonal selection
possible.

**C2. B cells and T cells recognise different things.** **B cell receptors bind epitopes of intact
antigens**, whether on a pathogen or **free in body fluids**. T cell receptors bind **only fragments of
antigens displayed on the surface of host cells**. The displaying protein is the **MHC molecule**, whose
groove **binds peptide fragments of 8 to 20 amino acids**.

**C3. MHC class I against class II, which your notes state exactly once.**

| | **MHC class I** | **MHC class II** |
|---|---|---|
| Found on | **any nucleated body cell** | **antigen-presenting cells** |
| Presents to | **cytotoxic T lymphocytes** | **helper T lymphocytes** |
| Co-receptor | **CD8** | **CD4** |
| What it signals | this cell is **infected from within** | this cell has **ingested something from outside** |

When the receptor binds, the co-receptor also binds the MHC protein, **strengthening the interaction and
enhancing activation of the T cell**. **MHC proteins are unique to each person** and **identify the
body's own cells as self**.

## D. Clonal selection, clonal expansion, and the sequence they sit in

**D1. Clonal selection is the antigen picking a clone that already exists.** **Clones specific for
different antigens develop before an encounter with those antigens**, and each antigen **selects and
activates one specific clone**. Nothing is built to order: the repertoire is generated first, blind, and
the antigen then chooses from it.

**D2. Clonal expansion is the proliferation that follows.** Activated lymphocytes proliferate,
**generating many thousands of clonal progeny, all with the same antigen specificity**. It **enables few
antigen-specific lymphocytes to serve their defensive role** and **keeps pace with rapidly proliferating
microbes**. Expansion is also **why the primary response is slow**, since it must finish before any
effector cell exists.

**D3. The full adaptive sequence, which is how LO (b) gets examined.**

**Pathogen enters** → **processed by an antigen-presenting cell** → **fragments displayed on MHC** → **helper T lymphocyte with the complementary receptor binds** → **cytokines released** → **clonal selection** → **clonal expansion by mitosis** → **differentiation into plasma, memory and effector cells** → **antibody secretion** and **killing of infected cells**.

## E. Antibody structure paired to function

LO (c) asks for the relationship of structure **to** functions, so structure alone is half an answer. An
examiner comment reads that **students are reminded to write both structure and function**, and a scheme
rejects **variable region or heavy chain only**.

**E1. The molecule.** **Four polypeptide chains**: **two identical light chains** of about 220 amino
acids and **two identical heavy chains** of about 440, held by **disulfide bonds** in a **Y shape**. Each
chain has a **constant (C) region**, whose sequence varies little between B cells, and a **variable (V)
region**, whose sequence **varies extensively from one B cell to another**. A heavy-chain and a
light-chain V region **together form one binding site**, and the greatest diversity sits in **three
hypervariable regions** per chain. The heavy chain also carries a **hinge domain**.

**E2. Every feature pairs with something it lets the antibody do.** The pairing is the answer.

| Structural feature | Function it produces |
|---|---|
| **Variable regions with hypervariable loops** | a site **complementary in shape to one epitope**, giving **specificity** |
| **Two identical binding sites (bivalent)** | **cross-links antigens into a large lattice**, which is **agglutination**, and the lattice is **readily phagocytosed** |
| **Flexible hinge region** | lets the **distance between the two binding sites vary**, raising **cross-linking efficiency** |
| **Constant region of the heavy chain, the Fc region** | **binds Fc receptors on macrophages and neutrophils**, so the antibody acts as an **opsonin**, and it **activates complement** |
| **Large four-chain glycoprotein** | **cannot cross a cell membrane**, so antibodies **act only outside cells** |

**E3. Antigen binding is non-covalent, and the required phrase is antigen-binding site.** Binding occurs
**in the hypervariable regions**, held by **electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic
interactions and van der Waals forces**. Two rejects to respect: an antibody has an **antigen-binding
site, never an active site**, and is never described as an enzyme; and **disulfide bonds hold the four
chains together, never antigen to antibody**.

**E4. Four antibody functions, four separate marks.**

1. **Neutralisation.** Antibodies **bind surface proteins of a virus** and **prevent infection of a host
   cell**, and **bind toxins** so they cannot enter body cells.
2. **Agglutination.** Bivalent antibodies **link bacteria or viruses into aggregates**, which
   **immobilises them** and makes them **easier to phagocytose**.
3. **Opsonisation.** Bound antibody **presents a readily recognised structure for macrophages or
   neutrophils**, thereby **promoting phagocytosis**, through **Fc receptors**.
4. **Complement activation.** A complement protein binding an **antigen-antibody complex** triggers a
   **membrane attack complex that forms a pore**, so **ions and water rush in and the cell lyses**.

**E5. IgG is the outcome's named example, and five facts carry it.** It is the **most common circulating
antibody**, about **80% of serum immunoglobulins**; it has the **longest half-life**; it **crosses the
placenta** to protect the newborn; it is **produced in large quantities during secondary responses**; and
its **Fc region binds receptors on phagocytes**. Placental transfer is itself a mechanism: placental **Fc
receptors bind IgG**, take it in by **receptor-mediated endocytosis**, and release it into fetal blood by
**exocytosis**.

**E6. The other four classes, at comparison level only.** **IgM** is the **pentamer**, the **first class
released in a primary response**, and its ten sites make it a **powerful agglutinator**. **IgA** is the
antibody of **secretions and colostrum**. **IgE** binds **mast cells**. **IgD** sits on **naive B cells**
beside IgM. **IgG is the only class the syllabus names**, so the rest are context.

## F. Antibody diversity: three mechanisms, told apart by effect

**This is the highest-yield node in Book 9.** 9477 Specimen Paper 3 Q3(c) tested all three, **one mark
each, with the command Name precisely**. A school examiner's report gives the penalty in one line:
**recombination and hypermutation earn nothing in a class-switching question.** There is **no partial
credit** for naming the neighbouring mechanism.

**F1. The problem the mechanisms solve.** The **antibody repertoire is at least 10^11** and there are
nowhere near that many genes. **Recombining gene segments to build different V regions** cuts the number
of genes that would otherwise be needed. The segments are **variable (V), constant (C), leader (L),
joining (J) and diversity (D)**, each present in **multiple copies**.

**F2. Somatic recombination happens first, in developing B cells, before any antigen.** It is a
**rearrangement of DNA**, named to distinguish it from the **meiotic recombination** of gamete
production, and it **occurs even in the absence of antigen**. Mechanically, **germline sequences that are
initially separate are brought together by enzymatic deletion of intervening sequences**, and it occurs
**only in developing B and T lymphocytes**. What it builds is the **variable region**.

Four things inside it multiply the diversity, and they are reasons rather than mechanisms in their own
right: **multiple V, D and J segments** to choose from; **combinatorial diversity**, because segments are
**selected randomly** during rearrangement; **junctional diversity**, because the **joining is imprecise**
and staggers the junction by a few base pairs; and **any heavy chain can pair with any light chain**,
which matters because **both chains contribute to the binding site**.

**F3. Somatic hypermutation happens after antigen, in activated B cells, and changes affinity.** It
introduces **point mutations at a very high rate into the V regions**, **more than 10,000 times the rate
of regular DNA**. The consequence is **affinity maturation**: **B cells expressing higher-affinity
antibody are selectively stimulated**, so **high-affinity antibody dominates the secondary response**.
**Specificity is unchanged; the strength of binding improves.**

**F4. Class switching happens after antigen, in activated B cells, and changes the class.** The **same VH
exon is joined to a different CH exon**, so **one heavy-chain constant region gene is replaced by another
isotype**, switching production **from IgM to IgG, IgA or IgE**. It is **triggered by a cytokine signal
from helper T cells**. Two consequences carry marks: it is **irreversible**, because the **intervening
DNA between switch regions is deleted**; and **the V region does not change, so the same antigenic
specificity is retained**, which means switching **affects effector function but not specificity**.

**F5. The table the outcome is tested through.**

| | **Somatic recombination** | **Somatic hypermutation** | **Class switching** |
|---|---|---|---|
| When | **before antigen** | **after antigen** | **after antigen** |
| Where | **developing B lymphocytes** | **activated B lymphocytes** | **activated B lymphocytes** |
| What changes | the **V region is assembled** from separate segments | **point mutations in the V region** | the **heavy-chain constant region** |
| What stays | nothing yet exists | the **antigen specificity** | the **antigen specificity** |
| Effect | **different antigens can be recognised** | **affinity varies within one specificity** | **effector function changes**, IgM to **IgG, IgA or IgE** |
| Reversible | not applicable | yes, further mutation is possible | **no, the DNA is deleted** |

**Drill it in the direction the specimen ran it**, from a described effect back to the term. Different
antigens recognised, **somatic recombination**. Class changed with specificity kept, **class switching**.
Affinity varied within one specificity, **somatic hypermutation**.

**F6. Three documented rejects sit on this node.** **It is the cell that undergoes class switching, not
the antibody**, and a scheme records candidates describing it as something the antibody does.
**Alternative splicing is wrong, since that occurs in mRNA**, while somatic recombination happens in
**DNA**. And **class switching does not affect the variable regions**, so it can never answer a question
about changed specificity.

**One filing correction.** Book 2's question bank files antibody gene rearrangement under Control of Gene
Expression. The syllabus does not: it returns **somatic recombination once, hyper-mutation once and class
switching once**, and **all three hits are in Extension Topic A LO (d)**. **Drill them here.**

## G. Primary against secondary response

This is LO (b) directly, and it is the reason vaccination works in Cluster 3.

| | **Primary response** | **Secondary response** |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | the **first exposure to a foreign antigen** | a **subsequent encounter with the same antigen** |
| Onset | **slow**, because selection and expansion **start from a tiny clone** | **rapid**, because **memory cells already exist in numbers** |
| Magnitude | **small** | **larger** |
| Duration | shorter | **longer lasting** |
| Cells | **naive B and T lymphocytes** | **long-lived memory cells** from the primary response |
| Antibody class | **IgM first** | **predominantly IgG**, after class switching |

**The reason is the mark, not the description.** Secondary responses **result from activation of the
memory T and B cells induced during the primary response**. Two rejects: a scheme rejects **rapid alone,
because it lacks the idea of comparison with the primary response**, and another rejects a secondary
response answer **without specifying the same antigen**. Write the comparison and name the antigen.

**Do not quote a timing figure unless the question supplies one.** Your notes give the primary peak three
incompatible ways on two pages: **10 to 17 days**, **1 to 3 weeks**, and **1 to 2 weeks**.

## H. The four types of immunity

LO (a) names **active, passive, naturally acquired and artificially acquired**, which is a two-by-two
grid, not four unrelated things.

| | **Naturally acquired** | **Artificially acquired** |
|---|---|---|
| **Active**, the individual **makes his own antibodies** | **infection**, including **sub-clinical infection** | **vaccination**, by **injecting antigens** or taking them by mouth |
| **Passive**, **antibodies received from elsewhere** | **antibodies across the placenta**, and **IgA in colostrum and breast milk** | **injection of antibodies**, such as a **tetanus antitoxin** |

**Active immunity is long-lasting; passive immunity is not, and the reason is examinable.** Active
immunity follows **activation of the individual's own lymphocytes**, so it leaves **memory cells**.
Passive immunity is **immunity gained without an immune response**, so **no memory cells are made**, and
the transferred antibodies are not made by the body's own B cells, are **regarded as foreign themselves**,
and are **removed from the circulation by phagocytes in the liver and spleen**. Passive immunity is
therefore **useful for conferring immunity rapidly**, even before the individual can mount an active
response, but it **does not induce long-lived resistance**.

## J. Errors in your notes, and what to encode instead

**Three sit directly on an examined distinction.**

1. **Somatic hypermutation is twice attributed to the plasma cell**, on printed pages 71 and 73. The
   chapter's own definition box on page 71 gets it right: it occurs in **activated B cells**. A plasma
   cell is a **terminally differentiated effector that has stopped dividing**, so hypermutation and
   selection cannot happen there. **Specimen P3 Q3(c)(iii) uses the phrase activated B lymphocytes.**
   **Write activated B lymphocyte.**
2. **Clonal selection is defined as the mitosis on printed page 34**, where B cells with complementary
   receptors are **stimulated to divide by mitosis. This stage is known as clonal selection.** Pages 38
   and 40 are right. **Selection is the antigen binding the one pre-existing clone whose receptor fits;
   expansion is the proliferation that follows.**
3. **Cytotoxic T cells are said to kill with hydrogen peroxide on printed page 35**, and page 49 gives
   the right mechanism. **Hydrogen peroxide is a phagocyte's lysosomal agent.** Cytotoxic T cells
   **release perforin and granzymes**, and the target **dies by apoptosis**.

**Four more worth correcting.**

4. **Fab is equated with the variable region on printed page 56.** The Fab fragment **contains constant
   regions as well**. Say that the **variable regions form the antigen-binding site** and the **Fc region
   determines effector function**, and avoid the equation.
5. **V(D)J recombination is called a set of somatic mutations on printed page 68.** It is a
   **rearrangement of DNA segments, not a mutation**. LO (d) names the three mechanisms separately, so
   collapsing them is the exact thing the outcome tests against.
6. **B lymphocytes are said to reside in the bone marrow on printed page 33.** Page 29 is right: on
   maturing, both types **enter the bloodstream as mature naive cells and circulate through the
   peripheral lymphoid tissues**.
7. **The Key Points box calls the five heavy chain types subclasses.** They are **classes**; **IgG1 to
   IgG4 are subclasses**. This is a topic where a scheme will police the noun.

**Two places the notes are silent.** **MHC class I against class II is never stated in chapter 9.3**,
appearing only inside a figure caption in 9.4, despite being the most-tested MHC fact in the corpus. It
is in §C3 above. And the **antibody repertoire is given five different sizes**, from **10 million** to
**10^14**. **LO (d)'s own word is millions.** Quote a number only when the question supplies one.

**Terminology to fix on.** The Book calls one cell **cytotoxic T cell, TC cell, effector CD8+ T cell and
killer T cell**. **Write cytotoxic T lymphocyte**, because killer T is ambiguous beside **natural killer
cell**.

**Cut entirely, no outcome reaches any of it.** **RAG-1, RAG-2, TdT, the V(D)J segment counts and the
junctional arithmetic**; the **pro-B and pre-B stages and the surrogate light chain**; the **kappa and
lambda distinction**; the **IgG and IgA subclasses**; the **antigen receptor signalling cascade**, which
is Book 3; and **immunodeficiency and hypersensitivity classification**.
