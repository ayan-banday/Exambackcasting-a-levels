Answer - part (a)

(a) Which row shows possible identities for P, Q, R and S? [1] [INFERRED: the specimen papers ship without a mark scheme; the audit below is worked out here and cross-checked against the school keys quoted in the other parts]

1. The answer is D.

2. Q must be a B lymphocyte. It carries membrane antibody as its receptor, which is drawn as Y-shapes on its surface, and only B lymphocytes carry antibody as a receptor. It also divides by mitosis, which is clonal expansion. That eliminates A and C, both of which call Q a T lymphocyte.

3. S must be the plasma cell. It carries no surface receptor and is surrounded by free antibody, which is the picture of a cell that secretes antibodies rather than displaying them. A plasma cell is the effector cell of the B lineage. That eliminates A, which calls S a memory cell, and C, which calls S a B lymphocyte.

4. R must be the memory cell. It is drawn small with few receptors and it is not secreting. A memory cell survives long periods in the absence of antigen and is functionally inactive until stimulated. That eliminates B, which has R as the memory cell but Q's parent P as a T lymphocyte, and eliminates C, which calls R a plasma cell.

5. P can be a pathogen. The question asks for possible identities, and what activates a B lymphocyte is antigen, which may be carried on a pathogen. B is wrong because a T lymphocyte is not the thing that turns into a B lymphocyte; A and C are already out on Q.

6. What this question is really testing, and the guide's own summary of it: every wrong option is one cell type placed wrongly. The cells must be told apart by what they carry and what they do, not by name recognition.

(b) Describe the roles of cells U4 and V4 in a primary immune response. [3]

Answer - part (c)

(c) Describe the consequences of the lack of helper T cells on the functioning of the immune system. [4]

1. There are no helper T cells to bind to antigen displayed on class II MHC in antigen presenting cells (e.g. dendritic cells).

2. Hence no helper T cells are activated to release cytokines.

Question 2 [11 marks]

This is the full-cycle question. It runs one continuous chain: an antigen selects the one clone whose receptor fits, that clone expands, its progeny differentiate, antibody is secreted, memory persists, and the same antigen returns years later to a completely different response. Answer (a) to (c) as one story.

(a) Tetanus is a disease caused by a bacterium. When the tetanus bacteria enter the body they release a toxin which causes muscular rigidity and extreme pain. Explain what is happening at stages X and Y in the immune response to tetanus toxin. [2]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (a)

(a) Explain what is happening at stages X and Y in the immune response to tetanus toxin. [2]

1. X: only Q has receptor / clonal selection. Receptor and toxin have complementary shape.

2. Y: Q undergoes clonal expansion / forms a clone / divides / increases number / mitosis.

3. This is the exact distinction your notes get wrong. Printed page 34 says B cells with complementary receptors are stimulated to divide by mitosis. This stage is known as clonal selection. It is not. Pages 38 and 40 are right. Clonal selection is the antigen binding the one pre-existing clone whose receptor fits; clonal expansion is the proliferation that follows. Stage X in this figure is selection; stage Y is expansion. If you called stage Y clonal selection you have imported the error.

4. The reason selection can work at all, which a stem may ask for separately: clones specific for different antigens develop before an encounter with those antigens, so the repertoire is generated blind and the antigen then chooses from it. That is also why clonal expansion makes the primary response slow: it has to finish before any effector cell exists.

(b) A person who is confirmed as SARS-CoV-2-positive has also tested positive for the presence of antibodies to the virus. Outline the events that lead to the production of antibodies specific to SARS-CoV-2. [5]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (b)

(b) Outline the events that lead to the production of antibodies specific to SARS-CoV-2. [5]

1. When SARS-CoV-2 infects a person, the primary immune response will be stimulated.

2. The SARS-CoV-2 / viral antigen is taken up by an antigen-presenting cell (APC), via phagocytosis.

3. The antigen is processed by the APC and is bound to MHC class II receptors on the cell membrane of the APC.

4. The antigen is presented to naive CD4 T cells.

5. Naive CD4 T cells become activated, and proliferate and differentiate to form helper T cells.

6. The SARS-CoV-2 / viral antigen is also recognised by / binds to B cells.

7. Helper T cells fully activate B cells, via release of cytokines.

8. B cells proliferate and differentiate to form plasma cells that produce antibodies specific to SARS-CoV-2.

9. The sequence in your own notes, for cross-checking: pathogen enters → processed by an antigen-presenting cell → fragments displayed on MHC → helper T lymphocyte with the complementary receptor binds → cytokines released → clonal selection → clonal expansion by mitosis → differentiation into plasma, memory and effector cells → antibody secretion and killing of infected cells.

Answer - part (c)

(c) Explain why the IgG antibody levels peaked earlier in the subsequent infection compared to the first infection. [4]

1. Quote values: IgG antibody levels reached a peak of 100 a.u. 8 days after infection during the subsequent infection, against a peak of 100 a.u. only 13 days after infection during the primary infection.

2. Idea of delayed IgG production in primary infection as time is needed for activation / selection / expansion / differentiation of naive lymphocytes / class switching to form IgG. Question 3 [10 marks]

(a) Researchers looked at patients with celiac disease and found that they had much higher levels of antibodies against reoviruses than those without the disease. Describe three ways in which the structure of antibodies contributes to its function. [3]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (a)

(a) Describe three ways in which the structure of antibodies contributes to its function. [3]

1. Constant region; determines the class of antibody (e.g. IgG, IgA, IgM) / allows binding to phagocyte (e.g. macrophage / neutrophil).

2. Variable regions (of each light chain and heavy chain); form an antigen binding site that provides a lock-and-key fit for specific binding to a particular epitope of an antigen.

3. Disulfide bridges between chains; link the heavy and light chains together.

4. Hinge region; provides flexibility to change orientation / movement to bind antigen.

5. Examiner's comment, verbatim: students are reminded to write both structure and function. And the reject that goes with it: different combinations of heavy and light chains allow binding to different antigens on pathogens is rejected, because that addresses why a huge variety of pathogens can be recognised, which is a diversity answer and not a structure-to-function answer. LO (c)'s own wording is the relationship of the molecular structure of antibodies to their functions, so a bare structure list scores nothing.

6. Two further rejects the schemes enforce here. "R: active site" appears twice, with "Reject: if the antibody is described as an enzyme", and a marker's note adds that generic references to "binding site" were not acceptable: the required phrase is antigen-binding site. And "R: formation of disulfide bonds as covalent bonds never occur between antigens and naturally produced antibodies", so disulfide bonds hold the chains together and never the antigen.

(b) A variety of different membrane proteins are found on the cell surface membrane of naive T-lymphocytes. Amongst them, the T cell receptor (TCR) has an important function in antigen presentation. Compare the structure of the TCR shown in Fig. 3.1 to the structure of an antibody molecule secreted by plasma cells. [3]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (b)

(b) Compare the structure of the TCR shown in Fig. 3.1 to the structure of an antibody molecule secreted by plasma cells. [3]

Must have at least one similarity and one difference.

1. Similarities: both have disulfide bonds; both have antigen binding site(s); both have quaternary structure / more than 1 polypeptide chain.

2. Differences: the TCR has 2 polypeptides while the antibody has 4 polypeptides.

3. Hydrophobic R-groups of amino acids on the surface are present (embedded in the membrane) in the TCR but absent in the antibody.

4. The TCR has 1 antigen binding site while the antibody has 2 antigen binding sites.

5. Book 1 reach-back check. This part is asking for quaternary structure, the bonds that maintain it, and the surface R-group chemistry that decides whether a protein sits in a membrane or dissolves in plasma. If you could name the differences but not say why hydrophobic surface R-groups mean membrane-embedded, that is a Book 1 gap.

6. The functional consequence worth attaching, because it is what the two binding sites are for: being bivalent lets an antibody cross-link antigens into a large lattice, which is agglutination, and the lattice is readily phagocytosed. A TCR with one site cannot do that, and is never secreted.

(a) Name precisely the genetic process occurring in B lymphocyte progenitors, to give naive B lymphocytes capable of recognising different antigens. [1] [INFERRED: the specimen ships without a mark scheme; the answers below are cross-checked against the school keys quoted in the other parts, which use the same terms for the same described effects]

1. Somatic recombination, accept V(D)J recombination.

2. Why this and not one of the other two: the described effect is different antigens recognised, and somatic recombination is the only one of the three that builds the variable region in the first place. It happens before antigen, in developing B lymphocytes, by enzymatic deletion of the intervening sequences that separate the germline segments.

(b) activated B lymphocytes, to give plasma cells that secrete IgG antibodies instead of membrane-bound IgM antibodies that are produced earlier in the process [1]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (b)

(b) Name precisely the genetic process occurring in activated B lymphocytes, to give plasma cells that secrete IgG antibodies instead of membrane-bound IgM antibodies. [1]

1. Class switching, accept isotype switching.

2. Why this one: the described effect is the class changed while the specificity is kept. The same VH exon is joined to a different CH exon, so one heavy-chain constant region gene is replaced by another isotype, switching production from IgM to IgG, IgA or IgE. It is triggered by a cytokine signal from helper T cells and it is irreversible, because the intervening DNA between switch regions is deleted.

3. The penalty, in a school examiner's own words on a class-switching question: "Many discussed somatic recombination and/or somatic hypermutation, which were irrelevant." A separate reject line reads "R: somatic recombination / hypermutation". Recombination and hypermutation earn nothing in a class-switching question.

4. Two more rejects sitting on this node. It is the cell that undergoes class switching, not the antibody, and a scheme records candidates describing it the wrong way round. And alternative splicing is wrong, since that occurs in mRNA, while somatic recombination and class switching both happen in DNA.

(c) activated B lymphocytes, to give sub-clones of plasma cells that vary slightly in the ability of their antibodies to bind antigen X. [1]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (c)

(c) Name precisely the genetic process occurring in activated B lymphocytes, to give sub-clones of plasma cells that vary slightly in the ability of their antibodies to bind antigen X. [1]

1. Somatic hypermutation.

2. Why this one: the described effect is affinity varied within one specificity. It introduces point mutations at a very high rate into the V regions, more than 10,000 times the rate of regular DNA, and the consequence is affinity maturation, where B cells expressing higher-affinity antibody are selectively stimulated. Specificity is unchanged; the strength of binding improves.

3. Drill the three in the direction the specimen ran them, from a described effect back to the term. Different antigens recognised, somatic recombination. Class changed with specificity kept, class switching. Affinity varied within one specificity, somatic hypermutation. Nothing else separates them cleanly.

(d) Besides the location, describe two differences between the process you named in (a) and class switching. [2]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (d)

(d) Besides the location, describe two differences between somatic recombination and class switching. [2]

1. Somatic recombination occurs during the maturation process of the B cell, that is in developing B cells, whereas class switching occurs after activation of the B cell by cytokines from helper T cells, that is in activated B cells.

2. Somatic recombination involves changes to the variable region / antigen binding site, whereas class switching involves changes to the constant region.

3. Somatic recombination results in changes to antigen binding specificities, whereas class switching results in changes to effector function.

4. The reject that pairs with this: class switching does not affect the variable regions, so it can never answer a question about changed specificity.

Answer - part (e)

(e) Which of the following statements are correct about generating antibody diversity? [1]

1. The school's official key gives C (1, 3 and 4), and records that 61.90% of candidates answered correctly. No further commentary is given.

2. Statement 1 is true. Somatic recombination builds both variable domains: at the heavy chain locus one V, one D and one J segment are joined, and at the light chain locus one V and one J segment are joined, so diversity is created in VH and VL.

3. Statement 2 is false. D segments exist only in the heavy chain, and they are recombined. It is the light chain that has no D segments. The statement inverts it.

4. Statement 3 is true. Somatic recombination occurs even in the absence of antigen, in developing B lymphocytes, which is exactly what part (a) above tested.

5. Statement 4 is where you have to hold two things at once, and this is the reason this MCQ is in the drill. The school's key marks it true, and chapter 9.4 of your Book says the same thing twice, on printed pages 71 and 73: that hypermutation happens after the plasma cell has encountered antigen. The specimen says otherwise. 9477 Specimen P3 Q3(c)(iii) uses the phrase "activated B lymphocytes", and the chapter's own definition box on page 71 also says activated B cells. A plasma cell is a terminally differentiated effector that has stopped dividing, so selection of higher-affinity variants cannot happen there.

6. So: answer the school's paper with the school's key, and never write it that way in prose. In any written answer, somatic hypermutation occurs in an activated B lymphocyte. If a Cambridge stem offers you "plasma cell" as the site of hypermutation, it is a distractor.

(f) In humans, three genes code for the production of antibodies: the heavy chain gene, which comprises 50 variable segments, 25 diversity segments and 6 joining segments; the kappa light chain gene, which comprises 40 variable segments and 5 joining segments; the lambda light chain gene, which comprises 30 variable segments and 4 joining segments. Answer - part (b)

(b) Describe three ways in which antigen presentation differs in phagocytes and B lymphocytes. [3]

1. On the process used to internalise antigen, phagocytes use phagocytosis, with the pathogen in a phagosome, whereas B lymphocytes use receptor-mediated endocytosis, with the pathogen in an endosome.

2. On the purpose of antigen presentation, phagocytes present to activate naive T cells, whereas B lymphocytes present to activate itself.

3. On target cells, in phagocytes the antigen peptide is presented to both naive CD4 and CD8 T cells, whereas in B lymphocytes the antigen peptide is presented to a CD4 T helper cell already activated by the same antigen.

4. The underlying reason for row 1, which makes it worth remembering rather than memorising: a B cell receptor binds epitopes of intact antigens, so a B cell can only take in the one antigen its receptor fits, whereas a phagocyte's Toll-like receptors recognise structures shared by classes of microbes, so it takes in anything that looks like a pathogen. The specificity of the uptake is what forces the different route.

5. What both cells have in common, which the question does not ask for but a stem often does: an antigen-presenting cell displays peptide fragments of protein antigens in association with MHC molecules and activates antigen-specific T cells, and the MHC groove binds peptide fragments of 8 to 20 amino acids. The named APCs are dendritic cell, macrophage and B cell.
