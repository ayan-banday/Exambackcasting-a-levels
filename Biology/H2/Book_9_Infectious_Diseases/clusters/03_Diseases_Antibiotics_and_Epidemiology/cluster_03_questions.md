### Question 1

**(a)** *Explain how the influenza virus causes a runny nose, blocked nose and sore throat.* **[3]**

**1.** **The virus infects the epithelial cells in the respiratory tract.**

**2.** **Viral replication results in expression and insertion of viral glycoproteins on the epithelial cell surface membrane, causing the epithelial cells to be recognised as foreign by the body's immune cells.**

**3.** **Leading to a local inflammatory response, for example vasodilation, increasing blood vessel permeability, fluid entering tissues, induced by the release of histamine by mast cells.**

**4.** **Cytokines also recruit more white blood cells to the site of infection, causing damage to the epithelial tissue resulting in production of mucus.**

**5.** **Release / budding of influenza virus causes death of host cells which also results in production of mucus.** **"Mucus" must be seen once.**

**6.** Note the two routes of damage, because candidates routinely give only the first. The pathogen damages tissue **directly**, here by **excessive budding depleting the cell membranes of epithelial cells** so **the epithelial lining of the respiratory tract is lost**. The **host's own response damages tissue as well**, through **inflammation, fever and the killing of infected cells by activated cytotoxic T lymphocytes**. A full LO (g) answer needs both.

**7.** Then give the functional consequence, which is the outcome's own wording, **disruption of host tissue and functions**: losing the epithelium means **losing the mucociliary escalator**, so **the first line of defence is gone** and **secondary bacterial infection becomes likely**. Stopping at tissue damage is an incomplete answer.

**(b)** *Explain why a single dose of the vaccine is not likely to provide long-term immunity.* **[4]**

**1.** **Antigenic drift occurs where there is an accumulation of mutations within the genes that code for viral glycoproteins haemagglutinin and neuraminidase.**

**2.** This **causes a change in the conformation of haemagglutinin and neuraminidase.**

**3.** **(B cell receptors of) memory B cells produced by a single dose of the vaccine may not recognize mutated haemagglutinin and neuraminidase.**

**4.** **Hence they will not mount a secondary immune response, or the immune system will treat it as a new antigen and mount a primary immune response.**

**5.** This part is the **Book 4 bridge, and it is deliberate.** **Antigenic drift and antigenic shift are Core Idea 2 LO 2(f)**, which is Book 4, and chapter 9.5 correctly leaves them out. But **9477 Specimen P1 Q11 names HIV and influenza, which are LO (g)'s two viruses, and then asks about reproductive cycle features, which is Book 4's answer.** Book 9 framing over a Book 4 answer is this paper's house style, so a revision plan that never crosses the boundary loses those marks.

**6.** The Book 4 half in full, since a stem may want more of it: the influenza **RNA genome mutates faster because RNA polymerase lacks proofreading**; **antigenic drift is the gradual accumulation of point mutations in HA and NA under the selection pressure of antibody**; and **antigenic shift is the reassortment of the eight RNA genome segments when two different influenza viruses infect one host cell**, giving a new subtype. **Drift is gradual and within a subtype; shift is sudden and creates a new subtype.**

**(c)** *Suggest why the number of vaccinations administered for H1N1 remained at zero for the first 8 weeks.* **[1]**

**1.** **The H1N1 virus first appeared in April 2009, and the vaccine was not ready until the end of October.**

**2.** Why this counts as biology and not trivia: a vaccine is a **preparation containing antigens** matched to a **specific** pathogen, so a new subtype needs a **new preparation**, which takes time to identify, produce and test. That lag is exactly why influenza is the syllabus's own example in LO (k) and why a new subtype spreading between continents is the classic pandemic.

### Question 2

**(a)** *Explain how HIV causes disease in humans through the disruption of host tissue and functions.* **[3]**

**1.** **Hijacking of cellular machinery and resources towards producing new virus disrupts normal activities needed for cell survival, eventually causing cell death.**

**2.** **Budding of a large amount of viruses from the cell surface might also disrupt the cell membrane sufficiently for the host cell to die.**

**3.** **HIV may induce adjacent T helper cells to fuse together forming giant multinucleated cells or syncytia.**

**4.** **Shortly after syncytia formation occurs, the fused cells lose immune function and die.**

**5.** **Functional T helper cell levels decline to a critical point.**

**6.** The one sentence that carries the outcome, and it links straight back to Cluster 2: **when helper T cell numbers fall below a critical level, cell-mediated immunity is lost**, and the body becomes **progressively more susceptible to opportunistic infections**. It is catastrophic because the helper T lymphocyte **activates both B cells and cytotoxic T cells**, so **losing it disables both arms together**.

**7.** Two naming disciplines. **Write HIV, never "HIV virus"**, because the V already stands for virus. And **AIDS is not the virus and not a disease**: it is **a collection of opportunistic diseases** associated with the immunodeficiency HIV causes. The word **syndrome** is what makes that sentence true. The number worth holding is **helper T count below 200 cells mm⁻³**, the operational definition of progression to AIDS.

**(b)** *Suggest and explain how the destruction of memory T cells will contribute to a lowered secondary immune response.* **[3]**

**1.** **No / fewer memory T cells remain in circulation for a second encounter with antigen.**

**2.** **No / less cytokine secreted by T helper cells to stimulate naive B lymphocytes to undergo clonal expansion and differentiation to plasma cells.**

**3.** So **no / less antibodies specific to the antigens of HIV are produced.**

**4.** Note the shape of the chain. Every mark is a **consequence of the previous line**, which is what Explain means in this Book, and Explain is the command word this Book leans on hardest. Naming the cells that are lost is worth nothing on its own.

**(c)** *Using Fig. 2.1 and Fig. 2.2, suggest and explain which type of HIV is less virulent.* **[4]**

**1.** **HIV-2 is likely to be less virulent.**

**2.** **[Fig. 2.1] Reference to HIV-2 having a lower peak of plasma viral levels at the acute phase / just after HIV infection compared to HIV-1.** OR **reference to HIV-2 having lower plasma viral RNA levels during the asymptomatic / latency phase compared to HIV-1.** OR **reference to HIV-2 having lower plasma viral RNA levels throughout compared to HIV-1.**

**3.** **[Fig. 2.2] Patients with HIV-2 infection take an average 15.6 years after HIV infection, compared to patients with HIV-1 infection, 8.2 years after HIV infection, to die.** OR **patients with HIV-2 infection take an average 14.3 years after HIV infection, compared to patients with HIV-1 infection, 6.2 years after HIV infection, to develop AIDS.**

**4.** **Reference to Fig. 2.1 indicating that HIV-2 has a lower viral load in the host, releasing fewer virions / infecting fewer T helper (host) cells / a slower replication rate / a slower infection rate / less damage to T helper cells, while Fig. 2.2 indicates more time is required to develop AIDS and death compared to HIV-1, so HIV-2 is less virulent.**

**5.** **Reject: answers written in the reverse argument, explaining how HIV-1 was more virulent.**

**6.** The examiner's own report on this question, and it is the most useful paragraph in this drill. **Most students obtained the two quoting marks, MP2 and MP3, but many forgot to explain how lower plasma viral RNA levels in HIV-2 infection leads to lower virulence.** **An alarming number explained their answers with respect to HIV-1**, that is, why HIV-1 is more virulent, **and reverse arguments were rejected because the question asked which type is less virulent.** And: **for Fig. 2.1 students often forget to quote the x-axis**, writing "the plasma viral RNA level is lower for HIV-2 than HIV-1" without saying **when**. **Quote both axes, every time.**

**7.** Note what this question is and is not. It is **LO (g)**, disruption of host tissue and functions, tested through **virulence**, which is **the ability of a pathogen to cause serious damage to its host**. It is **not** a question about the retroviral replication cycle, which is Book 4's LO 2(e)iii. Nothing in the scheme asks for reverse transcriptase or integrase.

### Question 3

**(a)** *With reference to Fig. 3.1, describe how M. tuberculosis could be transmitted from person to person.* **[2]**

**1.** **Via fine, aerosol droplets.**

**2.** **When an infected person with the active (pulmonary) TB disease sneezes or coughs and an uninfected person inhales the droplet.**

**3.** A parallel scheme adds the marking discipline: **maximum 1 mark if there is no reference to infected / uninfected.** Both ends of the transfer have to be named.

**4.** Where this sits in the outcome. LO (h) says **modes**, plural, and your notes print **mode**. There are **three modes of transmission**: **contact** with sub-modes direct, indirect through **fomites**, and droplet; **vehicle** with sub-modes waterborne, foodborne, airborne and bodily fluid; and **vector** with sub-modes mechanical and biological. TB is **droplet contact and airborne transmission**. Risk rises with **overcrowding** and **low immunity**.

**5.** Two spelling disciplines the schemes enforce: **"Reject abbreviation; spelling MUST be correct"** and **"[Reject: bacteria] [Accept: italics]"**. Write ***Mycobacterium tuberculosis*** in full and correctly the first time.

**(b)** *With reference to Fig. 3.2, explain how the bacteria causing tuberculosis can evade the mechanisms of the macrophage host cells.* **[1]**

**1.** Their **mycolic acid wax coat on their cell walls also protects them against hydrolytic enzymes in lysosomes.**

**(d)** *With reference to Fig. 3.4, explain why MTB-specific antibody responses are usually undetectable during the early phase of infection, but detectable as the infection progresses to active TB.* **[2]**

### Question 4

**(a)** *Describe the mode of action of penicillin.* **[3]**

**1.** **Penicillin acts as a competitive inhibitor which binds to the active site of the enzyme transpeptidase.**

**2.** **This prevents transpeptidase from forming cross-links between peptidoglycan chains, resulting in weakened bacterial cell walls in actively dividing cells.**

**3.** **When bacteria take in water by osmosis, there is increased turgor pressure which causes bacteria to swell and lyse.**

**4.** **Write transpeptidase, and nothing else.** A scheme carries the bare line **"Reject: peptidyl transferase"**, which is a different enzyme entirely, the **ribosomal activity chloramphenicol blocks**. And your own notes carry a third wrong name: **printed page 112 says penicillin blocks the "glycoprotein peptidases"**, while **page 111 correctly says transpeptidases**. There is no enzyme called a glycoprotein peptidase.

**5.** The full chain in your notes, which a longer version of this question wants: peptidoglycan is a backbone of **alternating N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM), cross-linked by peptide bridges**, which **protects the cell against rupture in hypotonic environments**. **Transpeptidation is the final step in wall synthesis**, forming the **cross-links between NAM residues**, while **autolysins hydrolyse the bonds connecting NAG and NAM** so new wall can be inserted. Then: **penicillin binds and blocks the transpeptidase → no new cross-links form → autolysins keep cutting the existing peptidoglycan → the wall weakens → water enters by osmosis down a water potential gradient → the weakened wall cannot resist the pressure → the cell lyses.** Penicillin is therefore **bacteriolytic**.

**6.** One further correction from your notes: a figure caption says **autolysin breaks glycolytic bonds**. The bonds between NAG and NAM are **glycosidic**; glycolytic refers to glycolysis, which is a different Book entirely.

**7.** The other three modes of action, since LO (i) says **modes**, plural. **Nucleic acid synthesis**: sulfonamides and trimethoprim competitively inhibit the enzymes making **tetrahydrofolic acid**, quinolones act on **DNA gyrase**, rifampin on **bacterial RNA polymerase**. **Protein synthesis**: aminoglycosides and tetracyclines on the **30S subunit**, chloramphenicol and erythromycin on the **50S subunit**. **Cell membrane integrity**: polymyxins bind **lipopolysaccharide** and disrupt the outer and inner membranes. All four rest on **selective toxicity**, the ability to **kill or inhibit the infecting microbial cells but not the host's cells**, which depends on **exploitable biochemical differences** between them.

**(b)** *State a named example of each: antibody, antibiotic.* **[2]** [INFERRED: the specimen ships without a mark scheme]

**1.** **Antibody: IgG.** Accept IgM, IgA, IgE or IgD.

**2.** **Antibiotic: penicillin.** Accept tetracycline, chloramphenicol, erythromycin, streptomycin, rifampin or any other named antibiotic.

**3.** **Two marks for two words, and the boundary is the whole point.** The papers record the examiner comment reduced to five characters: **"Vaccines ≠ Antibodies ≠ Antibiotics."** A separate reject reads **"Reject: vaccines produce antibodies"**. A **vaccine contains antigens** and the body makes its own antibodies; an **antibody is a protein made by a plasma cell**; an **antibiotic is a substance produced naturally by some microorganisms that kills or inhibits the growth of others**. **IgG and penicillin are the safe answers, and both are named in the syllabus.**

**(c)** *Explain how the size and shape of antibody molecules and antibiotic molecules relate to their modes of action and functions.* **[5]** [INFERRED: the specimen ships without a mark scheme; the marking points below are built from the LO (c) and LO (i) school keys reproduced in this file]

*Antibody:*

**1.** The **antibody is a very large protein with a quaternary structure of four polypeptide chains folded into a Y shape.**

**2.** The **two variable regions at the tips of the Y form antigen-binding sites whose three-dimensional shape is complementary to one specific antigen**, so the antibody binds only to that antigen and its action is **highly specific**.

**3.** Having **two binding sites lets one antibody bind two pathogens at once, so pathogens are clumped together in agglutination** and cannot spread, and are easier for phagocytes to engulf; the **constant region binds to receptors on phagocytes, so the antibody acts as an opsonin**; and antibodies also **neutralise toxins and block the attachment proteins of viruses**.

**4.** Because the antibody is **so large it cannot cross the cell surface membrane**, so it can only work **outside cells, in blood plasma, lymph and tissue fluid, on antigens exposed on the pathogen surface.**

*Antibiotic:*

**5.** The **antibiotic is a very small molecule.** Its **small size allows it to pass through the bacterial cell wall and membrane and reach targets inside the bacterial cell**, and to be **absorbed from the gut into the blood and delivered to the site of infection.**

**6.** Its **shape is complementary to the active site of a specific bacterial enzyme, or to a site on the bacterial ribosome or cell wall**, so it **inhibits one specific metabolic process such as peptidoglycan cell wall synthesis or protein synthesis**, killing the bacterium or preventing it multiplying.

**7.** **Being small, it can act inside cells where an antibody could never reach, but it is less specific**, so it may affect a wide range of bacteria including harmless gut flora.

**8.** How to read this question, because the figure is the question. **The two molecules are drawn to the same scale**, so the entire prompt is a size contrast: a large four-chain glycoprotein against a small molecule about **one-hundredth of its diameter**. **9.** One discipline that carries across from Cluster 2 and bites here too: the antibody has an **antigen-binding site**, the antibiotic binds an **active site**. **Do not swap those two words.** A scheme rejects **"active site" in an antibody** twice, with **"Reject: if the antibody is described as an enzyme"**.

### Question 9

**(a)** *Which conditions are expected to lead to an increase in the R₀ number of viral dengue disease?* **[1]** [INFERRED: the specimen ships without a mark scheme; the option audit below is worked out here]

**1.** The answer is **C**.

**2.** **R₀ is the mean number of secondary cases produced by one infected individual introduced into a wholly susceptible population**, so it rises when transmission becomes easier. Dengue is transmitted by the *Aedes* mosquito **vector**, and **increased rainfall leaves more standing water for the mosquitoes to breed in, so vector numbers rise and each infected person infects more people.** R₀ increases.

**3.** **A is wrong.** The new area has **no established vector population** to transmit the virus onward, so infected people arriving there produce few or no secondary cases.

**4.** **B is wrong, and it is the trap the specimen built deliberately.** In countries where the disease is **already well established the population has already been exposed**, so extra travel between them does not raise R₀.

**5.** **D is wrong, and it is the second deliberate trap.** In a population with frequent outbreaks **a large proportion is already immune**, which lowers the number of susceptible contacts and so **lowers, not raises, the effective reproduction number.** **Incidence is not the same quantity as transmissibility.**

**6.** Note what this question is doing. It gives **four unfamiliar applied scenarios and one definition**, and asks you to apply the definition. Neither of the specimen's two clear Book 9 MCQs is plain recall.

**(b)** `[CONSTRUCTED, no real paper question exists]` *Explain the meaning of the basic reproduction number, R₀, as an indicator of the transmissibility of an infectious agent.* **[2]**

**1.** **R₀ is the mean number of secondary cases produced by one infected individual introduced into a wholly susceptible population.** `[INFERRED]`

**2.** **The higher the R₀, the more transmissible or contagious the infectious agent**, because **each primary case generates more new cases**, so R₀ is used as **an indicator of the transmissibility (contagiousness) of infectious agents.** `[OFFICIAL]` on the outcome's own wording.

**3.** Three parts of the definition carry the meaning and are worth saying separately: it is a **mean**, not a fixed count; it counts **secondary cases arising from one primary case**; and it assumes **everybody else is susceptible**, which is why it describes a pathogen **entering a new population** rather than one circulating in a partly immune one.

**4.** Four things determine R0, and each one is a lever for control. `[INFERRED]` **Contact rate**, raised by **population density and overcrowding** and lowered by **isolation and quarantine**. **Transmission probability per contact**, set by the **route** and the **infectious dose**, and lowered by masks, hygiene and barrier methods. **Duration of the infectious period**, since **the longer a person stays infectious, the more secondary cases they generate**, so **treatment that shortens infectiousness lowers R0**. And **vector abundance** for vector-borne disease, which is the lever the specimen MCQ in part (a) pulled.

**(c)** `[CONSTRUCTED, no real paper question exists]` *Explain how R₀ can be used to predict the progress of a disease outbreak, and state what will happen to seasonal influenza cases after the control measures.* **[3]**

**1.** **R₀ = 1 is the threshold.** `[INFERRED]`

| Value | What it means | What happens next |
|---|---|---|
| **R₀ > 1** | each case produces **more than one** new case | **case numbers grow** and the **outbreak spreads** |
| **R₀ = 1** | each case produces **exactly one** new case | numbers **stay steady**; the disease is **endemic** |
| **R₀ < 1** | each case produces **fewer than one** new case | **case numbers fall** and the **outbreak dies out** |

**2.** **Reason from the arithmetic, not from the table.** If one case makes on average **two** further cases, the chain runs 1, 2, 4, 8 and **grows exponentially**. If it makes **half a case**, the chain runs 1, 0.5, 0.25 and **fades to zero**. Every control measure is an attempt to push a pathogen from above the threshold to below it.

**3.** **All four agents in Table 9.1 have R₀ greater than 1**, so on introduction into a fully susceptible population **every one of them would spread**, and they would spread **fastest for measles at R₀ = 15.0 and slowest for seasonal influenza at R₀ = 1.3.**

**4.** **After the control measures, seasonal influenza has R₀ = 0.8, which is below 1**, so **each case produces fewer than one new case on average** and **the number of cases will fall until the outbreak dies out.**

**5.** The quantity that makes this usable in a partly immune population is the **effective reproduction number**, which is **R₀ multiplied by the proportion of the population still susceptible**. `[INFERRED]` **Immunity reduces spread without changing the pathogen**, which is exactly why distractor D in part (a) is wrong.

**6.** Why this arithmetic is examinable at all rather than optional. The 9477 syllabus lists **"the use of Ro value"** in its **MATHEMATICAL REQUIREMENTS on page 25**, beside the **Hardy-Weinberg equation**, the **t-test** and the **chi-squared test**. `[OFFICIAL]` **No other Extension Topic A term appears anywhere outside its own outcome block**, so treat that as an instruction to **compute with R0, not only to explain it.**

**(d)** `[CONSTRUCTED, no real paper question exists]` *Calculate the percentage of the population that must be immune to break the transmission cycle of measles and of smallpox.* **[2]** `[INFERRED]`

**1.** **Measles: 1 − 1/15.0 = 1 − 0.0667 = 0.933, so 93.3% (accept 93%).**

**2.** **Smallpox: 1 − 1/5.0 = 1 − 0.200 = 0.800, so 80.0% (accept 80%).**

**3.** Two more worth having ready, because they make the relationship intuitive: **at R₀ = 2, half the population must be immune; at R₀ = 4, three quarters; at R₀ = 10, ninety per cent.**

**(e)** `[CONSTRUCTED, no real paper question exists]` *Suggest why smallpox was eradicated by vaccination in 1980 but measles has not been.* **[2]** `[INFERRED]`

**1.** **Smallpox needed only about 80% of the population to be immune to break the transmission cycle, which the WHO programme achieved, whereas measles needs about 93%, which is a far harder coverage target to reach and to hold.**

**2.** **Measles is much more transmissible (R₀ = 15.0 against 5.0), so any fall in vaccination coverage leaves enough susceptible people for transmission to resume**, whereas smallpox tolerated a larger susceptible fraction before spread restarted.

**3.** Accept any valid point that uses the smallpox properties from Question 6: **no animal reservoir**, **highly visible rash making cases easy to find for ring vaccination**, **short time from exposure to symptoms**, and **antigenic stability so one vaccine worked everywhere**.

**4.** **This is the bridge from LO (j) to LO (e), and it is the sentence that turns a herd immunity answer into an explanation.** Chapter 9.7 says only that the required proportion **varies for each disease** and stops. **The reason it varies is R0: the more transmissible the pathogen, the higher the proportion that must be vaccinated before the transmission cycle breaks.** It is also why the scheme in Question 7(c) refuses herd immunity for dengue: with under 1% of the population affected, the immune proportion is nowhere near any threshold.

### Question 10

**(a)** *What describes a virus such as influenza which emerges suddenly and spreads globally?* **[1]**

**1.** The answer is **C, pandemic.**

**2.** **A, endemic, is wrong.** Endemic describes the **constant background presence of a disease at an expected level** in a population or area. It is the reference level the other terms are measured against, not a description of sudden global spread.

**3.** **B, epidemic, is wrong here, and this is the option worth thinking about.** An epidemic is a **rise above the expected number of cases spread across a much larger area than an outbreak**, typically a region or a country. The stem says **spreads globally**, which is one rung higher.

**4.** **D, zoonotic, is wrong.** A zoonotic disease is one **transmitted from animals to humans**. That describes **where a pathogen came from**, not how far it has spread, so it is a different axis entirely.

**5.** **C, pandemic, is correct**, because a pandemic is **an epidemic that has spread across several countries or continents, affecting a large number of people.** "Emerges suddenly and spreads globally" is that definition restated.

**(b)** `[CONSTRUCTED, no real paper question exists]` *Outline what is meant by outbreak, epidemic and pandemic.* **[3]** `[INFERRED]`

**1.** **Outbreak: a rise in the number of cases above the number normally expected, confined to a small, localised area or a defined group of people, over a short period.** A school, a hospital ward or a town.

**2.** **Epidemic: the same rise above the expected number of cases, but spread across a much larger area**, typically a region or a whole country.

**3.** **Pandemic: an epidemic that has spread across several countries or continents, affecting a large number of people.**

**4.** **The ladder is about geographic extent, not about severity, and this is the point that has to be encoded deliberately because intuition says otherwise.** **A pandemic of a mild disease is still a pandemic, and a severe local outbreak is still only an outbreak.** Nothing in any of the three definitions mentions how ill anybody is.

**5.** The second thing the definitions alone will not give you: **the baseline matters.** Outbreak and epidemic are both defined **against the number of cases normally expected**, so **the same absolute number of cases can be an outbreak in one place and unremarkable in another where the disease is endemic.** That is why **endemic** has to be understood alongside the three, even though it is not one of them.

**(c)** `[CONSTRUCTED, no real paper question exists]` *For each of the three situations, state which term applies and justify your choice.* **[3]** `[INFERRED]`

**1.** **40 residents of one care home ill within a week: an outbreak.** The cases are **above the expected number for that setting**, but they are confined to **a small, localised, defined group** over a **short period**.

**2.** **National case numbers reaching 31 000 in a January when about 8 000 are expected: an epidemic.** Cases are **roughly four times the expected level** and the rise is **spread across a whole country**, not a single institution.

**3.** **The same subtype causing large numbers of cases on four continents: a pandemic.** The rise has **crossed several countries and continents** and affects **a large number of people**.

**4.** Why influenza is the syllabus's own example, and it is because **influenza occupies every rung at once.** **Seasonal influenza is endemic**, rising and falling around an expected level each year. **A localised rise in a care home is an outbreak.** **A national winter surge is an epidemic.** **A new subtype spreading between continents is a pandemic**, and it can do that because **the population has no pre-existing immunity**, which in R₀ terms means **the susceptible proportion is close to the whole population** and the effective reproduction number is close to R₀ itself.

**5.** The mechanism generating a new subtype is **antigenic shift and drift**, which is **Book 4**, Core Idea 2 LO 2(f). Know the link; do not write the reassortment mechanism into a Book 9 epidemiology answer unless the stem asks for it.
