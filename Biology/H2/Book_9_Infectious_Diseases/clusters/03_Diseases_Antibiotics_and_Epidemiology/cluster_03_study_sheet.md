## B. Influenza: how a virus destroys the tissue it infects

LO (g) writes the target into the outcome, so **the epithelial cells of the respiratory tract** are named
for you.

**B1. Transmission and entry.** Spread by **coughing and sneezing** in **respiratory droplets**, entering
through the **oral or nasal cavities**. The **primary targets are the epithelial cells lining the
respiratory tract**.

**B2. Neuraminidase is how the first line of defence fails.** The virus meets the **mucociliary layer**
from Cluster 1, which would normally trap it and sweep it out. **Neuraminidase cleaves the sialic acid
residues**, which **prevents the virus becoming trapped in the mucus** and so **facilitates infection of
the underlying cells**. That one enzyme step turns a barrier answer into an infection answer.

**B3. Damage arrives by two routes, and a full answer needs both.** Directly, **excessive budding depletes
the cell membranes of epithelial cells**, so **the epithelial lining of the respiratory tract is lost**,
and replication **depletes the host cell's raw materials**. Immune-mediated, **infected epithelial cells
die by apoptosis as a result of the host immune response mediated by activated cytotoxic T cells**, which
is Cluster 2's perforin and granzyme mechanism arriving as a symptom.

**B4. Then give the functional consequence, which is the outcome's own wording.** Losing the epithelium
means **losing the mucociliary escalator**, so **the first line of defence is gone** and **secondary
bacterial infection becomes likely**. LO (g) asks for **disruption of host tissue and functions**, so
stopping at tissue damage is an incomplete answer.

**B5. Symptoms follow from mechanisms.** **Sore throat** from **loss of epithelial lining**. **Excess
mucus and swelling** from inflammation. **Fever** because **cytokines act as endogenous pyrogens on the
hypothalamic thermoregulatory centre**. Trace each one, because a Suggest question will hand you an
unfamiliar symptom and expect the trace.

## C. HIV: how a virus disables the immune system itself

**C1. The definition carries the outcome.** **HIV is a retrovirus that infects and destroys helper T
cells**, and when the number of these cells is low the body **cannot defend itself**, so a range of
pathogens cause **opportunistic infections**.

**C2. Entry needs a receptor and a co-receptor.** HIV attacks **helper T cells with the CD4 receptor**,
plus **macrophages and dendritic cells**, and entry also requires **a co-receptor, either CCR5 or CXCR4**.
Cluster 2 gave you CD4 as the **helper T cell's co-receptor for MHC class II**, which is why this cell is
the target.

**C3. Integration buys the virus invisibility.** **Reverse transcriptase copies the single-stranded RNA
into double-stranded DNA**, and **integrase integrates the viral DNA into the host chromosome** as a
**provirus**. HIV therefore **establishes latent infection** in helper T cells and **remains invisible to
cytotoxic T cells**, so replication can resume later. **Release is by budding rather than lysis**, so
infected cells survive and keep producing virus.

**C4. The core damage mechanism is one sentence, linked back to Cluster 2.** **When helper T cell numbers
fall below a critical level, cell-mediated immunity is lost**, and the body becomes **progressively more
susceptible to opportunistic infections**. It is catastrophic because the helper T lymphocyte **activates
both B cells and cytotoxic T cells**, so **losing it disables both arms together**.

**C5. Three clinical phases, and this graph is the likeliest data stem in the chapter.**

| Phase | What is happening | Markers |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute** | **2 to 4 weeks after infection**; flu-like symptoms from **pro-inflammatory cytokines** | **high viraemia**, high transmission risk, settling to a **viral set point** |
| **Chronic latency** | integration allows **evasion**; **low-level replication** and **macrophage reservoirs** | **CD4 count falls slowly**, **viral load rises**, no symptoms |
| **Crisis (AIDS)** | **cell-mediated immunity collapses**; **opportunistic infections** appear | **helper T count below 200 cells mm⁻³** |

**The one number worth holding is 200 cells mm⁻³**, the operational definition of progression to AIDS.

**C6. AIDS is not the virus and not a disease.** **AIDS, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, is a
collection of opportunistic diseases** associated with immunodeficiency caused by HIV infection. The word
**syndrome** is what makes that sentence true.

## D. Bacterial transmission and infection

**D1. Three modes of transmission, each with named sub-modes.**

| Mode | Sub-modes | The wording |
|---|---|---|
| **Contact** | **direct**, **indirect**, **droplet** | direct has **no intermediary**; indirect passes through **fomites**, non-living articles such as bedding or needles; **smaller droplets are more dangerous**, because they stay airborne longer |
| **Vehicle** | **waterborne**, **foodborne**, **airborne**, **bodily fluid** | pathogens **riding along on supposedly clean components**; airborne means spread **via an aerosol**, a cloud of droplets and particles suspended in air |
| **Vector** | **mechanical**, **biological** | vectors are **animals, typically arthropods, that transmit disease between hosts**; mechanical vectors **only passively carry pathogens**, biological vectors **also host the pathogen** while it multiplies |

**D2. Four classes of virulence factor, which is the infection half of LO (h).** **Virulence factors are
characteristics of a pathogen that enhance its pathogenicity.** One mechanism and one example each.

1. **Adherence**, using **pili (fimbriae)**, **adhesion molecules** or **hydrophobic cell walls**. The
   reasoning: it prevents the bacteria being **carried away by mucus** or **washed from organs with
   significant fluid flow**, and lets each cell **form a microcolony**.
2. **Invasion**, using extracellular enzymes. **Hyaluronidase hydrolyses hyaluronic acid**, the ground
   substance cementing cells together; **collagenase breaks down collagen**; **coagulase clots blood
   proteins**, providing a hiding place; **kinase digests blood clots**.
3. **Toxicity**, covered in D3.
4. **Evasion of host defences**: **capsules that prevent activation of complement**; a mucoid capsule that
   **stops the phagocyte contacting the bacterium**; **chemicals that prevent fusion of lysosomes with
   phagocytic vesicles**; and against adaptive immunity, **changing surface antigens**, **IgA proteases**,
   and **proteins that bind the Fc portion of immunoglobulins**, blocking opsonisation.

**D3. Exotoxin against endotoxin, on five axes.**

| | **Exotoxins** | **Endotoxins** |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical nature | **proteins** | **lipopolysaccharides**, the **lipid portion** |
| Secreted | **secreted** | **not secreted**; integral components of the **cell wall** |
| Source | **gram-positive and gram-negative** | **gram-negative only** |
| Released | actively, during growth | **when cells die or are digested by phagocytes** |
| Effect | **cytotoxins kill host cells**, **neurotoxins interfere with nerve function**, **enterotoxins affect the gut lining** | **fever, shock, hypotension and thrombosis**, together **septic shock** |

**D4. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, transmission.** Named in the outcome, so write it in full and correctly.
It is **transmitted by droplet contact and airborne transmission**: **fine droplets of respiratory mucus
containing the bacteria are expelled into the air**, and **uninfected people inhale them**. Risk rises
with **overcrowding** and **low immunity**.

**D5. The infection chain, the single most examined item in the chapter.** An examiner comment names vague
versions of it as **lacking rigour**, so name the compartments.

**Droplet nuclei inhaled into the alveoli** → **alveolar macrophages engulf the bacteria by phagocytosis** → the pathogen sits in a **phagosome** → ***M. tuberculosis* modifies the compartment so lysosomal fusion cannot occur** → **no phagolysosome forms, so hydrolytic enzymes never reach the bacterium** → it **survives and replicates inside the macrophage** → a **tubercle forms** → **latency** → **rupture and active disease**.

A **tubercle** has **a central core of bacteria and enlarged macrophages**, with **an outer wall of
fibroblasts, lymphocytes and neutrophils**.

**D6. Latent against active tuberculosis, three axes and three marks.**

| | **Latent** | **Active** |
|---|---|---|
| Tubercle | **contained, but persists as a site of infection** | **expands and ruptures**, **damaging lung tissue and function** |
| Symptoms | **none** | **chest pain, cough, shortness of breath** |
| Contagious | **no** | **yes** |

## E. Antibiotics

**E1. Selective toxicity is the concept every antibiotic answer runs on.** It is the **ability to kill or
inhibit the infecting microbial cells but not the host's cells**, and it depends on **exploitable
biochemical differences between the infecting organism and the host**. An **antibiotic** is a substance
**produced naturally by some microorganisms that kills or inhibits the growth of others**.

**E2. Four modes of action, each with an example and its exploitable difference.**

| Mode | Example | What is exploited |
|---|---|---|
| **Cell wall synthesis** | **penicillin** | **eukaryotes have no peptidoglycan wall** |
| **Nucleic acid synthesis** | **sulfonamides and trimethoprim**, which competitively inhibit the enzymes synthesising **tetrahydrofolic acid (THFA)**; **quinolones** on **DNA gyrase**; **rifampin** on **bacterial RNA polymerase** | **mammals derive THFA from their diet** and lack the pathway |
| **Protein synthesis** | **aminoglycosides** and **tetracyclines** on the **30S subunit**; **chloramphenicol** and **erythromycin** on the **50S subunit** | **bacterial ribosomes are 70S, host ribosomes 80S**, and this must be stated, not implied |
| **Cell membrane integrity** | **polymyxins**, which **bind lipopolysaccharide** and disrupt the **outer and inner membranes** | the **outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria** |

**E3. Penicillin, end to end, because the outcome names it.** Peptidoglycan is a backbone of **alternating
N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM)**, **cross-linked by peptide bridges**, which
**protects the cell against rupture in hypotonic environments**. **Transpeptidation is the final step in
wall synthesis**, forming the **cross-links between NAM residues**, carried out by **transpeptidases**,
while **autolysins hydrolyse the bonds connecting NAG and NAM** so new wall can be inserted. Then:

**Penicillin binds and blocks the transpeptidase** → **no new cross-links form** → **autolysins keep cutting the existing peptidoglycan** → **the wall weakens** → **water enters by osmosis down a water potential gradient** → **the weakened wall cannot resist the pressure** → **the cell lyses**.

Penicillin is therefore **bacteriolytic**, and a scheme calls it an **irreversible competitive inhibitor
of transpeptidase**. **Penicillin G acts mainly on gram-positive bacteria**, because gram-negative
bacteria are impermeable to it.

**E4. β-lactamase is the counter-mechanism, and it belongs here.** Penicillin is a **β-lactam antibiotic
with a characteristic β-lactam ring**, and **β-lactamase breaks open that ring through hydrolysis**.

**E5. Why antibiotics do not act on viruses, in two lines.** **Viruses are not cells and do not have the
targets**: no peptidoglycan wall, no ribosomes of their own, no bacterial enzymes. A replicating virus
**uses the host cell's transcription and translation machinery**, and **antibiotics do not bind the
proteins host cells use**. **Antivirals are used instead.** Three separate rejects exist for vague
versions of this, so **name the missing target**.

## F. Vaccination

**F1. Definitions, and a boundary an examiner reduced to five characters.** A **vaccine is a preparation
containing antigens used to stimulate an immune response artificially**, and **vaccination is the act of
introducing a vaccine into the body** to produce immunity to a specific disease. **Vaccination confers
artificial active immunity**, one cell of Cluster 2's grid. The reject is blunt: **vaccines do not produce
antibodies**, and the examiner's note reads **Vaccines ≠ Antibodies ≠ Antibiotics**. **A vaccine contains
antigens; the body makes its own antibodies.**

**F2. LO (e) clause one, immunity without the disease.** Vaccination exposes the body to **antigens
similar to those on a pathogen**, which **develop immunity by imitating an infection**, and **this
infection does not cause illness**. The antigens **induce clonal expansion in specific T and B cells**,
leaving **memory lymphocytes**, so the next encounter with the same antigen **induces a secondary response
which is more rapid and effective**, where **the primary response is often too slow to prevent serious
disease**. That last clause is the argument; without it the answer is a description.

**F3. LO (e) clause two, breaking the transmission cycle.** **Herd immunity occurs when vaccination of a
significant portion of a population provides protection for individuals who have not developed
immunity.** The mechanism: **so few susceptible people are left to infect that the disease cannot
spread**. It therefore **reduces the reservoir of infected people**, **reduces transmission**, and
protects those who **cannot be vaccinated or respond poorly**, such as newborns, pregnant women, people on
chemotherapy and people with HIV. **When immunisation rates fall, herd immunity breaks down**, producing
**measles outbreaks in the UK** and **pertussis outbreaks in the US**.

Two disciplines. The **required proportion varies by disease**, and §G explains why. And a scheme
**rejects herd immunity where the proportion affected is under 1%**, so it is a claim about **the
proportion immune**, not a general-purpose explanation.

**F4. Smallpox eradication, the outcome's named case.** **Smallpox is caused by variola virus.** The
related **cowpox virus, vaccinia, causes a mild local blistering reaction**, and **Jenner showed that
administering vaccinia protects against smallpox**, because **the two viruses share an antigen** that
induces a protective response. **Eradication means the disease is eliminated worldwide**, and **smallpox
was declared eradicated in 1980**.

Two reasons it worked. First, **vaccination combined with focused surveillance and ring vaccination**,
where the household, the surrounding households and possible contacts were all vaccinated. Second,
**smallpox was a good candidate**, because **the disease is highly visible**, since patients develop an
obvious rash; because **the time from exposure to symptoms is short**, so it cannot spread far; and
because **only humans can be infected**, so there is **no animal reservoir** from which it could re-emerge.

**F5. Three vaccine types carry the whole risk argument.**

| Type | What it is | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Live attenuated** | living organisms with **virulence reduced by heat or chemicals** | **strong response, long-term protection** | may **revert to the virulent form**; needs refrigeration |
| **Inactivated (killed)** | **microbe killed with chemicals, heat or radiation** | **more stable and safer**; no refrigeration needed | **weaker response**, so **booster doses** are required |
| **Toxoid** | used **when a bacterial toxin is the main cause of illness**; **inactivated with formalin** so it is **no longer toxic but still stimulates a response** | targets the actual cause of damage | protects against the toxin, not colonisation |

**Extract, recombinant and DNA vaccines have no outcome behind them.** Skip them.

**F6. LO (f), benefits and risks, and the command word is discuss.**

| Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|
| **reduce rates of death and complications**; disease caught afterwards is **milder** | **immunity from vaccination may be less effective than natural immunity** |
| **protect the community**, since **high enough coverage prevents epidemics** | **live attenuated vaccines may revert to virulence** |
| **eradicate some diseases completely**, cutting suffering and treatment costs | **people with weak immune systems may not respond**, or may have **more side effects** |
| **save direct and indirect health care costs** | |

Two rules here. A scheme records that **inequitable access and cost are not risks of vaccination but
challenges to vaccinating**, and that **side effects mentioned without specifying them** score nothing.
And because the command is **discuss**, **no stand means zero marks**, and **both sides without a
judgement caps the answer at one**. Weigh both, then commit.

## G. R0, the basic reproduction number

**Your notes contain no R0 content at all.** The strings **R0**, **basic reproduction number**,
**transmissibility** and **contagiousness** return **zero hits across all 139 pages**, even though the
cover page promises epidemiology. **Specimen Paper 1 Q30 is an R0 question.** Everything below is built
from the syllabus and standard epidemiology, tagged accordingly.

**G1. The definition.** `[INFERRED]` **R0 is the mean number of secondary cases produced by one infected
individual introduced into a wholly susceptible population.** Three parts of that carry the meaning: it is
a **mean**, not a fixed count; it counts **secondary cases from one primary case**; and it assumes
**everybody else is susceptible**, which is why it describes a pathogen **entering a new population**
rather than circulating in a partly immune one. The outcome's own framing is that R0 is **an indicator of
the transmissibility, or contagiousness, of infectious agents**. `[OFFICIAL]`

**G2. The threshold is R0 = 1, and each side predicts a different future.** `[INFERRED]` This is what
LO (j) means by **predict the progress of a disease outbreak**. `[OFFICIAL]`

| Value | What it means | What happens next |
|---|---|---|
| **R0 > 1** | each case produces **more than one** new case | **case numbers grow** and the **outbreak spreads** |
| **R0 = 1** | each case produces **exactly one** new case | numbers **stay steady**; the disease is **endemic** |
| **R0 < 1** | each case produces **fewer than one** new case | **case numbers fall** and the **outbreak dies out** |

**Reason from the arithmetic, not the table.** If one case makes on average **two** further cases, the
chain runs 1, 2, 4, 8 and grows exponentially. If it makes **half a case**, the chain runs 1, 0.5, 0.25
and fades. **R0 = 1 is the tipping point**, and every control measure is an attempt to push a pathogen
from above it to below it.

**G3. Four things determine R0, and each is a lever for control.** `[INFERRED]`

1. **Contact rate**, the number of contacts per unit time. **Population density and overcrowding raise
   it**; **isolation and quarantine lower it**.
2. **Transmission probability per contact**, set by the **route** and the **infectious dose**. Masks,
   hygiene and barrier methods lower it.
3. **Duration of the infectious period.** **The longer a person stays infectious, the more secondary cases
   they generate**, so **treatment that shortens infectiousness lowers R0**.
4. **Vector abundance**, for vector-borne disease. **No vector present means no transmission**, whatever
   the pathogen.

**G4. The specimen's own distractors give two traps.** `[OFFICIAL]` Specimen P1 Q30 asked which conditions
**increase R0**. **More travel between countries where the disease is already endemic does not raise
R0**, because **those populations have already been exposed**. And **higher incidence in a population with
frequent outbreaks does not raise it either**, because **frequent outbreaks leave high immunity**, which
lowers transmission. **Incidence is not the same quantity as transmissibility.**

**G5. R0 is something you use, not only something you define.** `[OFFICIAL]` The syllabus lists **the use
of Ro value** in its **MATHEMATICAL REQUIREMENTS** on page 25, beside the **Hardy–Weinberg equation**, the
**t-test** and the **chi-squared test**. **No other Extension Topic A term appears anywhere outside its own
outcome block**, so treat this as an instruction to compute with R0, not only to explain it.

Two quantities follow. `[INFERRED]` The **effective reproduction number** is **R0 multiplied by the
proportion of the population still susceptible**, so **immunity reduces spread without changing the
pathogen**. And the **herd immunity threshold**, the proportion that must be immune before transmission
breaks, is **1 minus 1 divided by R0**. Worked through: **at R0 = 2, half the population must be immune**;
**at R0 = 4, three quarters**; **at R0 = 10, ninety per cent**.

**G6. This is the bridge from LO (j) to LO (e).** Chapter 9.7 says only that the proportion **varies for
each disease** and stops. **The reason it varies is R0**: **the more transmissible the pathogen, the
higher the proportion that must be vaccinated before the transmission cycle breaks.** That sentence turns
a herd immunity answer from an assertion into an explanation.

## H. Outbreak, epidemic and pandemic

**Also taught nowhere in your notes.** Chapter 9.1 is titled Definitions and Overview and its §1.2 is
headed KEY TERMINOLOGIES, so this is where the three should sit. **It defines twelve terms and none of
them is an epidemiological one.** Across the whole Book the three words appear **five times, all in
passing, and never defined**. The syllabus names **influenza** as the example. `[OFFICIAL]`

**H1. The three terms are a scale ladder, and the axis is geographic extent.** `[INFERRED]`

| Term | What it describes | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| **Endemic** | the **constant background presence** of a disease at an expected level | the reference level, not one of the three |
| **Outbreak** | a **rise in cases above the number normally expected**, in a **small, localised area or defined group**, over a **short period** | a school, a ward, a town |
| **Epidemic** | the same **rise above the expected level**, **spread across a much larger area** | a region or a country |
| **Pandemic** | an **epidemic that has spread across several countries or continents**, affecting **a large number of people** | multi-continental |

**H2. Two things follow that the definitions alone will not give you.** `[INFERRED]` First, **the ladder
is about spread, not about severity**. A pandemic of a mild disease is still a pandemic, and a severe
local outbreak is still only an outbreak. Second, **the baseline matters**: outbreak and epidemic are both
defined **against the number of cases normally expected**, so the same absolute count can be an outbreak
in one place and unremarkable in another where the disease is **endemic**.

**H3. Influenza is the syllabus's own example because it occupies every rung.** `[INFERRED]` **Seasonal
influenza is endemic**, rising and falling around an expected level each year. **A localised rise in a
care home is an outbreak.** **A national winter surge is an epidemic.** **A new subtype spreading between
continents is a pandemic**, and it can do that because **the population has no pre-existing immunity**,
which in R0 terms means **the susceptible proportion is close to the whole population**. The mechanism
generating that new subtype is **antigenic shift and drift**, which is **Book 4**.
