## A. The definitions the whole topic runs on

Chapter 9.1 §1.2 on printed pages 4 and 5 is the most reliable page in the Book.

**A1. Disease and infectious disease are defined separately, and the second is the examinable one.**
A disease is a **disorder or illness that disrupts the normal functioning of the body or mind**, and each
is associated with a **set of signs and symptoms**. Infectious diseases are **caused by pathogens and
passed from infected to uninfected people**, so they are also called **communicable diseases**. That
transmissibility clause is what makes a disease infectious, and Cluster 3's epidemiology outcomes depend
on it.

**A2. Pathogen, host and pathogenicity, and pathogenicity is the one that carries reasoning.**
**Pathogens are disease-causing microorganisms**, namely **viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoans,
parasitic worms, and prions (infectious proteins)**. A **host is an organism that harbours a parasite**,
which lives on or in it and causes damage. **Pathogenicity is the ability of a pathogen to inflict damage
on the host**, and it **differs considerably among potential pathogens**, as does the **resistance or
susceptibility of the host**. That last clause is worth holding: **whether infection produces disease
depends on two variables at once**, what the pathogen brings and what the host brings.

**A3. Immunity, immune response and immune system are three different nouns.** **Immunity is resistance
to diseases, specifically infectious diseases.** An immune response is a **collective and coordinated
response to the introduction of foreign substances**, mediated by the cells and molecules of the immune
system. The immune system is the **collection of cells, tissues and molecules that mediate resistance to
infections**, and its primary role is to **recognise self from non-self**. **Self against non-self is the
single distinction the whole system rests on.**

**A4. Antigen, antigen presentation and antigen-presenting cell are named directly in LOs (a), (b) and
(c).** An **antigen is any substance that can be recognised by the immune system**, and it binds to an
**antibody or a T cell receptor (TCR)**, which **elicits a B or T cell response respectively**. Antigen
presentation is the **display of peptides bound by MHC molecules on the surface of an antigen-presenting
cell**, permitting **specific recognition by T cell receptors and activation of T cells**. An
antigen-presenting cell **displays peptide fragments of protein antigens in association with MHC
molecules**, and **activates antigen-specific T cells**. Learn these now, in these words, because
Cluster 2 assumes them.

**A5. Specificity and memory are the two features that separate the two systems.** Every
innate-against-adaptive comparison turns on them, and **neither belongs to the innate system**.

## B. How a pathogen causes disease, in outline

Cluster 3 supplies the named cases. What follows is the shape all of them share.

**B1. A pathogen must enter, reproduce and exploit the host, and entry happens at a portal of entry.**
Portals map one to one onto **modes of transmission**. Prevention acts on the transmission step, by
**personal hygiene, sanitation and chlorination**.

**B2. Causing disease is a cycle, not an event.** The school states the requirement plainly: **multiple
features, called virulence factors, are required to persist, cause disease, and escape to repeat the
cycle**. A successful pathogen therefore does **four things: get in, survive the defences it meets,
damage the host, and get out to a new host**. The four classes of virulence factor belong to LO (h) and
Cluster 3. What matters here is that **surviving the host's defences is one of the four requirements**,
which is why the innate system is the thing pathogens are built to defeat.

**B3. Damage arrives by two routes, and candidates routinely give only the first.** The pathogen damages
tissue **directly**, by destroying cells, depleting their materials or releasing toxins. The **host's own
response damages tissue as well**, through **inflammation, fever and the killing of infected cells**.
**Loss of function follows loss of tissue**, which is the wording LO (g) uses: **disruption of host
tissue and functions**. Hold that two-route structure, because it is the whole influenza answer later.

## C. Three lines of defence, in a fixed order

**C1. The first line stops entry.** It is a set of **natural physical barriers that limit the entry of
microorganisms into the body**, and the school attaches a standing clause to it that is the
non-specificity mark in disguise: they **operate regardless of the exact nature of the invader**.

**C2. The second line acts inside the body.** If the surface barriers are penetrated, a variety of
**non-specific internal defences, collectively called the innate immune response**, swing into action.
Lines one and two are both innate.

**C3. The third line is adaptive.** Immune cells, the **B and T lymphocytes**, **selectively destroy the
specific invading toxin or microbe and then remember the invader**, allowing a faster response if it
reappears. **Selectively** and **remember** are the two words that make it the third line rather than
more of the second.

**C4. The response runs in three phases, and adaptive immunity is late for a reason.** The phases are the
**innate phase**, the **early induced innate response**, and the **adaptive immune response**. Adaptive
immunity occurs late because the **rare B and T cells specific for the invading pathogen must first
undergo clonal expansion** before they differentiate into effector cells. That is the mechanism behind
the delay, and it is what makes the primary response slow in Cluster 2.

**C5. Five features define innate immunity, and one of them has to be stated carefully.**

1. **Always present, from birth**, and available **before the onset of infection**.
2. **Immediately available** to combat a wide range of pathogens.
3. **Non-specific**, and it **does not lead to lasting immunity**, so it is the **same magnitude on every
   re-exposure**.
4. **It instructs the adaptive system**, which is what the dendritic cell does.
5. It is **phylogenetically older** and **common to all animals**, while adaptive immunity is **found
   only in vertebrates**.

**The careful one is number three.** Chapter 9.2 calls innate components **non-specific** on printed page
15, then says **all these mechanisms of innate immunity specifically recognize and react against
microbes** on page 16. Read literally, those contradict. Chapter 9.3 resolves it: innate mechanisms
**recognize structures shared by classes of microbes**, as opposed to recognising one individual
pathogen. **Never write that innate immunity is specific**, because LO (a) itself calls it **the
non-specific (innate) immune system**.

## D. The first line is a set of external barriers

**Do not skip this section because it looks like a list.** A marker's note in the corpus reads that a
first-line question is **on external non-specific barriers** and that **adaptive immune responses were
not accepted**. These are cheap marks thrown away by answering the topic instead of the stem.

**D1. Physical and mechanical barriers.** The **epidermis** is a **dry, watertight barrier continually
sloughing dead cells (keratinocytes)**. The **mucous-membrane epithelium** lines the **gastrointestinal,
respiratory and urogenital tracts**. Mucus is a **viscous fluid that traps pathogens and other
particles**, and **ciliated epithelial cells sweep mucus and any entrapped material upward**, helping
prevent infection of the lungs. Add the **washing action of saliva and tears**. Mucus and cilia together
are the **mucociliary escalator**, and **losing it is how influenza disables the first line of defence**.

**D2. Chemical and environmental barriers.** **Acidic pH** on the **skin**, in the **stomach at pH 1.0 to
3.0**, and in the **vagina**. Then **microcidal secreted molecules**, of which the named one is
**lysozyme**: sweat contains **lysozyme, an enzyme that breaks down peptidoglycan**, a constituent of most
bacterial cell walls. **Peptidoglycan is also penicillin's target**, so link this forward.
**Antimicrobial peptides, RNases and DNases** complete the inventory, which is not itself examinable.

**D3. Biological barriers.** **Commensal microbes exist in a symbiotic relationship with the body.** They
colonise the skin and gastrointestinal tract, **defend their territory and inhibit the establishment of
other potentially pathogenic microbes**. The mechanism is **competitive exclusion**: commensals occupy
the niche, so a pathogen arriving later finds no space or nutrients. This also explains **opportunistic
disease in the immunocompromised**.

## E. The second line runs on four named cells

**E1. Phagocytes are a class, not a cell.** A group of **white blood cells, leukocytes, collectively
called phagocytes, ingests invaders by phagocytosis**. They are **motile, moving by amoeboid action**,
and most have **lysosomes, which contain bactericidal substances such as hydrogen peroxide, lysozyme,
proteases, phosphatases, nucleases and lipases**. **Note where hydrogen peroxide lives: in a phagocyte's
lysosome.** Cluster 2 needs that, because the notes later attribute it to the wrong cell.

**E2. Learn one distinguishing fact per cell, because that is how the specimen tested it.** 9477 Specimen
Paper 1 Q29 built **every wrong option out of one cell type placed wrongly**, so identification runs on
**what a cell carries and what it does**, not on name recognition.

| Cell | What it is | Distinguishing fact | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Neutrophil** | **most numerous leukocyte**, about 60% of blood leukocytes, polymorphonuclear | **first cells recruited to acute inflammatory sites** | **ingest, kill and digest microbial pathogens**; dying ones form **pus** |
| **Macrophage** | a **monocyte in the circulation**, a **macrophage once it enters the tissues** | **tissue-resident and long-lived**, and an **antigen-presenting cell** | **samples its environment by phagocytosis** and scavenges **cellular debris** |
| **Dendritic cell** | phagocytic cell named for its **branchlike cytoplasmic projections** | sits at the **common portals of entry** | **antigen-presenting cell** that activates **helper T cells, cytotoxic T cells and B cells**; the **bridge between innate and adaptive immunity** |
| **Natural killer cell** | **large, non-phagocytic, granular lymphocyte**, 5% to 10% of circulating lymphocytes | **kills without any antigen-specific receptor** | **non-specific defence against abnormal (infected or malignant) host cells**, **before the viruses can reproduce and spread** |

**E3. The dendritic cell is the reason the two systems are one system.** It ingests pathogens at the
portals of entry and **displays their antigens at its surface in a form that can be recognised by T
cells**, so **innate detection becomes adaptive activation**. That is feature four of §C5 made concrete.

**E4. Natural killer cells kill host cells, not pathogens.** That distinction is the examinable one:
**a phagocyte destroys the microbe; an NK cell destroys the body cell harbouring it.** The purpose is
containment, **eliminating reservoirs of infection** before replication finishes.

## F. Defensive proteins: cytokines and complement

**F1. Cytokines are the signalling arm.** Cytokines are **secreted proteins that function as mediators of
immune and inflammatory reactions**, and in innate responses they are **produced by macrophages and NK
cells**. The named antiviral example is **interferon**, which **travels to other cells and increases their
resistance to viral attack**. **Write interferon, not cytokine, whenever the answer is about antiviral
resistance.**

**F2. Complement is the killing arm, and it has exactly three effects.** The complement system is a
**group of at least 20 proteins** whose activities **enhance the body's other defence mechanisms**, and
until activated by infection they **circulate in the blood in an inactive state**. The three effects are
three separate marks:

1. **Destruction of the pathogen by pore formation.** The **membrane attack complex forms a pore**, so
   **ions and water rush into the cell, causing it to swell and lyse**.
2. **Enhancement of phagocytosis**, which is **opsonisation**.
3. **Stimulation of inflammation.**

Chapter 9.6 repeats the same three under bacterial infection, which is a strong signal the school treats
them as a fixed set. **Complement is also the clearest case of adaptive immunity enhancing innate
immunity**, because antibody bound to antigen triggers the same cascade in Cluster 2.

## G. Inflammation, phagocytosis and fever

**G1. Inflammation delivers the defences to the site.** Inflammation is a **tissue reaction that delivers
mediators of host defence to sites of infection and tissue damage**, by **recruitment of cells and
leakage of plasma proteins through blood vessels**. Run the chain in order:

**Tissue damage** → **histamine released from mast cells** → **blood vessels dilate and become more permeable** → **increased local blood flow, exudation of plasma proteins, triggering of nerve endings** → **redness, warmth, swelling and pain** → **neutrophils accumulate** → dying neutrophils form **pus**.

**The four cardinal signs are consequences of two vascular changes**, vasodilation and increased
permeability. Derive them rather than memorise them, because a Suggest question will hand you an
unfamiliar inflamed tissue and expect the derivation.

**G2. Phagocytosis is a four-stage sequence and the compartment names are marked.** **Recognition and
attachment**, then the **cell surface membrane engulfs the pathogen** and traps it **within a vacuole by
endocytosis**, then **fusion with lysosomes**, then **digestive enzymes are secreted into the vacuole**,
destroying the pathogen. Your notes call that compartment three different things across the Book, a
phagocytic vacuole, a phagosome and an endosome. **Use phagosome, becoming a phagolysosome after lysosome
fusion.** An examiner comment in the corpus names vague versions of this sequence as **lacking rigour**,
and the reason is that **the whole tuberculosis answer in Cluster 3 turns on the fusion step**.

**G3. Fever is innate, and it has four listed effects.** **An elevated body temperature increases the
activity of phagocytic leukocytes while simultaneously slowing bacterial reproduction.** It also causes
**iron deficiency**, **speeds the proliferation of adaptive cells**, and **stimulates virus-infected
cells to produce interferon**. Chapter 9.5 reuses two of these verbatim as influenza symptoms.

## H. Innate against adaptive, compared point for point

A stock three-mark table in the corpus, and the comparison LO (a) sets up by naming both systems in one
outcome.

| Axis | **Innate (non-specific)** | **Adaptive (specific)** |
|---|---|---|
| Present | **from birth, before infection** | **developed during the lifetime of the individual** |
| Speed | **immediate** | **slower to start**, because clonal expansion must happen first |
| Specificity | **non-specific**; recognises **structures shared by classes of microbes** | **specific**; receptors recognise **a much wider variety of individual antigens** |
| Memory | **none**, so **no lasting immunity** | **immunological memory**, giving **lifelong protective immunity to reinfection with the same pathogen** |
| Re-exposure | **same magnitude every time** | **larger, faster and more effective on repeat exposure** |
| Cells | **neutrophil, macrophage, dendritic cell, NK cell**, plus barriers and defensive proteins | **B lymphocytes and T lymphocytes** |
| Distribution | **common to all animals** | **found only in vertebrates** |

**The two systems are not sequential rivals; each feeds the other.** **Innate instructs adaptive**
through the dendritic cell presenting antigen. **Adaptive enhances innate** through antibody, because
**opsonisation makes phagocytosis more efficient and antigen-antibody complexes activate complement**.
That sentence is usually the mark separating a full comparison from a list.

## K. Errors in your notes, and the corrected versions

**Four errors, learn the corrected version.**

1. **Dendritic cells are called differentiated macrophages on printed page 19**, while the same page also
   correctly calls them **a third group of phagocytic cells with both myeloid and lymphoid origins**. A
   dendritic cell is not a macrophage that differentiated further. **Write dendritic cell and macrophage
   as two separate named antigen-presenting cells**, never one as a form of the other, because LO (b)
   names antigen-presenting cells as a class and a scheme may want more than one example.
2. **Natural killer cells are said to detect infected cells by MHC display on printed page 20**, which
   inverts the mechanism. **NK cells respond to the loss of MHC class I** from a stressed or infected
   cell, and **a healthy cell displaying normal MHC class I is the one an NK cell spares**. Answer with
   the version the same page gives correctly two bullets earlier: NK cells **kill infected and abnormal
   host cells without needing antigen-specific receptors**.
3. **The chapter contradicts itself on specificity within two pages.** Resolved in §C5. **Innate
   receptors recognise structures shared by classes of microbes**, and the outcome's own word is
   **non-specific**.
4. **The phagocytosis account on printed page 24 assumes antibodies are already present**, describing
   pathogens as **covered in antibodies**. **Antibodies are adaptive.** The biology is right and the
   ordering is wrong: that is **antibody-dependent opsonisation**, given properly in chapter 9.3. Do not
   use antibody in an answer about the second line of defence acting alone.

**One documentation error that changes what you revise.** Printed page 1 carries a second outcome block
headed **LEARNING OUTCOMES FOR SECTION 1 TO SECTION 4**, and its **(a)** and **(d)** are the **old
9744-era wordings**. That (a) reads **active and passive, natural and acquired immunity**, which makes
natural and acquired look like the opposed pair. They are not. **The current (a) is a two-by-two grid of
active or passive against naturally or artificially acquired.** **Cross out the Section 1 to 4 block.**
