Answer - part (a)

(a) With reference to Fig. 1.1, describe the role played by the gut epithelial cells in preventing infections of S. typhimurium. [3]

Any 3.

1. Epithelial cells form a layer, serving as an anatomical / physical barrier to prevent entry of S. typhimurium into the body.

(c) Human lysozyme is an important enzyme which is part of the human body's defence against some bacteria. It is found in abundance in tears, saliva and mucous. Answer - part (c)

(c) Suggest why some bacteria are more resistant to digestion by lysozymes. [1]

1. These bacteria cells have a slime layer that makes the β-(1,4) glycosidic bonds less accessible to the lysozymes.

The same wall is penicillin's target in Cluster 3, so the two answers share a molecule and nothing else.

Answer - part (d)

(d) With reference to Fig. 1.2, explain why H. pylori is able to colonize the gastric epithelial cells despite the harsh environment of the stomach. [2] [INFERRED: no official key for this paper]

1. Its flagella allow it to swim through and burrow beneath the thick mucus layer, so it escapes the very low pH of the stomach lumen and lives in the near-neutral environment next to the epithelial surface.

2. It also secretes urease, which hydrolyses urea to ammonia and neutralises the acid immediately around the cell, protecting its own enzymes and membrane proteins from denaturation.

3. The transferable point, which is the reason this part is in the set: the chemical barrier and the physical barrier are separate defences, and a pathogen can defeat one while the other still holds. The stomach's pH of 1.0 to 3.0 is a first-line chemical barrier; the mucus that protects the host's own epithelium from that acid is the thing the bacterium hides inside.

(a) The diagram shows the process of phagocytosis of a pathogen by a neutrophil. Which row shows the correct identification of the structures and function? [1]

AP antibiotic, Q fimbriae, R lysozyme, S lysosome; innate immunity.
BP antibiotic, Q pseudopodia, R Golgi vesicles, S phagosome; cell-mediated immunity.
CP antigen, Q fimbriae, R Golgi vesicles, S lysozyme; cell-mediated immunity.
DP antigen, Q pseudopodia, R lysosome, S phagosome; innate immunity.
Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (a)

(a) Which row shows the correct identification of the structures and function? [1] [INFERRED: the official key gives the letter only; the audit below is worked out here]

1. The answer is D.

2. P is an antigen, not an antibiotic. An antigen is any substance that can be recognised by the immune system; an antibiotic is a substance produced naturally by some microorganisms that kills or inhibits the growth of others and has nothing to do with a neutrophil recognising a pathogen. That rules out A and B.

3. Q is a pseudopodium, an extension of the cell surface membrane, not of a cell wall. Neutrophils are animal cells and have no cell wall, and fimbriae (pili) are bacterial adherence structures, which is why they appear in this option list at all. That rules out A and C.

4. R is the lysosome, the membrane-bound organelle containing hydrolytic enzymes. Golgi vesicles are secretory, and lysozyme is an enzyme, not an organelle, so it cannot label a membrane-bound structure. That rules out B and C.

5. S is the phagosome, the vacuole containing the engulfed pathogen. After fusion with the lysosome it becomes the phagolysosome. Your notes call this compartment a phagocytic vacuole, a phagosome and an endosome in three different places; use phagosome, then phagolysosome.

6. The line of defence is innate immunity. Phagocytosis by a neutrophil is second line and non-specific, so cell-mediated and humoral are both wrong, which finally separates D from B and C.

(b) Describe the mode of action of this phagocyte in killing bacteria cells. [3]

Question 3 [9 marks]

(a) The figure below illustrates an infection by influenza virus. Which one of the following statements is correct? [1]

AThe presence of mucus facilitates the movement of influenza virus into the lung.
BMacrophages are examples of cells that express both MHC Class I and Class II.
CDendritic cells (DCs) are part of the adaptive immunity and are responsible for secreting immunoglobulin.
DThe epithelial cells lining the upper respiratory tract (URT) are not part of the immune system.
Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (a)

(a) Which one of the following statements is correct? [1]

1. The correct answer is B.

2. B is correct. A macrophage is a nucleated body cell, so like every nucleated cell it expresses MHC class I, and it is also an antigen-presenting cell, so it expresses MHC class II as well. Class I presents to cytotoxic T lymphocytes and class II to helper T lymphocytes.

3. A is wrong, and it inverts the mucus. Mucus is a viscous fluid that traps pathogens and other particles, and ciliated epithelial cells sweep mucus and any entrapped material upward. Mucus obstructs the virus; it does not carry it inward. The one case where a virus turns this around is influenza's neuraminidase cleaving sialic acid residues so the virus is not trapped, which is Cluster 3, and even there the mucus is being defeated, not used.

4. C is wrong twice, and it is the option that punishes your own notes. Dendritic cells are innate, not adaptive: they are phagocytic cells at the common portals of entry and they are the bridge between innate and adaptive immunity. And only plasma cells secrete immunoglobulin. Printed page 19 of your Book calls dendritic cells differentiated macrophages; the same page also correctly calls them a third group of phagocytic cells with both myeloid and lymphoid origins. Write dendritic cell and macrophage as two separate named antigen-presenting cells.

5. D is wrong. The epithelial cells lining the upper respiratory tract are the first line of defence, which is part of the immune system by definition. The first line is a set of natural physical barriers that limit the entry of microorganisms into the body, and they operate regardless of the exact nature of the invader.

(b) Natural killer cell deficiency (NKD) is a primary immunodeficiency where the main defect lies in NK cells. One possible cause of NKD is the GATA2 mutation. GATA2 is a transcription factor that regulates the proliferation, differentiation and survival of blood stem cells. GATA2 has a zinc finger domain that allows for DNA-protein interaction. Natural killer (NK) cells are innate immune cells that show strong cytolytic functions against physiologically stressed cells such as tumour cells and virus-infected cells. Suggest how GATA2 mutation may lead to increased incidence of cancer. [4]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (b)

(b) Suggest how GATA2 mutation may lead to increased incidence of cancer. [4]

1. Failure of GATA2 transcription factor to bind to DNA of blood stem cells prevents differentiation into NK cells.

2. Loss of immunity.

3. Decrease in population of NK cells to bind to tumour cells.

4. Reduction in cytolytic ability of immune system to remove tumour cells, hence increased chance of cancer.

5. The correction your notes need. Printed page 20 says natural killer cells detect infected cells by MHC display, which inverts the mechanism. NK cells respond to the loss of MHC class I from a stressed, infected or malignant cell, and a healthy cell displaying normal MHC class I is the one an NK cell spares. The version the same page gives correctly two bullets earlier is the one to use: NK cells kill infected and abnormal host cells without needing antigen-specific receptors.

6. The distinction the exam actually tests here is what the cell kills. A phagocyte destroys the microbe; an NK cell destroys the body cell harbouring it, or the malignant cell, and the purpose is eliminating reservoirs of infection before the viruses can reproduce and spread.

7. Reach-back check. GATA2 is a transcription factor with a zinc finger domain, which is Book 2, and the immune surveillance of tumour cells is Book 3. If either half stalled you, log it as a Book 2 or Book 3 gap.

Question 4 [10 marks]

This is the full-cycle question. It runs one continuous chain: a pathogen crosses a barrier, the innate system recognises it, phagocytes are recruited, inflammation follows, cytokines reach the hypothalamus, symptoms appear, and then the last part asks you what happens when the same chain is too small to produce any symptom at all. Answer (a) to (d) as one story.

(a) The immune system is the body's defence against infectious organisms and other invaders. Through a series of steps called the immune response, the immune system attacks organisms and substances that invade body systems and cause disease. Describe how the innate immune system normally responds to a microbial infection in the skin tissue, such as DENV. [3]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (a)

(a) Describe how the innate immune system normally responds to a microbial infection in the skin tissue, such as DENV. [3]

1. (Tissue-resident) dendritic cells / Langerhans cells and macrophages recognise the PAMPs on the microbes by their PRRs.

2. This results in (receptor-mediated) phagocytosis of the bound microbe.

3. The phagocyte becomes activated and secretes cytokines.

4. Which aid in the recruitment of circulating phagocytes (monocytes / neutrophils) from the blood circulation into the infected tissue.

5. Resulting in inflammation.

6. The recruited phagocytes assist in the elimination of the microbes at the site of infection.

7. The one marking point this part shares with Question 2 is phagocytosis happens. Everything else here is recognition, signalling and recruitment, and everything in Question 2 is the compartments inside the cell. If you found yourself giving Question 2's phagosome answer here, you answered the wrong half of the outcome.

(b) The body's immune system normally functions to protect itself by attacking pathogens like bacteria and viruses. In autoimmune diseases, the immune system mistakenly attacks cells in the body instead. Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial membrane, which is a layer of connective tissue that lines the cavities of joints, causing inflammation. Fig. 4.1 shows both a healthy joint and an arthritic joint. With reference to Fig. 4.1, outline the inflammatory response in the arthritic joint and its resultant effects. [3]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (b)

(b) With reference to Fig. 4.1, outline the inflammatory response in the arthritic joint and its resultant effects. [3]

Any 3.

1. Macrophages and dendritic cells secrete signalling proteins / chemokines / cytokines.

2. Signalling proteins / chemokines / cytokines result in increased blood flow / dilation of blood vessels to joint, resulting in redness and warmth in the joint.

3. Signalling proteins / chemokines / cytokines result in increased permeability of blood vessels to recruit neutrophils (accept white blood cells) and other plasma proteins / complements to joint, resulting in fluid accumulating / swelling in the joint.

4. From the figure: eventually leading to thickening of the synovial membrane / bulging of the joint / bone erosion by osteoclast.

5. Derive the cardinal signs, do not memorise them. The four cardinal signs are consequences of exactly two vascular changes, vasodilation and increased permeability. Vasodilation gives redness and warmth because more blood arrives; increased permeability gives swelling because plasma proteins and fluid leak out; pain follows from the swelling triggering nerve endings. A Suggest question will hand you an unfamiliar inflamed tissue and expect exactly this derivation.

6. The full chain in your notes, for checking: tissue damage → histamine released from mast cells → blood vessels dilate and become more permeable → increased local blood flow, exudation of plasma proteins, triggering of nerve endings → redness, warmth, swelling and pain → neutrophils accumulate → dying neutrophils form pus.

(c) COVID-19 is a respiratory disease. Early signs of COVID-19 infection include a fever, a sore throat and headache. Explain how the COVID-19 virus may cause these symptoms. [2]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (c)

(c) Explain how the COVID-19 virus may cause a fever, a sore throat and a headache. [2]

1. The virus stimulates / activates the innate / non-specific immune response / immune cells (e.g. dendritic cells), resulting in cytokines and chemokines being released by (alveolar) macrophages.

2. Which increased permeability of blood vessels resulting in inflammation, causing the sore throat and headache.

3. Pyrogen released by activated macrophages leads to a rise in systemic body temperature resulting in fever. Accept: protein from liver leads to increase in temperature.

4. The scheme for the parallel dengue and malaria versions of this question adds one instruction in bold: award marks only when reference is made to the respective symptoms. Naming the mechanism without attaching it to the named symptom scores nothing.

5. Fever is innate and it has four listed effects worth holding: it increases the activity of phagocytic leukocytes while simultaneously slowing bacterial reproduction, it causes iron deficiency, it speeds the proliferation of adaptive cells, and it stimulates virus-infected cells to produce interferon.

(d) According to studies, as many as three out of ten people carrying the influenza virus have no symptoms of influenza and are at risk of transmitting viral particles to other susceptible individuals. Using your knowledge of host-pathogen interactions, suggest how this phenomenon is possible. [2]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (d)

(d) Suggest how three out of ten people carrying influenza can have no symptoms. [2]

1. For symptoms to show, inflammatory responses must be triggered, and sufficient damage must be caused to respiratory tract tissue.

2. OR in early infection of influenza virus, not many respiratory epithelial cells are infected yet / have not caused enough damage to respiratory tract tissue. Hence the individual will have no symptoms.

3. Viral particles have been released into the body / bloodstream / mucus and can be transmitted through contaminated fluid / air.

4. This is the part that closes the cycle, and it is worth saying out loud why. Parts (b) and (c) establish that the symptoms are the host's response, not the pathogen. Part (d) then makes you use that: if the response is small, the symptoms are absent, and yet the pathogen is still being shed.
