## 2.1 DNA Structure and Replication

1. A **nucleotide** is a **phosphate**, a **deoxyribose sugar** and a **nitrogenous base**; nucleotides join by **phosphodiester bonds** into a sugar-phosphate backbone.
2. The two strands are **antiparallel** — one runs 5'→3', the other 3'→5' — and they are held together by **hydrogen bonds** between **complementary bases**: **A–T with two**, **G–C with three**.
3. DNA suits heredity because it **stores information stably**, can be **replicated accurately**, and can still **vary** — and every one of those three is a structural consequence.
4. **RNA** differs by having **ribose**, **uracil** instead of thymine, and being **single-stranded**. The three types are **mRNA** (carries the message), **tRNA** (brings amino acids), **rRNA** (builds the ribosome).
5. Replication is **semi-conservative**: each new molecule is one old strand and one new one.
6. Because polymerases only build **5'→3'**, one strand is made continuously (**leading**) and the other in pieces (**lagging**, as **Okazaki fragments**). The asymmetry is forced by the chemistry, not chosen.
7. The job is shared out: **helicase** unwinds, **single-strand binding proteins** hold the strands apart, **topoisomerase** relieves the strain ahead, **primase** lays down RNA primers, **DNA polymerase III** extends, **DNA polymerase I** swaps primer for DNA, **ligase** seals the nicks.
8. Linear chromosomes cannot be finished at their very ends, so they shorten each round — the **end replication problem**. Circular chromosomes do not have it.

## 2.2 Eukaryotic Gene Expression

1. Information flows **DNA → RNA → protein**, and only one DNA strand, the **template strand**, is read; the other is the **coding strand**.
2. The **genetic code** is read in **triplets** (codons), is **degenerate** (several codons per amino acid), **non-overlapping**, and near-**universal**.
3. **Transcription** copies a gene into **pre-mRNA**: RNA polymerase binds the **promoter** with **transcription factors**, elongates 5'→3', then terminates.
4. Eukaryotic pre-mRNA is **processed** before it leaves the nucleus: a **5' cap** is added, a **3' poly(A) tail** is added, and **introns are spliced out** by the **spliceosome** while **exons** are joined.
5. **Alternative splicing** means one gene can yield **several different polypeptides** — which is why gene count and protein count don't match.
6. **Translation** builds the polypeptide at the **ribosome**: each **codon** on mRNA pairs with the **anticodon** on a **tRNA** carrying the right amino acid, and **peptide bonds** join them in order.
7. Translation runs through **initiation**, **elongation** with **translocation**, and **termination** at a **stop codon**.
8. The chain is not finished when it leaves the ribosome — **post-translational modification** folds, cuts and decorates it into the working protein.

## 2.3 Organisation of the Eukaryotic Genome

1. A eukaryotic genome is enormous relative to the nucleus, so it must be **packed** — and packing is not just storage, it decides **access**.
2. The packing hierarchy is **DNA wound round a histone octamer** to make a **nucleosome**, nucleosomes coiled into a **30 nm fibre**, then **looped domains**, then the **condensed chromosome**.
3. **Euchromatin** is loosely packed and **accessible for transcription**; **heterochromatin** is tightly packed and largely **silent**. Packing state is therefore a control mechanism in its own right.
4. Most eukaryotic DNA does **not** code for protein or RNA — it is **non-coding DNA**, and it has jobs.
5. The non-coding parts that matter are **introns**, **centromeres**, **telomeres**, **promoters**, **enhancers** and **silencers**.
6. **Centromeres** hold sister chromatids together for division; **telomeres** cap the chromosome ends and absorb the shortening from the end replication problem.
7. Genomes differ in **linearity or circularity**, **presence or absence of introns**, **gene density**, and **how much is repetitive** — and those differences are the comparison the exam wants.

## 2.4 Control of Eukaryotic Gene Expression

1. Every cell in an organism carries the **same genome**, so cells differ by **which genes they express** — **differential gene expression**, in **space** and in **time**.
2. Control is possible at **five levels**, and the syllabus names all five. Knowing three is not knowing the topic.
3. **Chromatin level** — **histone modification** (acetylation loosens, deacetylation and methylation tighten) and **DNA methylation** decide whether the gene can be reached at all.
4. **Transcriptional level** — **control elements** (**promoters**, **enhancers**, **silencers**) and **proteins** (**transcription factors**, split into **activators** and **repressors**) decide whether transcription starts.
5. **Post-transcriptional level** — **splicing** (including alternative splicing), **polyadenylation** and **5' capping** decide which mRNA, and how stable, leaves the nucleus.
6. **Translational level** — the **half-life of the RNA** and the **initiation of translation** decide how much protein gets made from that mRNA.
7. **Post-translational level** — **biochemical modification** and **protein degradation** decide how much working protein actually persists.
8. The through-line: control is exerted at every step from **packed DNA** to **degraded protein**, and the earlier the step, the more economical the control.

## 2.5 Gene and Chromosomal Mutations

1. A **gene mutation** is a change in the **base sequence**; a **chromosomal aberration** is a change in chromosome **number or structure**. Different scale, different consequences.
2. Gene mutations come as **substitution**, **addition** or **deletion** of bases.
3. A **substitution** changes at most one amino acid — and because the code is **degenerate**, it may change nothing at all (**silent**), change one residue (**missense**), or create a stop (**nonsense**).
4. An addition or deletion that is **not a multiple of three** shifts the reading frame — a **frameshift** — and corrupts every codon downstream. This is why indels are usually far worse than substitutions.
5. **Numerical aberrations** change chromosome number — **aneuploidy**, of which **trisomy 21** is the named example.
6. **Structural aberrations** rearrange chromosome pieces: **translocation**, **duplication**, **inversion**, **deletion**.
7. Mutation reaches disease through the protein: **sickle cell anaemia** is one base substitution changing one amino acid, which changes how haemoglobin behaves, which changes the shape of the cell.
8. Mutations arise **spontaneously** or are induced by **mutagens**.

## 2.6 Molecular Techniques

1. **PCR** amplifies a chosen DNA sequence **in vitro** — no cell involved — by cycling **denaturation**, **annealing** of primers, and **extension** by a heat-stable polymerase.
2. PCR is powerful because it works from **tiny amounts** of starting material, and limited because it needs **known flanking sequence** for primers and will faithfully amplify **contamination** too. The syllabus asks for both halves.
3. **Gel electrophoresis** separates DNA fragments by **size**, because DNA is **negatively charged** and migrates toward the **anode**, with **smaller fragments travelling further**.
4. A gel tells you the **sizes present**, not the sequence.
5. **Southern blotting** transfers the separated fragments to a membrane so they can be probed.
6. **Nucleic acid hybridisation** uses a **labelled probe** that binds only to a **complementary sequence** — which is how you find one specific fragment among thousands.
7. Together the three techniques answer: *amplify it, sort it by size, then identify the one you care about.*

## 2.7 Bioethics

1. Genetic **screening** can be offered to prospective mothers to detect mutations before birth, **trisomy 21** being the named case.
2. The case **for** rests on **information and autonomy** — a family can prepare, or decide.
3. The case **against** rests on **risk, accuracy and consequence** — procedures carry risk, results carry uncertainty, and what the information gets used for is contested.
4. Wider concerns are **privacy** of genetic information, **discrimination** on the basis of it, and **consent** where the person tested cannot give it.
5. The exam asks you to **discuss** — both sides, then a judgement. A list of considerations with no position does not answer the question.
