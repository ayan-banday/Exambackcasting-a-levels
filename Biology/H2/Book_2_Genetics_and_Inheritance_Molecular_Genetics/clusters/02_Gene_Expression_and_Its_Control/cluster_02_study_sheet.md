# PART 1 — FROM GENE TO PROTEIN (2.2)

## A. The gene, the dogma, and which of the two strands gets read

**A1. The two definitions, verbatim.**

> **Gene expression:** "The process by which the information within a gene is used, first to
> synthesise RNA (transcription) and then to synthesise a polypeptide (translation) eventually to
> affect the phenotype of an organism."

> **Central Dogma of Molecular Biology:** "the flow of genetic information in cells proceeds from DNA
> to RNA (transcription) to polypeptide (translation). This **unidirectional** information flow from
> DNA to RNA to polypeptide / protein, is known as the Central Dogma."

Two words there are marked and routinely thrown away: **unidirectional**, and **polypeptide** rather
than protein. Schemes reject "protein" as the product of translation.

**A2. A eukaryotic transcription unit has three parts.**

| Part | What it is | Transcribed? |
|---|---|---|
| **Promoter** | contains the **TATA box** and the **transcription start site (+1)**. The TATA box is **typically located 25 bp upstream** of the start site and **binds TFIID**, which facilitates binding of RNA polymerase. **"The promoter determines which of the two strands of DNA is used as the template."** | **not transcribed except for the transcription start site** |
| **Coding region** | **"bounded by the transcription start site and termination site. Only 1 of the 2 strands serves as the template… read in the 3' to 5' direction to facilitate synthesis of RNA in the 5' to 3' direction."** | yes → **pre-mRNA / primary transcript** |
| **Termination sequence** | **"codes for a polyadenylation signal sequence (AAUAAA) in the pre-mRNA"** | **the whole termination sequence is transcribed** |

**A3. Template versus non-template, verbatim, because this is the single most-swapped pair in Book 2.**

> **Template strand:** "The DNA strand that is transcribed. The sequence on this DNA strand is
> **complementary** to that of the RNA. It serves as the template to direct the synthesis of the RNA
> molecule."

> **Non-template strand:** "The DNA strand that is not transcribed. The sequence on this DNA strand is
> **exactly the same as that of the RNA, except that the nitrogenous base thymine in DNA is replaced by
> uracil in RNA**."

The promoter picks the template. That single sentence is why a gene on one chromosome can be read
left-to-right and its neighbour read right-to-left.

**A4. The three RNAs the LO names, and the two it does not.** Verbatim from the 2.2 table:

- **mRNA** — **"Carries information, which codes for amino acid sequences, from DNA to ribosomes"**
- **tRNA** — **"Serves as an adaptor molecule in protein synthesis; Translates mRNA codon sequence into amino acid sequence"**
- **rRNA** — **"Plays catalytic and structural roles in ribosomes"**
- *snRNA* — **"Plays catalytic and structural roles in spliceosomes"**. Not in LO (a); it is needed to name what a spliceosome is made of.
- *siRNA and miRNA* — listed in the notes, **required by no learning outcome**. See §P.

## B. Transcription — the ordered chain

**Location:** nucleus. **Enzyme:** RNA polymerase (pol II for mRNA). **Substrate:** ribonucleoside
triphosphates. **Template read 3'→5'. RNA made 5'→3'.**

**B1. RNA polymerase, verbatim.** **"an enzyme comprising of several protein subunits… found in the
nucleoplasm"**, using **ribonucleoside triphosphate (NTP)** as substrate. It **"reads the DNA template
in the 3' → 5' direction, catalysing the assembly of ribonucleotides, which form complementary base
pairs with the template; and the formation of phosphodiester bond between the free 5'-phosphate group
of the incoming ribonucleotide and the free 3'-hydroxyl group of the growing RNA polynucleotide
chain."**

**B2. General / basal transcription factor, verbatim.** **"a protein that is required for an RNA
polymerase molecule to bind to its promoter and initiate transcription."** Two functions: **(i)
position RNA polymerase correctly at the promoter (MAIN)**, and **(ii) release RNA polymerase from the
promoter to begin elongating the RNA** once transcription has begun.

**B3. Initiation — three steps, each a mark.**

1. **Formation of the transcription initiation complex.** **General transcription factors** assemble
   along the promoter; **TFIID binds the TATA box**; the general transcription factors **mediate the
   binding of RNA polymerase to the promoter**, forming the **transcription initiation complex**.
2. **Unwinding and separation.** Binding of RNA polymerase **unwinds the helix and separates the two
   strands**; **hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs are disrupted**; a **transcription
   bubble** is created.
3. **First phosphodiester bond.** The exposed template directs **assembly of incoming ribonucleotides
   supplied as nucleoside triphosphates**, and RNA polymerase **catalyses formation of the first
   phosphodiester bond**. That bond marks the end of initiation.

**B4. Elongation — three steps.**

1. **Movement of the transcription bubble.** RNA polymerase **moves along the template DNA 3'→5'**
   and the helix **continues to transiently unwind**.
2. **Elongation of the polynucleotide.** Ribonucleotides **base-pair with the template**; as each NTP
   is brought in, **its two terminal phosphates are removed**, and the remaining **free 5'-phosphate
   is joined to the free 3'-hydroxyl of the growing chain by a phosphodiester bond**. mRNA is
   synthesised **5'→3'**.
3. **Re-annealing and proofreading.** RNA polymerase **re-anneals the unwound DNA behind it**,
   **dissociating the growing RNA chain from the template**, and **proofreads and removes any
   incorrectly inserted ribonucleotide**.

**B5. Termination — four steps.**

1. RNA polymerase **transcribes a termination sequence** in the DNA, triggering release of the RNA
   chain and dissociation of the polymerase.
2. The transcribed terminator **codes for the polyadenylation signal sequence AAUAAA**.
3. RNA polymerase continues to a point **about 10 to 35 nucleotides downstream** of that signal;
   **proteins bind at this point to cut and free the pre-mRNA**.
4. **That cleavage site is also the site of poly(A) tail addition.** Termination and polyadenylation
   are the same physical spot, which is why a change to the poly(A) signal shows up as a change in
   transcript length.

**B6. Why the transcript is continuous while the lagging strand is not.** Both polymerases build
**5'→3' only**. In replication the **template is double-stranded and antiparallel**, and the fork
**unwinds in the direction opposite to lagging-strand synthesis**, so the lagging strand must be built
in pieces. In transcription **only one strand is the template**, so nothing forces a break. Cluster 1
supplies the other half of this answer.

## C. RNA processing — the three modifications, all in the nucleus

**C1. 5' cap.** Added **"as soon as a length of the pre-mRNA has been transcribed"**. It is a
**methylated guanine nucleotide / methylguanosine triphosphate** (2.4 gives **7-methylguanosine
triphosphate**). Three functions, verbatim:

- **"protects the mRNA from degradation by hydrolytic enzymes such as nucleases"**
- **"defines the 5' end of the mRNA, which serves to recruit the small subunit of the ribosome for translation initiation"**
- **"distinguishes mRNAs from the other types of RNA molecules"**

**C2. Exons and introns, verbatim.** **"Exons, which are protein-coding sequences in the gene, and
Introns, which are long stretches of nucleotides inserted between exons that do not code for any
portion of the polypeptide… Both intron and exon sequences are transcribed into pre-mRNA."**

**C3. Splicing.** **"RNA splicing occurs after the release of pre-mRNA from RNA polymerase. During
this process, introns are removed while the remaining exons are spliced / joined together to form
mature mRNA. This process requires the hydrolysis of ATP."** It is **"carried out by spliceosome which
is a large complex comprising of several subunits known as small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs).
Each snRNP contains small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs) and a set of proteins."**

The mechanism itself is two cuts:

1. **Cleavage at the 5' splice site** and joining of the intron to a **branch point** within the
   intron, giving a **lariat-like intermediate**.
2. **Cleavage at the 3' splice site with simultaneous ligation of the exons**, excising the intron as
   a lariat.

**The DNA sequences at the 5' and 3' ends of an intron are the recognition sites for spliceosome
binding**, and the recognition is **complementary base pairing between snRNA and the splice site**.
That is why a mutation inside an intron, which codes for nothing, can still destroy a protein.

**C4. 3' poly(A) tail.** **"the pre-mRNA is cleaved by an endonuclease at a site 10–35 nucleotides
after the AAUAAA polyadenylation sequence"**, then **"approximately 200 adenine nucleotides"** are
added, **"catalysed by the enzyme poly (A) - polymerase"**. Two functions:

- **"protects the mRNA from degradation by nucleases… makes the mRNA a more stable template for translation in the cytoplasm"**
- **"required to facilitate export of mRNA out of the nucleus via nuclear pores"**

**C5. Alternative splicing.** **"Use of different splice sites… allows exons to be joined together in
different combinations. This produces different mature mRNAs from the same pre-mRNA that in turn,
generate different proteins."** Only introns come out; **the order of the exons never changes**, and
**the first exon cannot be spliced out**.

## D. The genetic code

**D1. Why three.** 20 amino acids, 4 bases. A singlet gives 4 codons, a doublet **4² = 16**, a
triplet **4³ = 64**. **"Of the 64 possible codons, 61 code for amino acids and include a start signal…
while 3 serve as termination signals."**

**D2. The four features, the notes' own mnemonic TUND.**

| Feature | Verbatim wording | The consequence the exam wants |
|---|---|---|
| **T**riplet | **"Each mRNA codon that specifies an amino acid… consists of three nucleotide bases"** | an indel of 3 does not shift the frame |
| **U**niversal | **"(almost) universal. The same code is used by almost all organisms"** | mitochondrial exceptions exist and are fair novel-data material |
| **N**on-overlapping | **"read as a triplet in the 5' to 3' direction… continuously, as successive groups of 3 nucleotides, one codon at a time without skipping any nucleotides"**; **"there can be 3 possible reading frames for every mRNA sequence"** | a frameshift corrupts every codon downstream |
| **D**egenerate but unambiguous | **"A single amino acid can be coded by more than one different codon… Only 2 amino acids – methionine (AUG) and tryptophan (UGG) are coded for by a single codon each. However, every codon codes for just one amino acid"**; **"Most of the amino acids are encoded by degenerate codons that differ in the 3rd position"** | silent mutations exist |

**D3. Wobble.** **"The violation of the usual rules of base-pairing at the 3rd nucleotide of a codon
is called wobble."** One tRNA can therefore read more than one codon. *(Not named in any LO — hold it
as an explanation, not as a recall item.)*

**D4. Punctuation.** **AUG** defines **"(i) the first amino acid of the polypeptide chain, and (ii)
the reading frame used from that point on"**. **UAA, UAG and UGA** are stop signals: **"They do not
code for any amino acid. There is no tRNA with an anticodon complementary to these 3 codons."** That
last sentence is the mark, not "translation stops".

## E. The machinery of translation

> **Translation:** "The process in which a polypeptide chain is synthesised by ribosomes using genetic
> information encoded in a mature mRNA template."

**Direction, verbatim:** the ribosome **"travels along the template mRNA in the 5' to 3' direction"**
and **"A polypeptide is assembled by the sequential addition of amino acids beginning from the
N-terminal to the C-terminal."** Two directions, two marks.

**E1. Mature mRNA.** **5' cap → 5' UTR (leader sequence, precedes the start codon) → protein-coding
region (AUG to a stop codon) → 3' UTR (trailer sequence, follows the stop codon) → poly(A) tail.**
Only the coding region is translated; both UTRs matter later as control sites (§L).

**E2. tRNA.** **~80 nucleotides.** Secondary structure is a **2D cloverleaf held by complementary base
pairing within the single-stranded molecule**, with **3 loops**; the anticodon loop carries **3
unpaired bases**. Tertiary structure is a **compact 3D L-shape maintained by hydrogen bonds**. The
**3' end (CCA stem) is the attachment site for a specific amino acid**. Its adaptor ability rests on
exactly two things, verbatim: **"The anticodon being able to determine the specific amino acid attached
to the CCA stem"** and **"The anticodon being able to form complementary base pairs with the mRNA
codon."**

**E3. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase.** **20 enzymes, one per amino acid.** Each **"covalently attaches a
specific amino acid to the 3' CCA stem of its appropriate set of tRNA molecules via an ester linkage,
forming aminoacyl-tRNA. This reaction requires the hydrolysis of ATP."** Specificity is a Book 1
argument: **"The active site of each aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase must be complementary to 3D conformation
of the specific amino acid and specific anticodon sequence of the tRNA in order for them to bind."**
This enzyme is the only proofreader of the codon-to-amino-acid rule; the ribosome checks the
codon–anticodon pair and nothing else.

**E4. rRNA and the ribosome.** rRNA **"forms the core of the ribosome – it is the main constituent of
the A and P sites and of the interface between the large and small ribosomal subunits"**, and **"rRNA
in the large ribosomal subunit has peptidyl transferase activity. It catalyses the formation of peptide
bonds between amino acids."** The ribosome is a **large ribonucleoprotein complex of ribosomal proteins
and rRNA**; **bacterial ~70S, eukaryotic ~80S**. The **small subunit (40S) contains the mRNA binding
site**; the large subunit carries three sites:

- **A site (aminoacyl-tRNA site)** — **"Holds the incoming tRNA carrying the next amino acid to be added"**
- **P site (peptidyl-tRNA site)** — **"Holds the tRNA carrying the growing polypeptide chain"**
- **E site (exit site)** — **"Site of release of the deacylated tRNA"**

**E5. Translation factors.** **Initiation factors** for **"assembly of mRNA, the first tRNA and
ribosomal subunits"**; **elongation factors** for **"synthesis of polypeptide chains"**; **release
factors** for **"recognition of the stop codon and disassembly of the translation machinery"**.
**Several use GTP as an energy source.** ATP is used once, at amino acid activation; everything inside
the ribosome runs on GTP.

## F. Translation — the ordered chain

**F0. Amino acid activation (prerequisite).** The synthetase recognises **both** the amino acid and the
anticodon, attaches the amino acid to the **3' CCA stem** by an **ester linkage**, and the resulting
**aminoacyl-tRNA** is released to deliver its amino acid to the ribosome. **ATP hydrolysis.**

**F1. Initiation.**

- **1A** — **eIFs bind the small subunit** and position the initiator **tRNAi^Met** at its **P site**. **GTP required.**
- **1B** — the **small subunit binds the mRNA by recognition of its 5' cap**, then **moves downstream 5'→3' in search of the start codon AUG**.
- **1C** — the **initiator anticodon base-pairs with AUG**; **eIFs dissociate with hydrolysis of GTP**; the **large subunit binds**, completing the **80S translation initiation complex**. The **initiator tRNA sits in the P site**, methionine forms the **N-terminus**, and **the A site is vacant**.

**F2. Elongation and translocation.**

- **2A — codon recognition.** An aminoacyl-tRNA carrying amino acid 2 **binds the A site by complementary base pairing between anticodon and codon**, **held in place by hydrogen bonds**, brought by **elongation factors**, with **hydrolysis of GTP**.
- **2B — peptide bond formation.** **Peptidyl transferase in the large subunit** catalyses a **peptide bond between the carboxyl end of methionine and the amino group of the 2nd amino acid**. The **ester bond between methionine and its tRNA is broken**. A **deacylated tRNA** is now in the P site and a **new peptidyl-tRNA** in the A site.
- **2C — translocation.** The ribosome moves **one codon (3 nucleotides) 5'→3'**, guided by **elongation factors** with **hydrolysis of GTP**: deacylated tRNA **P → E** and out, peptidyl-tRNA **A → P**, next codon exposed at **A**.
- 2A–2C repeat **until a stop codon reaches the A site**.

**F3. Termination.**

1. A **stop codon (UAG, UAA, UGA) reaches the A site**.
2. A **release factor binds directly to the stop codon in the A site**.
3. It **causes the addition of a water molecule instead of an amino acid**, freeing the **carboxyl end**
   of the polypeptide from the P-site tRNA **by hydrolysis**.
4. The polypeptide leaves **through the exit tunnel of the large subunit**.
5. The ribosome **releases the mRNA and separates into subunits**; tRNAs are recycled.

**F4. Polysomes.** Clusters of ribosomes **"simultaneously translating polypeptides from the same mRNA
strand"**. Advantage, verbatim: **"many more polypeptide molecules can be made in a given time than
would be possible if each had to be completed before the synthesis of the next could start."**

---

## G. Post-translational modification

**The immediate product of translation is a polypeptide, not a protein.** It must **coil and fold into
its specific 3D conformation**, in the cytoplasm or in the **lumen of the rough ER**, and transport to
the **Golgi apparatus** allows modification. Four routes:

1. **Attaching biochemical functional groups** — **glycosylation** (an **oligosaccharide**, common in
   membrane proteins, giving **glycoproteins important in signal recognition and immune activation**);
   **reversible phosphorylation** of **threonine, serine or tyrosine** residues by **kinases** (add)
   and **phosphatases** (remove).
2. **Making structural changes** — **formation of disulfide linkages**.
3. **Removing a sequence of amino acids or cutting the chain in the middle — proteolytic cleavage.**
   Insulin: a **connecting peptide is removed from the middle after the disulfide bonds have formed**,
   leaving **two chains held together by disulfide bonds**.
4. **Attaching ubiquitin.** Verbatim: **"Ubiquitin marks proteins for proteolysis by the proteasome. At
   least four ubiquitins are required on the substrate before a proteasome can bind to it. The
   polyubiquitin chain on a target protein is recognised by a specific receptor in the proteasome. In
   this manner, the selective degradation of proteins allows for the control of the length of time in
   which a protein can function."**

Route 4 is the hinge into §M. It is written here as chemistry and there as control.

# PART 2 — CONTROL OF EUKARYOTIC GENE EXPRESSION (2.4)

## H. What is being controlled, and why eukaryotes need more layers

**H1. Differential gene expression, verbatim.** **"the genes contained in the nuclei of all somatic
cells are exactly the same, but the cell types differ morphologically and functionally. The differences
between cell types are due to differential gene expression, i.e. the expression of different sets of
genes by cells with the same genome."** And: **"only a fraction of the genes in a eukaryotic cell are
expressed at any one place (tissue specificity) and at any one time (temporal specificity)."**

Spatial and temporal are the two words the LO uses. Both need naming.

**H2. Three structural reasons eukaryotic control is not prokaryotic control.**

1. **DNA is organised into nucleosomes**, so a gene must be in an "active" structure before anything
   can reach it. Cluster 1's packing hierarchy is the reason level 1 exists at all.
2. **Eukaryotic genes are not organised into operons**, so **each gene needs its own promoters,
   silencers and enhancers**.
3. **Transcription and translation are separated by the nuclear envelope**, so pre-mRNA **must be
   processed and exported before translation** — which creates levels 3 and 4 as places to intervene.

**H3. The five levels, named as the syllabus names them.** LO 2(i) reads: *"explain how differential
(i.e. spatial and temporal) gene expression in eukaryotes can be regulated at different levels:
i. chromatin level (histone modification and DNA methylation) ii. transcriptional level (control
elements, such as promoters, silencers and enhancers, and proteins, such as transcription factors,
including activators and repressors) iii. post-transcriptional level (processing of pre-mRNA in terms
of splicing, polyadenylation and 5' capping) iv. translational level (half-life of RNA and initiation
of translation) v. post-translational level (biochemical modification and protein degradation)."*

Everything in §§I–M is one of those brackets. Nothing outside them is required.

---

## I. Level 1 — Chromatin

**I1. The state the level works on.** **"euchromatin, which is diffused; or heterochromatin, which is
highly condensed. Genes found in euchromatin are available for transcription, whereas those in
heterochromatin are transcriptionally inactive."** And the mechanism sentence: **"Condensation prevents
transcription factors and RNA polymerases from gaining access to the promoter of a specific gene, thus
inactivating transcription of that gene."**

**I2. Histone modification — the four-step charge chain.** Start from Cluster 1: the nucleosome is DNA
wound round a **histone octamer**, and **"eight histone N-termini protrude outwards - these protrusions
are known as histone tails."**

> **"Histone tails are rich in lysine residues, which are positively-charged. They interact strongly
> with the negatively-charged phosphate groups of the DNA backbone and increase the affinity of DNA for
> the nucleosome surface."**

**Acetylation up-regulates:**

**Histone acetyltransferases (HATs) acetylate the lysine residues → the positive charges are
neutralised → the affinity of the histone complex for DNA is reduced → the chromatin becomes more
diffused / less compact → control regions of genes are exposed to transcription factors and RNA
polymerase → transcription rate increases, hence quantity of RNA increases.**

**Deacetylation down-regulates:**

**Histone deacetylases (HDACs) deacetylate → lysine residues regain their positive charges → affinity
of the histone complex for DNA increases → chromatin becomes more compact → access of transcription
factors and RNA polymerase to control regions is prevented → transcription rate decreases.**

Charge → affinity → compaction → access → rate. Five links, and a scheme pays for each.

**I3. DNA methylation.** **"DNA can be covalently modified by the addition of methyl groups to specific
nucleotides after DNA replication. DNA methylation in vertebrate DNA is restricted to cytosine (C)
nucleotides in the sequence 5'–CG–3' (known as CpG dinucleotides). This process is catalysed by DNA
methyltransferases."** **"CpG dinucleotides are clustered to form CpG islands, which are usually found
in the promoter regions of many genes."**

Two mechanisms, and the LO expects both:

1. **"Methylation changes the 3D conformation of DNA and thus, prevents binding of transcription
   factors to the promoter. Transcription initiation is prevented."**
2. **"Methylated DNA serves as recognition signals for methyl-CpG-binding proteins (MeCPs) that, in
   turn, recruit other proteins such as histone deacetylases (HDACs). HDACs modify chromatin in the
   region of the CpG island such that it becomes more condensed."**

Outcome differs from acetylation in kind, not degree: **transcription is prevented, hence no RNA is
formed.** Acetylation moves a dial; methylation closes a switch, and it is the mechanism used for
**long-term inactivation during differentiation**.

**I4. What this level cannot do.** Methylation adds no base and removes none, so **the nucleotide
sequence, the codons and therefore the type of protein encoded are unchanged**. It changes how much,
never which.

## J. Level 2 — Transcriptional

The notes label initiation of transcription **the most important** level.

**J1. Control elements — DNA, verbatim.** **"non-coding DNA sequences that regulate transcription by
binding transcription factors."** Three classes by position:

| Class | Position | What binds and what it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Promoter** | **TATA box ~25–30 bp upstream** of the start site | **determines the start-point**; **general transcription factors and RNA polymerase assemble here to form a transcription initiation complex** |
| **Proximal control elements** | **100–200 bp upstream** | **also bind general transcription factors**; **essential for efficient transcription** |
| **Distal control elements — enhancers and silencers** | **hundreds or even thousands of bp upstream or downstream**, and **may be located within an intron** | **function in an orientation-independent fashion**; **enhancers greatly increase and silencers greatly decrease the transcription rate** |

Two consequences that the distal row alone earns marks for: an enhancer **does not have to be adjacent
to the promoter**, and it **does not have to sit outside the gene** — which is how a mutation inside a
non-coding intron changes how much protein a cell makes.

**J2. Transcription factors — protein, verbatim.** **"A transcription factor is a regulatory protein
that binds to DNA and affects transcription of genes."**

- **"Activators (proteins) bind to enhancers (DNA sequence), triggering a series of interaction that results in an increased rate of transcription."**
- **"Repressors (proteins) bind to silencers (DNA sequence), triggering a series of interaction that results in a decreased rate of transcription."**

They **"contain two binding domains… 1. a DNA binding domain… and 2. one or more protein binding
(a.k.a activation) domains"**. Protein binds DNA. Activator and repressor are proteins; enhancer and
silencer are sequences. Papers set this pair as a trap in almost every sitting.

**J3. How an enhancer thousands of base pairs away acts — the four-step chain.**

1. **Activators bind their respective enhancers.**
2. **General transcription factors bind the promoter and mediate the binding of RNA polymerase,
   forming a transcription initiation complex.** At this point the rate is **basal**.
3. A **DNA-bending protein causes looping of the DNA**, bringing activators bound to distant enhancers
   **close to the promoter**.
4. Activators interact with **mediator proteins**, **adaptor molecules** that facilitate interaction
   between the activator, the general transcription factors and RNA polymerase. This gives **improved
   recruitment**, a **stable transcription initiation complex**, and **proper positioning** of it.
   → **rate of transcription is increased.**

Repressors run the mirror image: bind **silencers**, **destabilise** the complex, rate falls.
**Basal versus increased is the discriminating point** — an enhancer does not switch transcription on,
it raises a rate that already exists.

**J4. Why two cells with the same genome make different proteins.** **"The particular combinations of
control elements and transcription factors are specific to each gene and result in different
transcription rates in different cell types (i.e. spatial specificity) or at different stages of
development (i.e. temporal specificity)."** **Albumin** is made only in liver cells and **crystallin**
only in lens cells because each enhancer's **unique combination of control elements** is matched only
by the **activators present in that cell type**. The gene is present in both cells. The activator is
not.

## K. Level 3 — Post-transcriptional

The same three processing events as §C, now read as control points. What changes is the outcome
column.

| Process | Regulatory outcome |
|---|---|
| **5' capping** | **small subunit of the ribosome recruited for translation initiation, hence polypeptide formed**; **mRNA protected from degradation, hence quantity of RNA increased** |
| **Polyadenylation** | **export of mRNA out of the nucleus facilitated, hence polypeptide formed in the cytoplasm**; **mRNA protected from degradation, hence quantity of RNA increased** |
| **Alternative splicing** | **different mature mRNAs, hence different proteins** |

Alternative splicing is the only level in the whole list that changes **which** protein rather than
**how much**. It is also the answer to why gene number and protein number do not match, and therefore
the bridge back to Cluster 1's point that genome size and gene count do not track complexity.

---

## L. Level 4 — Translational

The LO names exactly two things here: **half-life of RNA** and **initiation of translation**. Nothing
else belongs at this level.

**L1. Half-life of mRNA, verbatim.** **"Stability of mRNA determines the duration for which
translation can occur. The less stable (i.e. the more rapidly an mRNA molecule is degraded), the less
time is available for it to be translated."** **"The half-life, or time required for 50% of the initial
amount of RNA to be degraded, varies among eukaryotic mRNAs."** Eukaryotic mRNAs are **more stable than
prokaryotic ones and have longer half-lives**.

Two things set it:

1. **"length of poly(A) tail, i.e. mRNAs with longer poly(A) tails tend to be more stable than mRNAs
   with shorter poly(A) tails."**
2. **"stabilising / destabilising sequences in the 3' UTR, i.e. these sequences contain binding sites
   for specific proteins that increase or decrease the rate of poly-A tail shortening."**

The decay route: **gradual shortening of the poly(A) tail by an exonuclease begins as soon as the mRNA
reaches the cytosol and acts as a timer.** Below a critical length (**about 25 nucleotides in humans**)
the paths split — **decapping followed by rapid 5'→3' degradation**, or **continued 3'→5' degradation
through the tail into the coding sequence**. Some mRNAs are instead **cut internally by an endonuclease
recognising 3' UTR sequences**.

Longer tail → longer half-life → more rounds of translation from one transcript → more polypeptide.
A cell can therefore raise output without transcribing the gene once more.

**L2. Initiation of translation.** **eIFs** are **"involved in scanning the mRNA for the start codon
AUG, locating the binding site of initiator tRNA to the AUG codon and forming the translation initiation
complex at the 5' mRNA region."** Varying their abundance and activity changes the rate — but,
verbatim, **"this has a global effect on overall translational activity, rather than on the rate of
translation of specific mRNAs."**

**Translational repressors** are **"molecules that bind to various regions of the mRNA molecule, usually
the 5' or 3' UTRs, and interfere with the initiation of translation by blocking the attachment of
ribosomes or other translation initiation factors."** These are **gene-specific**.

**Global versus specific is the discriminator.** An eIF change turns the whole cell up or down; a
translational repressor on one UTR touches one message.

## M. Level 5 — Post-translational

The LO names exactly two things: **biochemical modification** and **protein degradation**.

**M1. Biochemical modification / protein activation.** **"some polypeptides need alteration before
becoming functional, so controlling the timing of such modification controls the rate of functional
protein formation."** The chemistry is §G: **removal of amino acids or cleavage of the polypeptide**,
**structural changes such as disulfide bond formation**, and **addition of biochemical functional
groups**. Outcome: **different proteins, e.g. activation of proteins.**

This is the only level that can act on a protein already sitting in the cytoplasm, so it is the only
one that can respond **in seconds**. Everything upstream has to wait for transcription, processing,
export and translation.

**M2. Protein degradation.** **"many proteins have limited lifespans"**; **proteasomes break them
down**, letting a cell **"adjust the kinds and amounts of its proteins in response to environmental
change"** and **"maintain its proteins in working order"**. **Ubiquitin marks target proteins for
proteolysis by the proteasome**, with **at least four ubiquitins** needed before the proteasome binds.
Outcome: **decreased quantity and type of proteins.**

Degradation is not tidying. It is how a cell lowers a protein level without touching the gene, and it
is what makes the response to the previous stimulus stop before the next one arrives.

## N. The whole outcome on one page

The tightest statement of LO 2(i) in the Book is the master overview table on **2.4 p.3**, extracted at
`figures/2_4_p03_master_control_table.png`. Its right-hand column is the part that is hard to
reconstruct and easy to lose marks on, because it forces every level to end in a stated **outcome**:

| Level | Process | Outcome the table gives |
|---|---|---|
| **Chromatin** | histone modification | **↑ or ↓ transcription rate, hence ↑ or ↓ quantity of RNA** |
| | DNA methylation | **transcription prevented, hence no RNA formed** |
| **Transcription** | initiation of transcription *(most important)* | **basal quantity of RNA**; **↑ or ↓ transcription rate, hence ↑ or ↓ quantity of RNA** |
| **Post-transcription** | 5' capping | **small subunit recruited, hence polypeptide formed**; **mRNA protected, hence ↑ quantity of RNA** |
| | polyadenylation | **export facilitated, hence polypeptide formed in cytoplasm**; **↑ quantity of RNA** |
| | alternative splicing | **different mature mRNAs, hence different proteins** |
| **Translation** | stability of mRNA | **↑ translation rate, hence ↑ quantity of polypeptide** |
| | initiation of translation | **↑ or ↓ translation rate, hence ↑ or ↓ quantity of polypeptide** |
| **Post-translation** | chemical modification | **different proteins, e.g. activation of proteins** |
| | selective degradation | **↓ quantity and type of proteins** |

Read the column downwards and the through-line falls out: **the earlier the level, the more economical
the control, and the slower it acts.** Silencing chromatin costs nothing thereafter but takes a whole
pipeline to reverse. Degrading a finished protein is instant and wastes everything already spent on it.
That trade is the answer to any question asking why five levels exist rather than one.
