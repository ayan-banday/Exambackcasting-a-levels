# PART 1 — GENE MUTATION (LO 2k)

## A. What a mutation is, and whether it is inherited

**A1. Mutation** — *2.5, p. 4*

> "a permanent change in DNA … **an alteration to an organism's characteristics that is inherited,
> due to a change in the genetic material of a cell**, and it can destroy an organism if it occurs in
> a vital position in the DNA sequence."

"Mutations can involve **(i) just a single or a few nucleotide pair(s)** or **(ii) large regions of a
chromosome**." That two-way split *is* the structure of LO 2(k): gene mutation, then chromosomal
aberration.

**A2. Germline versus somatic.** The distinction decides who is affected, and it is a one-mark answer
either way.

| | **Germline mutation** | **Somatic mutation** |
|---|---|---|
| Where | **in germline cells (germ cells and gametes)** | **in somatic cells** |
| Inherited? | **may be transmitted to the offspring and to successive generations** | **not inherited by the progeny**, not passed to the next generation |
| Named consequence | if adverse, the mutant condition is a **genetic disorder / hereditary disease** | affects only the individual, and only the clone of cells descended from the mutated one |

**A3. Gene / point mutation** — *2.5, p. 4*

> "Gene / point mutations involve **chemical changes that affect the DNA sequence of just one gene**.
> They involve **changes at specific sites in a gene, resulting in a change in one or a few bases** in
> the DNA sequence."

Two basic types, and the whole of §B and §C hangs off the split: **nucleotide substitution**, and
**nucleotide insertion or deletion**.

**Nucleotide substitution** — *2.5, p. 4* — "**The replacement of one nucleotide pair with another**."

---

## B. Substitution — four outcomes, and what separates them

A substitution changes **at most one codon**, so it can change **at most one amino acid**. Which of
the four you get depends on two questions asked in order: *did the amino acid change?* then *did the
function change?*

| Outcome | What happens on the mRNA | Amino acid sequence | 3D conformation | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Silent** | codon changed, but **the same amino acid is inserted because of the degeneracy of the genetic code** | **unchanged** | **no change** | **not altered** |
| **Neutral** | codon changed **and the amino acid translated changed** | **changed** | **no change in overall conformation** | **not altered** |
| **Missense** | codon changed so **a different amino acid is translated** | **changed** | **changed** | **altered** |
| **Nonsense** | **a codon for an amino acid is changed into a stop codon** | **shortened / truncated** | **changed** | **altered** |

**B1. Missense** — *2.5, pp. 4, 6*
> "a nucleotide substitution in a DNA sequence results in the translation of a different amino acid."
> "The **amino acid sequence of the polypeptide is changed**, resulting in a **change in the specific
> 3-dimensional conformation of the protein**. Hence, the **function of the protein is altered**."

**B2. Nonsense** — *2.5, pp. 4, 6*
> "a nucleotide substitution … results in a **codon for an amino acid being changed into a stop
> codon**, leading to the **premature termination of translation**."
> "The resulting polypeptide will be **shorter (i.e. truncated)** than the normal polypeptide encoded
> … Hence, the function of the protein is altered. **Nearly all nonsense mutations result in
> non-functional proteins.**"

**B3. Silent** — *2.5, pp. 4, 7*
> "a nucleotide substitution … changes the mRNA codon. However, **the same amino acid is inserted
> into the protein because of the degeneracy of the genetic code**."

**Why degeneracy makes silent mutations possible.** From Cluster 2: there are **64 codons for 20
amino acids**, so most amino acids have several codons. The redundancy sits **mostly at the third
base of the codon**, so a substitution at the third position very often gives a synonymous codon.
**Only methionine (AUG) and tryptophan (UGG) have a single codon each** — those two positions have no
silent option. This is the single most common under-claim in the topic: candidates insist every base
change alters the protein.

**B4. Neutral** — *2.5, pp. 4, 8*
> "a nucleotide substitution … **changes the mRNA codon and amino acid translated. However the
> resulting amino acid substitution produces no detectable change in the function of the protein**."

Two named routes to it: **"the substitution of the original amino acid with an amino acid of similar
physical and chemical properties"** OR **"the substitution of an amino acid residue that is
non-essential to that protein's structure and function."**

> **The silent/neutral discriminator is one question: did the amino acid change?** Silent — no.
> Neutral — yes, and it did not matter. Getting these the wrong way round is a free mark surrendered.

---

## C. Insertion and deletion — the reading frame decides everything

**C1. In multiples of three** — *2.5, p. 5*
> "Addition or deletion of deoxynucleotides **in multiples of 3** results in: • **missense mutation**,
> i.e. an mRNA codon was added or deleted and the resulting polypeptide has an amino acid added or
> deleted respectively. **OR** • **nonsense mutation**, i.e. a stop codon was added, leading to
> premature termination of translation."

The reading frame survives, so **only the local region changes**. Conformation *may* change, so
function *may* change — the protein is not automatically wrecked.

**C2. Not in multiples of three — frameshift** — *2.5, pp. 5, 8*
> "As the resulting mRNA is **read as a series of non-overlapping codons**, an insertion or deletion
> of nucleotides **not in multiples of threes will result in a frameshift mutation**."
> "**All the nucleotides downstream of the insertion / deletion site will be improperly grouped into
> codons, resulting in extensive missense.**"
> "The frameshift may also cause **a new, premature stop codon** to be generated (i.e. nonsense
> mutation) in the reading frame, or result in a **read-through of the normal stop codon, resulting in
> polypeptides of altered lengths. In any case, a frameshift usually results in a non-functional
> protein.**"

**C3. Why the frame shifts at all.** Cluster 2 gives the reason and it must be said out loud, not
assumed: the genetic code is **triplet and non-overlapping**, and the reading frame is **set by the
start codon AUG**. Insert or delete anything that is not a whole number of triplets and every codon
after the site is re-grouped from different bases. Nothing upstream changes.

**C4. The chain that earns the marks**, in order, for any indel:

**insertion / deletion of n bases → n not a multiple of 3 → all downstream nucleotides improperly grouped into codons → extensive missense (subsequent amino acid sequence changed) and / or a new premature stop → primary structure changed → R groups in different positions → different hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds → changed tertiary conformation → active site or binding site no longer complementary → non-functional protein.**

> **The two symmetrical traps.** Claiming a single substitution shifts the reading frame — it cannot,
> because no base is added or removed. And treating every indel as catastrophic — a **3-base deletion
> does not frameshift at all**.

# PART 2 — FROM MUTATION TO DISEASE (LO 2l)

## D. Sickle cell anaemia — the only disease the LO names

Learn this as **one unbroken chain**, because that is how it is marked. Every arrow is a mark.
*(2.5, pp. 13–14, verbatim throughout.)*

**1. The gene.** "Sickle-cell anaemia involves a mutation in the **β-globin gene**, which encodes
**one of the polypeptide subunits that make up haemoglobin (Hb)**."

**2. The base change.** "**Substitution of a thymine for an adenine at one position in the Hb gene
(template strand)**, which results in a **missense mutation**." *(Schemes also accept the DNA triplet
**CTT → CAT** or the mRNA codon **GAG → GUG**; quote one of them, and quote it exactly.)*

**3. The amino acid.** "**Sixth amino acid residue in polypeptide is changed from a glutamate
(hydrophilic) to a valine (hydrophobic).**"

**4. The protein.** "**Specific three-dimensional conformation and function of the Hb protein is
altered.**" The primary structure changed, so the **R groups form different interactions** — where
glutamate's charged R group made **ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds**, valine's non-polar R group makes
**hydrophobic interactions**.

**5. The exposed patch.** "This substitution creates a **hydrophobic spot on the outside of the Hb
protein that sticks to the hydrophobic region of an adjacent Hb protein's beta chain**."

**6. Polymerisation, and its trigger.** "The mutant Hb subunits tend to stick to one another **when
the oxygen concentration is low, particularly when the red blood cells are in capillaries and
veins**." "The **aggregated proteins form fibre-like structures within red blood cells**." It is
**reversible**: "At high oxygen concentration, haemoglobin resumes globular haemoglobin structure."

**7. The cell.** "The fibre-like structures cause the red blood cells to **lose their normal
morphology and become sickle-shaped**."

**8. The physiology, two separate effects.** "Sickled cells are **less able to move through
capillaries and can block blood flow, resulting in severe pain and cell death of the surrounding
tissue due to shortage in oxygen**." And: "The sickled red blood cells are also **fragile and easily
destroyed, further decreasing the oxygen carrying capacity of blood**."

**D1. Why this is the cleanest cross-book question in the syllabus.** Book 1 gives haemoglobin as the
worked **quaternary structure** example — four subunits, two α and two β, each with a haem group.
Book 2 changes **one amino acid in one of those subunits** and the whole chain follows. Note what does
*not* change: the **number and type of subunits is the same**, so the quaternary structure as an
assembly is intact. What changes is the **surface chemistry of the β subunit**, which is why HbS
molecules stick to *each other* rather than falling apart.

**D2. The heterozygote.** In malarial regions **HbAHbS heterozygotes have higher reproductive success
than either homozygote**: **HbSHbS individuals die of sickle cell anaemia**, **HbAHbA individuals die
of malaria**, because **the malarial parasite can only infect normal red blood cells and not
sickle-shaped ones**. The mutation is harmful at the level of the individual and advantageous at the
level of the population — say both, and say which level you are on.

> **The over-claim trap.** "The gene is faulty so the haemoglobin doesn't work" scores nothing. The
> marks are in Glu→Val, hydrophilic→hydrophobic, polymerisation at low oxygen, and cell shape.

# PART 3 — CHROMOSOMAL ABERRATION (LO 2k)

## E. The definition, and the split

**Chromosomal mutation / aberration** — *2.5, p. 18*
> "Chromosomal mutation/aberration can be defined as: **1. a change in the structure of a chromosome
> (involving several gene loci)** or **2. a change in the number of chromosomes**."

Both halves are needed for the definition mark. **Structural** is §F, **numerical** is §G.

---

## F. Structural aberration — four types, all named in the LO

**F1. Deletion** — *2.5, p. 18*
> "When a chromosome **breaks in one or more places, and a portion of it is lost** … The deletion can
> occur **near one end or from the interior** of the chromosome. These are called **terminal or
> intercalary** deletions."

Effects: "usually profound, in which the **genotype is altered due to the absence of certain gene
loci**. If a deletion affects **the same gene loci on both homologous chromosomes, the effect is
usually lethal**. If **only one of a homologous pair is affected**, the effect on the phenotype is
that **alleles on the non-deficient homologue will be expressed, even if recessive**."

> That last clause is the discriminating mark and almost nobody writes it. A deletion can **unmask a
> recessive allele** on the surviving homologue.

**F2. Duplication** — *2.5, p. 19*
> "When **any part of the genetic material, be it a single locus or a large piece of chromosome, is
> present more than once in the genome**."

Arises from **unequal crossing over between synapsed chromosomes during meiosis** (which produces a
duplication *and* a deletion in one event) or **a replication error prior to meiosis**. Effects:
**gene redundancy**, **phenotypic variation**, and **an important source of genetic variation during
evolution**.

**F3. Inversion** — *2.5, p. 19*
> "a **segment of a chromosome is turned around 180º within a chromosome**. An inversion **does not
> involve a loss of genetic information, but simply rearranges the linear sequence**. It requires
> **two breaks** along the length of the chromosome and subsequent **reinsertion of the inverted
> segment**."

May cause **position effects** altering gene expression, and heterozygotes may produce **aberrant
gametes**.

**F4. Translocation** — *2.5, p. 20*
> "the **movement of a chromosomal segment to a new location in the genome**. **Reciprocal
> translocation involves the exchange of segments between two non-homologous chromosomes**."
> "genetic information is **not lost or gained**, but there is **only a rearrangement of genetic
> material**. Hence, the presence of a translocation **does not directly alter the viability of
> individuals bearing it**."

**"Non-homologous" is the load-bearing word.** Exchange between homologues is crossing over, which is
normal. Like an inversion, a translocation **may also produce a position effect as it may realign
certain genes in relation to other genes**.

**F5. How inversion and translocation still cause harm** even though nothing is lost. Three routes,
and a *suggest* question wants them: **position effect** (a gene moved next to different regulatory
sequences is transcribed differently — Cluster 2's enhancers and silencers act at a distance and
orientation-independently, so relocation matters); **a break falling inside a gene**, destroying it;
and **unbalanced gametes**, because a rearranged chromosome cannot pair normally, so some gametes
receive a duplication and others a deletion.

## G. Numerical aberration — non-disjunction, aneuploidy, trisomy 21

**G1. Non-disjunction** — *2.5, p. 21*
> "the **failure of chromosomes or chromatids to disjoin and move to opposite poles during
> division**. When this occurs in meiosis, the **normal distribution of chromosomes into gametes is
> disrupted**."

Two versions, and the diagram question turns on telling them apart:

| | **Non-disjunction in meiosis I** | **Non-disjunction in meiosis II** |
|---|---|---|
| What fails to separate | **homologous chromosomes** | **sister chromatids** |
| Gametes produced | **n+1, n+1, n−1, n−1** — all four abnormal | **n+1, n−1, n, n** — two abnormal, two normal |
| Contents of the n+1 gamete | **both homologues**, so **both parental alleles** at that locus | **two copies of one chromatid**, so the **same allele twice** |

> That last row is how a paper makes you deduce which division failed, from a child's genotype alone.

**G2. Aneuploidy** — *2.5, p. 21*
> "the general condition in which an organism **loses or gains one or more chromosomes, but not a
> complete set**."

**Monosomy** — "**the loss of a single chromosome from an otherwise diploid genome**" (2n−1).
**Trisomy** — "**the gain of one chromosome**" (2n+1).

**Euploidy** is the contrasting term — "instances where **multiples of the haploid chromosome set**
are found" — and **polyploidy** is more than two such multiples (3n, 4n). Keep the one-line
distinction; the LO names **aneuploidy**, not polyploidy, so do not spend time on autopolyploidy,
allopolyploidy or the crop examples.

> **Naming trap, straight from an examiner's comment.** The consequence of non-disjunction is
> **aneuploidy**. "Down syndrome" is refused unless the stem fixes *which* aneuploidy, and
> "polyploidy" is refused outright, because non-disjunction gives an **unequal distribution, not a
> whole extra set**.

**G3. Down syndrome (trisomy 21)** — *2.5, pp. 21, 27* — named twice in the syllabus, in LO 2(k) and
again in LO 2(m).

- "**the most common chromosomal abnormality syndrome in humans**."
- "**the only human autosomal trisomy in which a significant number of individuals survive longer than
  a year past birth**." (Monosomy for any autosome is usually not tolerated; **45,X Turner syndrome**
  is the only monosomy tolerated in humans.)
- "Down Syndrome occurs when there is an **additional chromosome for the 21st pair of chromosomes**."
- "**Non-disjunction is found in 95% of Down syndrome, the other 5% are caused by Translocation,
  Mosaicism or partial trisomy.**"

The **95 / 5 split** is the factual anchor, and it is also the bridge between §F and §G: a
translocation that attaches most of chromosome 21 onto another chromosome delivers a third copy of
those loci **without a third free chromosome 21 in the count**.

# PART 4 — THE THREE MOLECULAR TECHNIQUES (LO 2j)

> LO 2(j) lists **exactly three** techniques: **(i) polymerase chain reaction (including its
> advantages and limitations), (ii) gel electrophoresis, (iii) Southern blotting and nucleic acid
> hybridisation.** Nothing else in chapter 2.6 is examinable. Read §K before you read the chapter.

## H. Polymerase chain reaction

**H1. What it is** — *2.6, pp. 3–4*
> "a technique that can **amplify a specific region of DNA from a trace sample**. This amplification
> is carried out **in vitro**, i.e. **heat is used to separate the 2 DNA strands**. For the
> amplification to occur, **at least part of the sequence of the DNA sample must be known**."
> "a method of **cell-free cloning** of a particular DNA region, which is performed directly with a
> **thermostable DNA polymerase**."

**H2. The five components, each with its job.** A near-guaranteed question, and the mark is for the
job, not the name.

| Component | What it is | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| **DNA template** | double-stranded sample containing the sequence of interest, from genomic DNA or **cDNA reverse-transcribed from RNA** | supplies the strands to be copied |
| **PCR primers** | "**Two sets of short, single-stranded DNA primers, i.e. oligonucleotides about 20 bases in length**", chemically synthesised | "**Primers must be specific to the sequence of interest. The primer sequences flank the sequence of interest and are complementary to the 3' end of both template strands.**" They give the **free 3ʹ-OH** the polymerase extends from |
| **Free dNTPs** | dATP, dTTP, dCTP, dGTP, in excess | raw material; the **pyrophosphate released supplies the energy** for bond formation |
| **Thermostable DNA polymerase** | "**Taq polymerase, from *Thermus aquaticus***" | "**stable at high temperatures (i.e. thermostable) and is not denatured by repeated heat treatments in the PCR cycle**", so it need not be replaced each cycle |
| **PCR reaction buffer** | contains **MgCl₂** | "**the Mg²⁺ ions function as the cofactors required for DNA polymerase activity**" |

**H3. The cycle — three steps, and the temperatures are marks** *(2.6, p. 5)*

1. **Denaturation of DNA template, 90–100 °C** (95 °C for 30 s) — "the **hydrogen bonds holding
   together the two strands of the DNA template are broken**. Thus, the DNA template is denatured,
   i.e. become **single-stranded DNA**."
2. **Annealing of primers, 50–65 °C** (54 °C for 1 min, **in the presence of a large excess of the
   two sets of DNA primers**) — "Cooling allows primers to **anneal specifically to complementary
   sequences at the 3' end of single-stranded DNA templates via hydrogen bonds**." The **optimum
   annealing temperature depends on primer length and base composition**.
3. **Extension of primers, 60–75 °C** (~72 °C for 2 min, **close to the optimum temperature of Taq**)
   — "The annealed primers prime DNA synthesis using the four deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates …
   catalysed by Taq polymerase. The region of DNA **downstream of each of the two primers is extended
   in the 5' to 3' direction**."

Run in a **programmable thermal cycler**, **repeated 20–30 times**.

**H4. Why it is a chain reaction, and why it is specific** — *2.6, p. 6*
> "The PCR is a chain reaction because **newly-synthesised DNA strands will serve as templates for DNA
> synthesis in subsequent cycles**. Within a few cycles, the predominant DNA species is **identical to
> the sequence of interest, i.e. the sequence flanked by and including the two primers**."
> "The PCR is specific. **Only the sequence of interest is amplified because primers are specific and
> do not attach elsewhere.**"

Numbers: after **30 cycles**, **>99.99% are exact copies** and **2³⁰ ≈ 1.07 × 10⁹** molecules exist.
Amplification is **2ⁿ**, doubling each cycle.

**H5. Advantages and limitations — the bracketed half of the LO, and the half that gets skipped.**

**Advantages, three:**
- **Sensitivity** — "**can amplify sequences from trace amounts of DNA**", down to a single cell.
- **Speed and ease of automation** — "a single PCR cycle takes **less than 5 minutes**; **20–30
  cycles takes only 2–3 hours**", set up in one tube in a thermal cycler.
- **Robustness** — "permits amplification of specific sequences from material in which the **DNA is
  badly degraded or embedded in a medium** from which conventional DNA isolation is difficult"
  (archaeological remains, formalin-fixed pathology samples).

**Limitations, five:**
- **Risk of contamination** — "**due to the extreme sensitivity of PCR, any contamination of the
  reaction by non-template nucleic acids in the environment could be easily amplified**", giving a
  false result.
- **Infidelity of in-vitro replication** — "**Taq polymerase lacks 3' to 5' exonuclease activity**",
  so there is **no proofreading**, error rate ≈ **1 in 10 000 bases**, and an early error is then
  amplified in every later cycle.
- **Limited product size** — efficient only over roughly **0.1–5 kb**.
- **Prior sequence information is required** to design the primers.
- **Applies only to nucleic acids, not proteins.**

> **The sensitivity point is the same fact twice.** It is advantage 1 and limitation 1. Say it as a
> trade-off and you have written both halves of the LO in one sentence.

> **The PCR-needs-a-cell trap.** PCR is **in vitro** and **cell-free**. Describing transformation of
> a host cell answers a different, deleted syllabus.

## I. Gel electrophoresis

**I1. The principle** — *2.6, p. 8*
> "Electrophoresis **separates charged molecules** … based on their **different rates of movement /
> migration in an electric field**."
> "As the **phosphate groups of the sugar-phosphate backbone in DNA are negatively-charged, DNA moves
> towards the positive electrode (anode)**."
> "DNA molecules essentially have a **constant charge density i.e. charge per unit of mass**, thus
> they separate in porous agarose and polyacrylamide gels **primarily on the basis of size**."
> "The complex network of pores in the gel matrix act as a **'molecular sieve'** to impede the
> movement of DNA molecules and separate them by size / length."
> "**Shorter DNA fragments are less impeded by the pores than longer ones and move through the gel
> more quickly**, so that DNA fragments of different lengths **migrate as distinct bands**."

Two conditions that get dropped: **"DNA molecules to be separated by electrophoresis should be
linear"**, and the gel choice — **agarose** is more porous and separates **0.5–20 kb**, while
**polyacrylamide** has smaller pores and can resolve fragments **differing in length by just a single
nucleotide**.

> **Constant charge density is the whole answer to "why does it separate by size and not by charge?"**
> Every fragment carries the same charge per unit mass, so charge cancels out of the comparison and
> only the sieving is left. Miss that phrase and the answer is incomplete.

**I2. The run, five steps** *(2.6, pp. 10–11)*

1. **Prepare the gel** — agarose in buffer, heated to dissolve, poured into a tray with a **comb**,
   cooled to solidify.
2. **Set up** — tray into a chamber with **enough buffer to cover the gel**, comb removed.
3. **Load** — samples mixed with **loading dye** and loaded into wells **near the negatively-charged
   cathode**; **one well is reserved for a molecular weight marker / DNA ladder**.
4. **Apply a direct current** — the **anode is at the opposite end from the samples**, so DNA is
   forced from cathode to anode. Run until the dye front has moved about **two-thirds of the gel**.
5. **Stain and visualise** — **methylene blue**, or **ethidium bromide**, which **intercalates DNA
   and fluoresces under UV**. Radioactively labelled DNA is instead viewed by **autoradiography**.

**Loading dye, three jobs** — *2.6, p. 10* — "**bromophenol blue + xylene cyanol + glycerol**":
makes the sample visible for loading; **bromophenol blue migrates slightly faster than DNA and xylene
cyanol slightly slower**, which brackets the DNA and lets migration be monitored; and **glycerol
increases the density of the sample so it sinks into the well**.

**DNA ladder** — "contains **DNA fragments of known length**. This **allows for size determination of
the bands**." Without it a gel gives relative positions only, not sizes.

**I3. What a gel can and cannot tell you.** It gives **the sizes present and the number of distinct
fragments**. It does **not** give the sequence. So a mutation that **does not change the length of
the amplified product** — a **substitution**, or a single-base indel in a long product — leaves the
band in the same place and is **invisible on a gel**. A caution the notes print alongside: gels run
under different conditions are **not directly comparable**.

> **The migration-reversed trap.** DNA is negative, so it runs **towards the anode**, and **small
> fragments end up furthest from the well**. Reversing either half loses the mark you understood.

## J. Nucleic acid hybridisation and Southern blotting

**J1. Hybridisation** — *2.6, p. 14*
> "the process by which **two complementary, single-stranded nucleic acid chains base-pair and reform
> a double-stranded hybrid**."

**Denaturation / melting:** conditions that disrupt the hydrogen bonds — "**heating to ~100 °C, high
pH of ≳13 or very low salt concentrations**."
**Renaturation / hybridisation:** "kept for a prolonged period at a **lower temperature of 65 °C** to
permit hydrogen bonds between complementary base-pairs to re-establish."
It can occur **DNA/DNA, RNA/RNA or DNA/RNA**, "provided they have complementary nucleotide sequences
… **the greater the complementarity between the two single-stranded molecules, the more favourable
the hybridisation reaction**."

**J2. Probes** — *2.6, p. 14* — "**single-stranded DNA or RNA ranging from 15 to several thousands of
nucleotides long**", which "**are labelled i.e. carry radioactive, fluorescent or chemical markers to
facilitate detection**", and can be cloned from genomic or cDNA, PCR-generated, or chemically
synthesised.

Two properties: **sensitive** — "complementary sequences present at a concentration as low as **1
molecule per cell** can be detected"; **selective** — "a probe **hybridises only to nucleic acid
molecules carrying all or part of the complementary sequence**."

**J3. Stringency** — *2.6, p. 15*
> "Nucleic acid sequences **do not need to be perfectly complementary** to be able to hybridise. The
> **stringency of hybridisation can be controlled by varying the incubation temperature**."

- **Higher temperature (42 °C) = more stringent** — the temperature sits just below the melting point
  of a perfect helix, so **all imperfect helices are unstable** and only the **identical match**
  survives.
- **Lower temperature (35 °C) = less stringent** — **imperfectly paired double helices can form**, so
  the probe also finds **related but non-identical sequences**.

So the choice of temperature is a choice of question: *is this exact gene here?* or *is anything like
this gene here?*

**J4. Primer versus probe** — *2.6, p. 15*, a compare question the notes pre-answer verbatim
> "**Both are short, single-stranded oligonucleotides which bind to the DNA via complementary
> base-pairing.** Their main difference is that a **primer anneals to a specific sequence on the DNA
> template and provides a free 3'-OH group for DNA polymerase to add nucleotides** (i.e. DNA
> replication) while a **probe allows of the detection of target DNA from a sample**. For ease of
> detection, **probes are structurally modified (i.e. labelled)** to carry radioactive, fluorescent or
> chemical markers."

**J5. Southern blotting, four steps** *(2.6, pp. 16–17)*

1. **Agarose gel electrophoresis** — the DNA in the sample is **separated on the basis of size**.
2. **Blotting** — the gel sits on a **paper wick absorbing buffer from a reservoir**; a
   **nitrocellulose or nylon membrane** is laid over it, then a stack of blotting paper. **Capillary
   action draws buffer through the gel and membrane**, transferring the DNA as a **replica of the
   bands**. Crucially, "**the double-stranded DNA molecules must be denatured to their single-stranded
   form before hybridisation with the probe; this is done either prior to or during the blotting
   process by exposing the gel to alkaline denaturing conditions.**"
3. **Incubation with the labelled probe** — the membrane is sealed in a bag with buffered salt
   solution containing the **labelled single-stranded DNA or RNA probe**, which **hybridises with the
   DNA of interest**.
4. **Detection** — the membrane is **washed to remove unbound probe**, then **autoradiography** or
   chemical / fluorescent detection. "**The size of the DNA that hybridise with the probe can be
   determined by reference to the molecular weight markers that were electrophoresed alongside.**"

**What the technique is for:** "Southern blotting **uses a DNA sample** and thus allows the
experimenter to **detect the presence of a specific gene or a specific DNA sequence**."

**J6. Why the blot shows fewer bands than the gel.** Only bands that **contain a sequence
complementary to the probe** hybridise, so only those are visualised. Every other fragment is still
on the membrane — it is simply **not labelled**, so it does not appear. This is the whole point of
the technique: it turns "here are all the fragments" into "here is the one I care about."

**J7. The three techniques as one workflow.** *Amplify it (PCR), sort it by size (gel), identify the
one you want (blot and probe).* Each answers a question the previous one cannot.

# PART 5 — BIOETHICS OF MATERNAL GENETIC SCREENING (LO 2m)

## L. What is being screened, and what a screening test actually is

**L1. Genetic screening** — *2.5, p. 24*
> "the **analysis of a person's DNA to check for presence of one or more alleles that are associated
> with disease**." "When **people who are free of the symptoms of a disease** are tested like this, it
> is known as genetic **screening**."

**Screening versus testing.** The laboratory procedure is the same. **Screening** is applied to people
**without symptoms**; **testing** confirms a diagnosis in someone **who has symptoms**. The
distinction is about who is being tested and why, not about the technique.

**L2. Maternal / prenatal genetic screening** — *2.5, pp. 25–26*
> "tests that **provide information about the health of a foetus** – an unborn child."
> "Maternal genetic screening tests **do not diagnose medical conditions in the foetus. These
> screening tests determine if the foetus is at an increased or decreased risk** of a particular
> abnormality."
> "**Diagnostic test is only performed when a screening test is abnormal.** Prenatal diagnostic tests
> are usually **more invasive tests that may pose a higher risk to the pregnant woman and the
> foetus**."

**That two-step structure carries most of the ethics.** A screen gives a **probability**, not an
answer; converting it into an answer requires an **invasive procedure that carries risk**.

**L3. High-risk pregnancies** — **age over 35**, **family or personal history of genetic conditions**,
**history of miscarriage or stillbirth**, **lifestyle factors** such as exposure to cigarette smoke or
radiation. Results are used **for counselling, to help parents make informed decisions about the
outcome of the pregnancy**.

**L4. The tests, in one line each** — enough to argue with, not to memorise.

| Screening tests (non-invasive, give risk) | Diagnostic tests (invasive, give an answer) |
|---|---|
| **Carrier screening** — blood from both biological parents, detects recessive alleles (cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease) | **Amniocentesis** — needle through the abdomen into the uterus for amniotic fluid |
| **Cell-free foetal DNA screening (NIPT)** — maternal blood after 10 weeks; Down syndrome, trisomy 18, trisomy 13 | **Chorionic villus sampling** — needle through the abdomen or vagina for placental cells |
| **Serum protein screening** — PAPP-A, free β-hCG, AFP, estriol, inhibin-A | Both feed **FISH, standard karyotyping or microarray** |

Screening for Down syndrome is **offered to all pregnant mothers, not only those with increased
maternal age** — which is itself an argument in the debate, because it detects trisomy-21 in mothers
who would never have been offered testing on age alone.

## M. The eight bioethical considerations — *2.5, p. 28, verbatim*

**This table is the learning outcome.** Every discuss mark comes from here. The right-hand column
names which of the four bioethics principles the issue draws on.

| Issue | The argument, verbatim | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy and informed consent** | "**Pre-test counselling is essential to ensure that pregnant women are fully informed about the purpose, process, and implications of genetic screening.** Counselling should also cover the possible outcomes and the subsequent steps to take." · "**Care must be taken to ensure that the pregnant woman has the right to accept or reject the screening test without coercion.**" | Respect for persons |
| **Clinical issues** | "**Genetic screening tests are not 100% accurate and there might be instances of false positives or false negatives.**" · "**False positives** might result in **psychological distress** … and might also result in **more invasive and riskier tests for a confirmatory diagnosis**." · "**False negatives might incorrectly give parents a sense of reassurance while, in reality, the foetus has a genetic condition.**" | Non-maleficence |
| **Degree of severity of disease** | "**The mental and physical impairments of Down syndrome can vary greatly. Screening tests cannot accurately predict the extent of the disease nor the specific challenges a child might face.**" | Respect for persons |
| **Termination of pregnancy** | "**abortion of a foetus with Down syndrome is a likely outcome** … it raises ethical issues about **the moral status of a foetus and the rights of parents**." · "A decrease in the Down syndrome population via termination of pregnancy might raise concerns about **eugenics, which is selective breeding for desired traits**." | Respect for persons |
| **Non-maleficence, "do no harm"** | "Such news might have a **negative psychological impact on the parents**." · "Parents might feel **particularly stressed and anxious should the condition of the foetus have no cure or effective treatment**." · "**Diagnostic tests such as amniocentesis and CVS carry high risks of harming the foetus and the pregnant woman.**" | Non-maleficence |
| **Beneficence, "promote and protect well-being"** | "**Parents are able to make an informed decision on the outcome of the pregnancy** and can potentially **prepare themselves for having a child with a specific medical condition**." · "parents who know that their child has Down syndrome can **prepare for early intervention and resources to help their child, potentially improving the outcome of the child**." | Beneficence |
| **Justice and equity** | "**Genetic screening might not be available to all pregnant women from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds.**" · "**Inequities in access could worsen existing health disparities** as parents from less fortunate backgrounds **might not be able to bear the financial burden of the medical condition of the child**." | Justice |
| **Social and ethical implications** | "**Disability advocates argue that screening for medical conditions like Down syndrome contributes to the devaluation of people with such lives.**" · "This can lead to the **marginalisation of individuals with such medical conditions** and the general negative societal attitude towards them." | Care |

**M1. The four principles** *(2.7, p. 3)* — learn the names, because a bioethics mark is earned by a
**specific mechanism attached to a named party**, never by a moral label like "playing God".

- **Respect for persons** — "**autonomy** emphasizes the **right to self-determination** and
  acknowledges a person's right to make choices … based on personal values and beliefs." Source of
  **informed consent, privacy, confidentiality and protection of vulnerable populations**.
- **Maximising benefits, minimising harms** — "**doing good (beneficence)**" which "**requires
  positive action**", and "**doing no harm (non-maleficence)**" which "**obligates others to avoid
  inflicting harm intentionally**."
- **Justice** — "**giving to each that which is his due**" (Aristotle), or fairness. "**Persons who
  are equals should qualify for equal treatment, and resources, risks, and costs should be
  distributed equitably.**"
- **Care** — "**the maintenance of healthy, caring relationships between individuals and within a
  community.** The principle of care **adds context to the traditional principles**."

## N. How to reach a judgement, because a list caps

**The command word is *discuss*, and *discuss* requires both sides plus a position.** A list of
considerations with no judgement caps, however long the list is.

**The scaffold** *(2.7, p. 2)*:
**Ethical Question → Facts → Stakeholders → Ethical Principles → Alternate Options and Rebuttals → Decision and Justification.**

Three things that separate a top answer from a complete one:

1. **Name the stakeholders separately** — the **pregnant woman**, her **partner**, the **foetus**, the
   **healthcare system**, and **people already living with the condition**. Most answers argue only
   for the parents, and the disability-advocacy point in the last row of §M belongs to a group that
   is not in the room.
2. **Attack the strongest version of the other side, not the weakest.** The serious argument for
   screening is not "information is good" — it is **beneficence through preparation**: early
   intervention measurably improves outcomes for the child. The serious argument against is not "it
   leads to abortion" — it is that a **probabilistic result forces a decision under uncertainty**, and
   the confirmatory test that removes that uncertainty **carries a risk to the pregnancy itself**.
3. **Land somewhere, and say what your position depends on.** A defensible landing: screening should
   be **offered** rather than **routine**, conditional on **pre-test counselling**, **explicit consent
   without coercion**, and **equitable access** — because the same test that serves autonomy when
   chosen freely undermines it when it becomes the default. Name the condition your judgement rests
   on and you have given the examiner a reason, not an opinion.

# P. Gaps and cautions — read before drilling

**P1. The bank has no maternal-screening bioethics question, anywhere.** Fifteen years of prelims
across every JC in the corpus contain **nothing** on amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling, NIPT,
informed consent or the trisomy-21 screening debate, and **trisomy-21 is never named in an ethics
question in the entire collection**. The nearest real question is preimplantation diagnosis of IVF
embryos, which is a **different scenario with a different decision at its centre** — discarding a
blastocyst, not terminating an established pregnancy, and no miscarriage risk from the sampling
procedure. LO 2(m) says *discuss* and is worth several marks. Build the answer from §M and §N
yourself; there is no past paper to lean on.

**P2. Inversion is untested in the entire bank.** Deletion, duplication and translocation all have
real questions. Inversion appears **only inside mark-scheme lists for other questions**. Prepare it
from §F3 and expect it to appear cold.

**P3. Two errors in the corpus you will meet.**
- One prelim stem opens "Sickle cell anaemia is caused by a somatic mutation as it affects the somatic
  cell." **That is wrong.** Sickle cell anaemia is inherited, so the mutation is **germline and present
  in every cell from the zygote onwards**. The question that follows it is sound; the premise is not.
- One school paper describes printed sequences as the **non-template** strand in the question and the
  **template** strand in the answer booklet. Check which strand you have been given before you
  transcribe anything.

**P4. Meiosis is Book 3, not Book 2.** Non-disjunction is taught here because LO 2(k) needs it, but
**mitosis, meiosis and their stages are LOs 2(n) and 2(s)**, which Book 3 teaches. If a question asks
about the stages themselves rather than the failure to disjoin, that is outside this cluster.

**P5. Cancer is Book 3 too.** p53, ras, tumour suppressor genes and proto-oncogenes appear in the
mark schemes of several mutation essays in this bank, because those essays were set to candidates who
had already done cancer. **LOs 2(p), 2(q) and 2(r) are Book 3.** Use sickle cell and Down syndrome as
your worked examples until then, and note where a scheme is asking for content you have not covered.

**P6. Where the mark-scheme lexicon is strictest in this cluster.** *Trisomy 21*, not "an extra
chromosome". *Taq polymerase*, not "a heat-stable polymerase". *Aneuploidy*, not "Down syndrome",
when naming the consequence of non-disjunction. *Non-homologous* in the translocation definition.
*Glutamate to valine*, with **hydrophilic** and **hydrophobic** attached to each. *Anode*, and
*constant charge density*.
