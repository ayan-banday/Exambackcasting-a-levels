### 2. Content node map

- **Introduction to cell signalling** (p.4–6)
  - Cell signalling as the process by which cells communicate, and why multicellularity demands it
  - Signal molecules and their chemical variety
  - Communication by direct contact versus by secreted molecules
  - The dependence of a cell on multiple extracellular signals: survival signals, division signals, differentiation signals, and death by apoptosis when survival signals are withdrawn
- **The three stages of cell signalling** (p.7–9)
  - Stage one, signal reception: ligand, receptor, ligand-receptor complex, conformational change
  - Stage two, signal transduction: relay molecules, phosphorylation cascade, second messengers, single-step versus multistep
  - Stage three, cellular response: cytoplasmic response versus nuclear response
- **Cell surface receptor signalling** (p.10–21)
  - Why hydrophilic signal molecules need surface receptors
  - The four types of cell surface receptor, two of which the chapter marks as out of syllabus
  - Protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation as a molecular switch
    - Protein kinase, protein phosphatase, the balance between them, availability for reuse
    - The phosphorylation cascade and its conformational-change basis
  - Second messengers: definition, why small and water soluble matters, the first-messenger relationship
  - G-protein linked receptors
    - Structure by level: primary, secondary (seven transmembrane helices), tertiary (barrel), the three domains
    - Structure-to-function table, five rows
    - Working principle: GDP to GTP exchange, dissociation, target protein, GTPase-driven shut-off
    - cAMP as a second messenger: adenylyl cyclase, protein kinase A, phosphodiesterase
    - Calcium ions and IP3 as second messengers: how low cytosolic calcium is maintained, PIP2 cleavage by phospholipase C, the IP3-gated ER channel, calmodulin
  - Receptor tyrosine kinases
    - Subunit structure, the three regions of each polypeptide
    - Working principle: dimerisation, autophosphorylation, relay protein docking on specific phosphotyrosines, multiple simultaneous pathways
- **Advantages and significance of cell signalling** (p.23–25)
  - Signal amplification: the three key features, the two reasons it works, the epinephrine and glycogen worked example
  - Regulation and signal termination: protein phosphatase, intrinsic GTPase, phosphodiesterase
  - Specificity: why two cells respond differently to the same signal
- **Homeostasis, marked BACKGROUND READING** (p.26–30)
  - Why homeostatic control is needed, internal environment, set point
  - The homeostatic control system: stimulus, receptor, integrating centre, effector, feedback
  - Feedback mechanisms: negative versus positive, oscillation about the set point
- **The endocrine system, marked BACKGROUND READING** (p.31–33)
  - Endocrine glands, ductless secretion, contrast with exocrine
  - Properties and functions of hormones
  - The pancreas: exocrine acinar tissue versus the islets of Langerhans
- **Regulation of blood glucose concentration** (p.34–41)
  - Negative feedback control with a 90 mg/100 ml set point, insulin and glucagon as antagonists
  - Fed state versus fasted state, laid out as a stimulus / receptor / integrating centre / effector / response / effects / outcome table
  - Mechanism of insulin, an RTK example: insulin response protein, GLUT4 vesicle migration, glycogen synthase activation
  - Mechanism of glucagon, a GPLR example: adenylyl cyclase, cAMP, protein kinase A, glycogen phosphorylase kinase, glycogen phosphorylase, and the parallel inhibition of glycogen synthase
- **Diabetes mellitus, marked Supplementary** (p.43–44)
  - Definition and characteristics
  - Type 1 versus Type 2

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 Glucagon is repeatedly said to act on skeletal muscle. It does not.** This is the most serious factual error in the chapter and it is the kind an examiner would penalise.

- Page 34, correct version: "Glucagon: Promotes secretion of glucose into the blood, by **hydrolysis of liver glycogen** between meals."
- Page 36, Effectors row, fasted state: "**Liver, skeletal muscle and adipose cells** are the main target cells"
- Page 37, fasted state, effect 2: "Stimulates glycogenolysis, which is the breakdown of glycogen to glucose, and inhibits glycogenesis **in liver and skeletal muscle cells**."
- Page 41: "A specific example of GPLR signalling is demonstrated by the glucagon receptor... leading to the cytoplasmic responses of glucose mobilisation **in liver or muscle cells**."

Skeletal muscle does not carry glucagon receptors, so glucagon cannot act on it. Separately, skeletal muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase, so even when muscle glycogen is broken down the glucose cannot leave the cell and enter the blood. Page 41's step 8, "Glucose eventually formed diffuses into the bloodstream", is therefore only true of liver. Page 34 states the correct restriction and pages 36, 37 and 41 contradict it. **Write liver, and only liver, for glucagon.** `[INFERRED]` on the correction, `[SCHOOL]` on both conflicting statements.

**5.2 The same muscle overreach appears twice more on the insulin side.**

- Page 37: "In liver and skeletal muscle cells, activated insulin receptors activate **glucokinase**, an enzyme which phosphorylates glucose into glucose-6-phosphate." Glucokinase is the liver and pancreatic beta-cell isoform. Skeletal muscle uses hexokinase.
- Page 36 lists liver among "the main insulin-dependent target cells" whose glucose uptake rises because GLUT4 vesicles fuse with the membrane. Hepatic glucose uptake runs through GLUT2, which is insulin-independent. The chapter's own Fig. 34 caption on page 38 gets this right, naming only "adipose tissue and resting skeletal muscle". So the page 36 text and the page 38 figure disagree.

The safe answer, and the one the school's own figure supports, is that GLUT4 recruitment happens in **muscle and adipose tissue**, and that the liver's insulin response is glycogenesis and reduced gluconeogenesis rather than GLUT4-mediated uptake.

**5.3 The chapter names adenylyl cyclase two different ways, and Cambridge uses a third.**

- Pages 17, 23 and 41: "**adenylyl** cyclase"
- Page 36: "**adenylate** cyclase is activated to produce cyclic AMP (cAMP) from ATP"
- 9477 Specimen Paper 1 Question 22 prints "**adenyl** cyclase" `[OFFICIAL]`

All three are in circulation and none is wrong, but Ethan should know that the official paper used the shortest form, and that a mark scheme is most likely to accept all three. This is worth knowing precisely because a candidate who has drilled only one form may not recognise the other two under time pressure.

**5.4 IP3 is called both trisphosphate and triphosphate, and only one is correct.**

- Page 13 and page 19: "inositol **tris**phosphate (IP3)"
- Page 3 outline and the page 18 section heading 2.4.1.2: "Inositol **Tri**phosphate (IP3)"

Trisphosphate is correct. It means three separate phosphate groups attached at three positions on the inositol ring, which is what IP3 is. Triphosphate means a chain of three phosphates joined end to end, which is what ATP has. Writing "inositol triphosphate" describes a different molecule.

**5.5 The chapter flags three receptor types as out of syllabus and then tests two of them.** Page 8: "intracellular receptors (located inside the cell) **(not in 9477 H2 syllabus, FYI)**". Page 10 marks ion channel receptors and integrin receptors the same way. Then Concept Check 1 on page 22 is a four-row table in which intracellular receptors are filled in as a worked row and ligand-gated ion channels are a fully worked row, and the exercise asks Ethan to complete the RTK and GPLR rows by comparing against them. The chapter is right about the scope and inconsistent about its own practice. `[OFFICIAL]` LO 3(m) is explicit: "knowledge of intracellular receptors is not required".

**5.6 Cross-reference error, page 36.** The GLUT4 bullet says "stimulate migration to and fusion of these vesicles with the plasma membrane (**Fig. 34, Pg 39**)". Fig. 34 is on page 38, not page 39. Page 39 carries Fig. 35.

**5.7 The claim that insulin signalling uses no second messenger is a teaching simplification, not a fact.** Page 40: "**No second messengers are involved** in signal transduction in insulin receptor signalling." Insulin signalling does generate a lipid second messenger, PIP3, downstream of PI3-kinase. `[INFERRED]` The school's simplification is defensible at A-level and is directly protected by LO 3(p), which says "Details of different second messengers and specific kinases activated in the pathway are not required". Reproduce the school's version if asked to contrast insulin with glucagon, because the contrast the exam wants is RTK versus GPLR, but do not treat "no second messenger exists in insulin signalling" as a general truth.

**5.8 Circular wording in the kinase and phosphatase section, page 11.** "PK phosphorylates and thus activates protein **kinases**" and "PP dephosphorylate and thus inactivate protein **kinases**." Read literally these say kinases only ever act on other kinases, which is false; a kinase acts on whatever substrate protein it is specific for, and the last kinase in a cascade phosphorylates a non-kinase effector. The chapter's own Fig. 9 on page 12 shows exactly that, with kinase 3 phosphorylating "a protein that brings about the cell's response". The page 11 wording is a description of a cascade written as if it were a definition.

**5.9 Phosphorylation is said to activate, then hedged twice, without resolution.** Page 10: "This conformational change is typically brought about by phosphorylation **which activates** the protein." Page 11: "**The reverse may also be true** with regards to the effects of the enzymes PK and PP." Page 12: "Note that phosphorylation **does not always** lead to the activation of a protein; in some cases, it can decrease a protein's activity." Page 20: a kinase transfers phosphate "leading to its **activation or inactivation**." The chapter's own glucagon figure on page 41 shows protein kinase A phosphorylating glycogen synthase to **inhibit** it. There is no contradiction once you read all four statements together, but a candidate who reads only page 10 will write the wrong general rule. The correct general rule is that phosphorylation changes conformation, and whether that activates or inhibits depends on the protein.

**5.10 Stray text fragment, page 9.** The second-messenger bullet ends "Second messengers through diffusion, rapidly relay the signal from the cell surface into the cell interior. **RESPONS**". A truncated word has been left in the text layer, probably from an overlapping text box. Not a content error, but do not memorise that bullet as printed.

**5.11 Minor typography and spelling.** Page 4: "receptor proteins on the surface or **in in** the interior". Page 21: "auto**phoshorylation**". Page 14: the caption for Fig. 11 is printed above the caption for Fig. 10 because of the two-column layout, which is a reading-order trap rather than an error. Page 10's heading is "2.1 Cell Surface Receptors" while the page 3 outline calls the same section "2.1 Cell Surface / Membrane Receptors".

Beyond these, the chapter is factually sound. The GPLR and RTK working-principle walkthroughs, the three signal-termination mechanisms, the amplification argument, the specificity argument and the calcium-homeostasis section are all internally consistent and correct.

### 6. Load-bearing versus skippable

**Load-bearing, and this is where every mark in the chapter sits:**

- The three stages of cell signalling on pages 8 to 9, in the syllabus's own three-part shape. LO 3(m) names the three stages and their sub-content, so a three-mark answer is written straight off the outcome: ligand-receptor interaction, signal transduction with phosphorylation cascade and amplification, cellular response as a change in gene expression. Note the outcome specifically says "cellular response (**change in gene expression**)", which means the nuclear response on page 9 is the one the syllabus foregrounds, not the cytoplasmic one.
- The GPLR working principle, page 16, step by step. GDP displaced by GTP, G protein activated, dissociation and diffusion along the membrane, binding to the effector, then the intrinsic GTPase shutting it off. The GTP-for-GDP **exchange** is the single most penalised detail in the corpus (see the trap model in the examiner guide).
- The RTK working principle, page 21, step by step. Ligand binding, aggregation and dimerisation, activation of tyrosine kinase activity, autophosphorylation of tyrosines on the tails, then relay proteins docking on specific phosphotyrosines. The reason RTKs trigger several pathways at once, which is that each phosphorylated tyrosine is recognised by a different relay protein, is a stock explain question.
- The GPLR structure-to-function table on page 15. Five rows, each pairing a structural feature to what it enables. This is the answer to any "relate the structure of a receptor to its function" question and it is already written in mark-scheme shape.
- Protein kinases and phosphatases on page 11, as a reversible molecular switch. LO 3(o) asks for their roles in signal amplification specifically, so the mark-bearing pairing is: kinases propagate and amplify by phosphorylating many substrate molecules each, phosphatases terminate and reset by removing those phosphates and freeing the kinase for reuse.
- Second messengers on page 13, with the properties argument. Small, non-protein, water-soluble, therefore able to diffuse rapidly through the cytosol, therefore able to spread a signal in three dimensions from one membrane event. LO 3(n) says "the **roles and nature**" of second messengers, so both halves are required, and cAMP is the named example.
- Signal amplification on pages 23 to 24, including the numbers. One receptor activates about 100 G proteins; each enzyme in the cascade turns over many substrate molecules; a few epinephrine molecules release hundreds of millions of glucose molecules. The two stated reasons amplification is possible, which are multiple catalytic steps and persistence of the active form, are the explain marks.
- Signal termination on page 25, all three mechanisms with the enzyme named: protein phosphatase, intrinsic GTPase, phosphodiesterase. This is the cheapest three-mark block in the chapter.
- Specificity on page 25. Two cells respond differently to the same signal because they differ in one or more of the receiving, transducing or responding proteins. That single sentence answers a recurring question.
- Section 6.1 and 6.2, pages 34 to 37, for LO 3(p). Insulin and glucagon as antagonists, alpha and beta cells, the 90 mg/100 ml set point, and the fed-versus-fasted effects. Be aware of error 5.1 and drop skeletal muscle from every glucagon line.
- Sections 6.3 and 6.4, pages 40 to 41, but **only at the level the outcome asks for**. LO 3(p) restricts the outline to "how the ligand induces a conformational change in a membrane-bound receptor to trigger downstream signalling pathways that elicit physiological changes in blood glucose concentration". So the required content is: insulin binds an RTK, glucagon binds a GPLR, each causes a conformational change, each triggers a pathway, one raises glucose uptake and storage and the other mobilises glucose. Fig. 37's eight numbered steps naming glycogen phosphorylase kinase are past the boundary.

**Background reading, and the school says so itself:**

- **Section 2, Homeostasis, pages 26 to 30.** The section header is literally "SECTION 2: HOMEOSTASIS - BACKGROUND READING". The word "homeostasis" does not appear anywhere in the 9477 syllabus, nor does "negative feedback", nor "set point". `[OFFICIAL]` This is five pages with no learning outcome behind it. Read it once for the vocabulary, because a question stem may use the words, and move on.
- **Section 3, The Endocrine System, pages 31 to 33.** Same label, same finding. The pancreas anatomy on page 33 is worth one careful read because alpha and beta cells are load-bearing for LO 3(p), but the endocrine gland tour on page 31 and the exocrine acinar detail on page 33 are not examinable.
- **Section 7, Diabetes Mellitus, pages 43 to 44.** Marked "(Supplementary)" by the school. The word "diabetes" appears zero times in the 9477 syllabus. `[OFFICIAL]` Useful as an application context for a stem, never as recall.
- **Intracellular receptors, ligand-gated ion channels and integrin receptors.** The chapter marks all three as out of syllabus, and LO 3(m) confirms it for intracellular receptors in writing. Skip Concept Check 1's rows for them.
- The aquarium analogy on page 28 and the oscillation graph on page 30.
- The calcium-pump inventory on page 18. Knowing that cytosolic calcium is kept low, and why that makes a brief rise usable as a signal, is the point. The four named pump types are not.

## Chapter 3.2: Cell and Nuclear Division (36 pages)

### 1. Stated learning outcomes (page 1, verbatim)

> **Learning Outcomes**
> Candidates should be able to:
>
> 2(n) describe the events that occur during the mitotic cell cycle and the main stages of mitosis (including the behaviour of chromosomes, nuclear envelope, cell surface membrane and centrioles)
>
> 2(o) explain the significance of the mitotic cell cycle (including growth, repair and asexual reproduction) and the need to regulate it tightly (knowledge that dysregulation of checkpoints of cell division can result in uncontrolled cell division and cancer is required, but details of the mechanism are not required)
>
> 2(s) describe the events that occur during the meiotic cell cycle and the main stages of meiosis (including the behaviour of chromosomes, nuclear envelope, cell surface membrane and centrioles) (names of the main stages are expected, but not the sub-divisions of prophase)
>
> 2(t) explain the significance of the meiotic cell cycle (including how meiosis and random fertilisation can lead to variation)

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 Fig. 12's caption says microtubules extend from the nearest chromosome. They extend from the nearest pole.** Page 12: "Here, each kinetochore is attached to a cluster of kinetochore microtubules extending from the nearest **chromosome**." A kinetochore microtubule runs from a centrosome at a spindle pole to a kinetochore on a chromosome. Saying it extends from the nearest chromosome makes the sentence describe chromosomes joined to each other, which is not the spindle. `[INFERRED]` The word should be **centrosome**, and the same figure in Campbell reads that way. This matters because Specimen Paper 2 Question 6 is built entirely on the distance between centromeres and spindle poles.

**5.2 When centrioles replicate is stated three different ways in one chapter.**

- Page 11: "**At the onset of mitosis**, centriole pairs are duplicated, and each pair moves to the opposite poles of the cell"
- Page 14, G2 phase: "**Centrioles replicate** and the mitotic spindle begins to form."
- Page 23: "The pair of centrioles also replicates **during interphase**."

Pages 14 and 23 agree with each other and with the biology; centriole duplication is an interphase event. Page 11 is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that would cost marks on LO 2(n), which names centrioles explicitly as one of the four structures whose behaviour must be described. **Use G2 of interphase.**

**5.3 DNA replication is said to occur during all three interphase sub-phases.** Page 14 opens the interphase section with "During all three sub-phases, the cell grows by **replicating DNA**, producing proteins and cytoplasmic organelles". The table directly beneath it, on the same page, restricts semi-conservative replication to S phase, which is correct. As printed, the introductory sentence contradicts the table it introduces. Replication happens in S phase only.

**5.4 Independent assortment is attributed to metaphase II as well as metaphase I in one place and to metaphase I alone in another.**

- Page 32, the significance-of-stages table, lists independent assortment under **Metaphase I only**.
- Page 34, section (b), is headed "Independent assortment of homologous chromosomes at metaphase I of meiosis **and metaphase II of meiosis**" and then says "Likewise, at metaphase II of meiosis, chromosomes randomly align singly at the metaphase plate. The orientation of one non-sister chromatid (due to crossing over) is independent of the other."
- Page 35, the mitosis-versus-meiosis table, lists independent assortment only in the **Metaphase** row.

The page 34 claim is not biologically wrong; random orientation of sister chromatids that have been made non-identical by crossing over does add variation at metaphase II. But it is non-standard for A-level, it conflicts with the chapter's own table two pages earlier, and the heading itself is self-contradictory because metaphase II has no homologous chromosomes to assort. **Answer independent assortment at metaphase I.** If a stem specifically asks about metaphase II, the safe wording is that sister chromatids made non-identical by crossing over separate randomly, which is a consequence of crossing over rather than an independent mechanism.

**5.5 The synaptonemal complex is said to contain RNA.** Page 24: "the homologues are bridged by the synaptonemal complex consisting of **proteins and RNA**". The synaptonemal complex is a proteinaceous tripartite structure: two lateral elements, a central element, and transverse filaments. `[INFERRED]` The RNA claim comes from older literature and is not the modern description. It costs nothing to say "a protein structure" instead, and the syllabus does not require the synaptonemal complex at all, so this is low stakes.

**5.6 The final section is misnumbered.** Page 35's heading reads "**4.** Comparing Mitosis and Meiosis". Section 4 is already Nuclear Division, Mitosis, back on page 15. The page 2 outline numbers this section correctly as **6**.

**5.7 "Four stages" versus a three-item list.** Page 13: "eukaryotic cells continually progress through **the four stages** of the cell cycle" and then immediately "The cell cycle consists of: 1. Interphase... 2. Mitosis... 3. Cytokinesis." Four refers to G1, S, G2 and M per Fig. 13a; three refers to the school's own grouping. Not wrong, but a candidate asked to name the stages of the cell cycle needs to know which list is wanted, and the answer depends on whether the question says "phases" (G1, S, G2, M) or "stages" (interphase, nuclear division, cytokinesis).

**5.8 The astral microtubule function is circular.** Page 12: astral microtubules "**Serve as a support for the functioning of the microtubules**." That defines microtubules by reference to microtubules. Their actual job is to anchor the spindle poles to the cell cortex so the spindle is positioned correctly. `[INFERRED]`

**5.9 Loose wording about what the spindle attaches to.** Page 12: "An organised system of microtubules that attaches to the **centromeres** of duplicated chromosomes". Page 10 is precise on the same point, saying the kinetochore "is the structure to which the microtubules of the spindle are attached". Kinetochore is the precise noun and is what a scheme wants. The page 12 wording is the loose one.

**5.10 Typographical.** Page 21: "Many animal and plant **series** are propagated by asexual reproduction" should read species. Page 14: "After the DNA **have** replicated". Page 27: "each pair **homologous of** chromosomes" has two words transposed. Page 30's exponent notation for the 2^n independent-assortment formula and 2^23 extracts as "2n" and "223" from the text layer, but the accompanying phrase "or about 8 million" confirms the printed superscripts are correct, so that is an extraction artefact and not a school error.

Beyond these, the chapter is careful and mostly excellent. The stage-by-stage descriptions, the significance tables on pages 18 and 32, the animal-versus-plant cytokinesis contrast and the full comparison table on page 35 are all internally consistent and match mark-scheme language closely.

### 6. Load-bearing versus skippable

**Load-bearing:**

- The four stages of mitosis on pages 15 to 18, described against all four structures LO 2(n) names. For each stage, what the chromosomes do, what the nuclear envelope does, what the cell surface membrane does, and what the centrioles do. Most candidates cover chromosomes and forget the other three. Specimen Paper 2 Question 6(a) asked for stage identification off a graph, so stage recognition from data is the tested form, not stage recitation.
- The definitions block on pages 9 to 12, all nine terms, at close to verbatim precision. Corpus schemes reject "same number of genes" as a difference and reject calling homologues "genetically identical".
- Centromere and kinetochore, both, kept distinct. Specimen Paper 2 Question 6(b) was "Outline the roles of centromeres" for three marks, which is a full three-point answer: joining sister chromatids after replication, providing the site at which kinetochores assemble, and holding sister chromatids together until anaphase so that separation is simultaneous and equal.
- The three microtubule types on page 12 with their distinct jobs. Kinetochore microtubules shorten and pull chromatids poleward; polar microtubules slide past each other and elongate the cell; astral microtubules anchor the poles. Specimen Paper 2 Question 6 shows all three as separate distance-against-time curves, so telling them apart on a graph is a real skill.
- The interphase table on page 14, G1, S and G2 each with its purpose, and the fact that DNA replicates once and only in S.
- The two significance tables, page 18 for mitosis and page 32 for meiosis. These are already written in the exact shape of a "chromosomal behaviour, therefore significance" answer, which is the shape LO 2(o) and LO 2(t) ask for. They are the single most efficient pages in the chapter.
- All of meiosis I, pages 24 to 27. Synapsis, bivalent and tetrad, crossing over between non-sister chromatids, chiasmata, independent assortment at metaphase I, disjunction of homologues at anaphase I with centromeres intact. Note that centromeres **not** dividing at anaphase I is the single fact that separates meiosis I from both mitosis and meiosis II, and the corpus schemes reject "duplicated chromosomes" and "V-shaped chromosomes" as answers where the precise noun is wanted.
- The three sources of variation on pages 33 to 34: crossing over, independent assortment, random fertilisation. LO 2(t) names meiosis and random fertilisation together in the outcome text, so an answer that stops at crossing over and independent assortment has left out a third of what the outcome asks for. The 2^n arithmetic and the 2^23 figure for humans are worth having.
- The comparison table on page 35, drilled to the point of reproduction. Specimen Paper 2 Question 6(c) was "State **four** ways in which the behaviour of chromosomes in meiosis is different from their behaviour in mitosis" for four marks, and this table is the answer key. - Animal versus plant cytokinesis, pages 19 to 20. This is the cell surface membrane half of LOs 2(n) and 2(s), which is the part most revision misses.
- Significance of mitosis on page 21: genetic stability, growth and repair and regeneration, asexual reproduction. LO 2(o) names growth, repair and asexual reproduction in exactly those words, so this is the outcome quoted back.
- The two image-only concept maps on pages 3 and 4, which carry the DNA-content-against-time graphs and the chromosome-number, DNA-amount and ploidy-level tables for every stage. Reading DNA content off a cell-cycle graph is a recurring exam skill and these are the only place in the chapter it is laid out.

- The tubulin polymerisation detail in Fig. 11, which the chapter itself marks "(FYI)". Thirteen protofilaments around a hollow core is not examinable.
- The ploidy exercise table on page 7. Useful as a five-minute drill, not as content.

### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

**5.1 The opening paragraph says cancer incidence is rising because mutations accumulate from one generation to the next. That is the classic somatic-mutation error.** Page 1: "The recorded incidence of cancer is on the rise and this could be due to the **accumulation of mutations from one generation to the next**". Cancer arises from somatic mutations accumulating within one individual's lifetime, in a single cell lineage. Somatic mutations are not passed to offspring. The chapter's own page 19 gets this right, saying the multi-step process is "characterised by accumulation of mutations **in a single cell lineage**", and page 30 gets it right again, saying "It is the predisposition to cancer (not the cancer itself) that is inherited." The opening paragraph contradicts both. This matters more than a typical error because the somatic-versus-germline distinction is an explicitly examined trap in the corpus.

**5.2 The colorectal case study gives two incompatible gene counts.**

- Page 24: "at least **three** tumour suppressor gene mutations and **one** oncogene must be mutated in sequence"
- Page 24, Fig. 20 caption: "In colon cancer, at least **5 genes** are mutated in a single cell lineage."
- Page 25 then walks through exactly **four** mutations: APC, ras, DCC, p53.
- Page 26, Fig. 21 caption names four chromosomes: 5, 12, 17 and 18.

Three plus one is four, not five. The body text and the figure caption on the same page disagree, and the detailed walkthrough that follows agrees with the body text.

**5.3 The same case study says mutations must occur in sequence and then says the order does not matter.**

- Page 24: "at least three tumour suppressor gene mutations and one oncogene must be mutated **in sequence**"
- Page 26, Fig. 21 caption: "Although the mutations on chromosomes 12, 17, and 18 usually occur at a later stage than those involving chromosome 5, **the sum of changes is more important than the order in which they occur**."

Both statements are quoted directly from different sources and the chapter never reconciles them. `[INFERRED]` The reconciliation is that a typical order exists and is well documented for colorectal cancer, but the requirement is that the mutations accumulate in one lineage, not that they arrive in a fixed order. If a question asks whether order matters, the second statement is the defensible one, and it is also the one the multi-step model actually claims.

**5.4 The BRCA section says tumour suppressor mutations are inherited in a dominant manner, two pages after establishing that they are recessive.**

- Page 13: "in order to abolish the function of the tumour suppressor genes totally, **both copies** of the tumour suppressor gene must be mutated... thus described as mutated tumour suppressor genes act in a **recessive** manner"
- Page 27: "BRCA1 and BRCA 2 are **tumor suppressor genes**." then "The cancer risk caused by BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations are inherited in a **dominant** manner even though usually only one mutated allele is directly inherited."

As printed these look like a flat contradiction, and the chapter offers no bridge. `[INFERRED]` Both are true at different levels of description, and knowing the bridge is worth marks: **at the level of the cell**, loss of tumour-suppressor function is recessive, because one working allele still makes enough protein. **At the level of the family**, predisposition is inherited as an autosomal dominant, because a person who inherits one mutant allele needs only one further somatic hit in any one cell rather than two, so their lifetime risk is greatly raised. This is the two-hit picture, and it is exactly what page 25 describes for APC without naming it. Ethan should hold that two-level explanation ready, because a question that hands him the apparent contradiction is a natural high-mark stem.

**5.5 All cyclin-Cdk complexes are called MPF.** Page 10: "Thus forming cyclin-Cdk complexes **also called maturation-promoting factor (MPF)**." MPF is specifically the cyclin B and Cdk1 complex that drives entry into M phase. The chapter's own Fig. 8 on the same page describes cyclin synthesis beginning in late S and MPF activity peaking at metaphase, which is the cyclin B story, not a general one. Calling every cyclin-Cdk pair an MPF is an over-generalisation. Low stakes, because see section 6: none of this content has a learning outcome.

**5.6 Cross-reference error, page 14.** "p53 protein is a transcription factor that binds to DNA to trigger transcription of genes involved in cell cycle inhibition **(Fig. 10)**." Fig. 10a and Fig. 10b are on page 13 and neither shows this. The figure on page 14 that does show it is **Fig. 11**.

**5.7 Cross-reference error, page 17, twice.** "Quantitative change **[Points (b), (c) and (e) on page 19]**" and "Qualitative change **[Points (a), (c), (d) and (e) on page 19]**". Points (a) to (e) are the five mechanisms in the Figure 15 caption on **page 18**. Page 19 is the opening of the multi-step model section. Both references are off by one.

**5.8 Two incompatible taxonomies of proto-oncogene conversion, one page apart.** Fig. 14 on page 17 says the genetic changes "fall into **three** main categories: movement of DNA within the genome, amplification of a proto-oncogene, point mutations". Figure 15 on page 18 lists **five**: point mutation, gene amplification, chromosomal translocation, local DNA rearrangement, insertional mutagenesis. The five collapse into the three if you group translocation and local rearrangement under movement of DNA and treat insertional mutagenesis as a special case of movement, but the chapter never says so. `[INFERRED]` Use the three-category version for a short answer and the five-mechanism version if asked for named examples.

**5.9 A non-sequitur about ageing, page 14.** "It can activate DNA repair proteins when DNA has sustained damage. **Thus, it may be an important factor in aging.**" Nothing in the surrounding text supports the inference and no ageing argument is developed anywhere in the chapter. The sentence appears to have been lifted from an external source with its context removed.

**5.10 Telomerase is twice said to let cancer cells evade apoptosis.** Page 5: "Telomerase appears to allow cancer cells to **evade apoptosis**, and it is thought that the abnormal retention of telomeres is involved in the development of some types of cancer." Page 21 repeats it. What telomerase actually prevents is replicative senescence and the crisis that follows critical telomere shortening. It does not act on the apoptotic machinery. `[INFERRED]` This one is worth knowing about because the corpus records an examiner comment on the neighbouring point: *"common misconception is that telomerase helps to maintain the telomere length and not extend it"* `[SCHOOL]`, so telomerase wording is already an area where schemes are fussy.

**5.11 Checkpoint versus kinetochore imprecision, page 9.** The M checkpoint box says "If spindle fibres are not formed or attachment to **centromere** is inadequate, mitosis is arrested", while the same box two lines earlier correctly says the checkpoint "Checks if there is successful formation of spindle fibres and attachment of spindle fibres to the **kinetochores** of chromosomes." Chapter 3.2 is careful about this distinction throughout, so this is a within-book inconsistency.

**5.12 An unattributed and mislabelled statistic in the breast cancer case study, page 27.** "Affected people have a 29% risk until age 50 and a 44% risk until age 70 to be affected by **ovarian** cancer." The sentence sits in the middle of a paragraph about breast cancer incidence, the population meant by "affected people" is never defined, and no source is given. `[INFERRED]` Treat the numbers as unusable. The section is marked FYI in any case.

**5.13 Typographical.** Page 10: "conditions in the cell favour degradation of **cyclic**" should read cyclin. Page 13 heading: "p53, A TUMOUR **SUPRESSOR** GENE" against "SUPPRESSOR" in the page 3 outline. Page 18 caption: "or it may **a** proto-oncogene next to a highly active gene" is missing its verb. Page 29: "**polycylic** aromatic hydrocarbons" and "**heterocylic** amines" should both carry the second c. Page 30: "an **oncognene**", "loss of normal **grown** control mechanisms", and "resulting uncontrollable cell division" missing "in". Page 30's UV section opens with "Ultraviolet radiation is the radiation from the sun to the earth", which is not a definition of ultraviolet radiation.

**5.14 Duplicated content.** Angiogenesis is taught twice, as characteristic 6 on page 6 and as section 3.3 on page 22, and metastasis is taught twice, as characteristic 7 on page 6 and as section 3.4 on page 23, with the three-step metastasis cascade printed word for word identically in both places. ### 5. Errors, contradictions and ambiguities

This is the least reliable chapter in Book 3. It is stitched together from at least six different external sources, and where the sources disagree the chapter reproduces both.

**5.1 Embryonic stem cells are said to be totipotent on page 14 and pluripotent everywhere else.** This is the most serious error in the chapter because it destroys exactly the distinction LO 1(t) is built on.

- Page 8: "**Embryonic Stem Cells (ESCs) are examples for pluripotent stem cells.**" and pluripotent cells "**do not** have the potential to make differentiated cells that form the extra-embryonic membranes"
- Page 13, Table 1.1: Embryonic stem cells are listed as "**Pluripotent**"
- Page 14: "that cell begins to divide, producing a blastocyst... At this very early point, each of these cells is identical. **We call these cells embryonic stem cells. Each one of them is capable by itself of developing into a healthy individual.**"

A cell capable by itself of developing into a healthy individual is by definition totipotent. Page 14 therefore states that ESCs are totipotent, in the section headed "1.3.1 Embryonic Stem Cells", two pages before the chapter lists pluripotency as a defining ESC property. The correct position is the one on pages 8, 13 and 16: ESCs are pluripotent, they make all three germ layers, and they cannot make the placenta, which is why they cannot alone form an individual.

**5.2 When totipotency ends is stated three different ways.**

- Page 1, chapter narrative: totipotent cells are the zygote and the products of the first few divisions "**up to the eight-cell stage**"
- Page 8, definition box header: "(Formed immediately upon fertilization, **up to first 5 days after fertilization**)"
- Page 8, body: "totipotent stem cells occur at the earliest stage of embryonic development; **before the blastocyst stage**"

Day 5 after fertilisation is the blastocyst, per the chapter's own Table 1.2 on page 14, which puts the blastocyst at "Fifth day through second week". So the parenthetical header contradicts the body of the very box it heads. The eight-cell stage is roughly day 3. `[INFERRED]` The defensible line for an exam is that totipotency is lost as the cells of the early cleaving embryo begin to specialise, and that by the blastocyst stage the inner cell mass cells are pluripotent, not totipotent. If a question forces a number, use the morula and the eight-cell stage rather than day 5, because day 5 is the blastocyst and the blastocyst is the pluripotent stage.

**5.3 A myeloid progenitor is said to generate all blood cell types.** Page 11: "For example, the **myeloid progenitor cell** depicted in Fig. 1.4B on page 10 **can generate all the different types of blood cells**." It cannot. A common myeloid progenitor gives rise to erythrocytes, platelets, granulocytes and monocytes; it cannot give rise to lymphocytes. The chapter itself sets out the correct division of labour nine pages later, on page 20: "Erythroid and myeloid lineages are derived from the common myeloid progenitor cell. **The lymphoid lineage is derived from the common lymphoid progenitor cell.**" Page 20 is right and page 11 is wrong. This matters because Specimen Paper 3 Question 3 opens on lymphoid stem cells specifically. `[OFFICIAL]`

**5.4 The chapter says commitment is irreversible and also that it is not.**

- Page 12, IN SUMMARY: "Once committed, however, they **usually do not switch commitment**. When placed in a new environment, **they will not change the type of cells they generate**."
- Page 4: "adult stem cells from one tissue appear to be **capable of developing into cell types that are characteristic of other tissues**... adult stem cell plasticity i.e. transdifferentiation"
- Page 11: progenitor cells are committed to a pathway "although this characteristic **may not be as definitive as once thought**"
- Page 27: iPSCs are a "powerful new way to **de-differentiate** cells whose developmental fates had been previously assumed to be determined"

Page 12's flat statement is contradicted by three other places in the same chapter. `[INFERRED]` The resolution is that commitment is stable under normal physiological conditions and can be overridden experimentally, and that is the version worth writing. Specimen Paper 1 Question 8 tests exactly the stable version: a bone marrow stem cell moved to skin never makes a skin cell. `[OFFICIAL]`

**5.5 Page 18 denies any pluripotent adult stem cell, which sits oddly with the rest of the chapter.** Page 18: "there is **no evidence, at this time**, of an adult stem cell that is pluripotent." Table 1.1 on page 13 says "some studies suggest certain adult stem cells may be able to become pluripotent", and section 1.4.2 on page 27 is entirely about adult cells reprogrammed to pluripotency. The three are reconcilable, because an iPSC is a reprogrammed adult cell rather than a naturally pluripotent adult stem cell, but the chapter never says so. For LO 1(t) and 1(u) the page 18 position is the one to use: naturally occurring adult stem cells are multipotent at most.

**5.6 Cross-reference errors.**

- Page 11: "as illustrated by Fig. 1.5 below and **Fig. 1.6 on page 11**". Fig. 1.6 is on page **12**.
- Page 20: "Erythrocytes, leukocytes and platelets are derived from 3 main lineages (as shown in Fig. 1.13 on page 22 & **Fig. 1.11 on page 19**)". Fig. 1.11 is "Cellular Components of Blood" and shows no lineages at all. The intended reference is almost certainly Fig. 1.12 on page 20.
- Page 15: "as shown in **Table 1.2 on page 14**". Table 1.2 is indeed on page 14, so that one is correct, but because Table 1.2 is an image with no text layer it looks missing to any tool that reads the PDF text only.

**5.7 A typo that inverts the meaning, page 13.** Table 1.1, adult stem cell row: "some studies suggest certain adult stem cells may be able to become pluripotent, but it has been difficult to **replace** these studies". The word should be **replicate**. As printed the sentence says something different and slightly nonsensical, and the point being made, which is that the plasticity findings have been hard to reproduce, is a real and important caveat.

**5.8 A garbled parenthesis, page 13.** Table 1.1, adult stem cell source cell: "Often from adult tissues/ organs (note: **this term is also multipotent cells found in fetuses or younger individuals**)". The sentence does not parse. `[INFERRED]` The intended meaning is that the label "adult stem cell" is also applied to multipotent cells found in fetuses and in young individuals, so the word "adult" describes the cell's degree of commitment rather than the donor's age. That is a genuinely useful clarification and it is worth reconstructing rather than skipping.

**5.9 The chapter says pluripotent cells can generate germ cells, and separately that they arise from three germ layers.** Page 10: "Pluripotent stem cells are derived from the inner cell mass of the blastocyst. They can generate ectoderm, endoderm, mesoderm **and germ cells**." Page 8 defines pluripotency by the three germ layers only. Germ cells do arise from pluripotent cells, so page 10 is not wrong, but a candidate answering LO 1(t) should give the three-germ-layer definition, because that is the one the outcome's own vocabulary supports.

**5.10 Small inconsistencies and typography.** Page 20: "hemato**poe**tic" against "hematopoietic" elsewhere. Page 4 says pluripotent cells "can develop into any cell type in the body" in one sentence and "almost all of the more than 200 different known cell types" in the next. The chapter uses American spellings throughout ("unspecialized", "specialized", "fertilization") because its sources are American; Ethan should write British forms. The section heading numbering runs 1.1 to 1.4 with no chapter-level "1", and the body never prints the "1.4 CHALLENGES IN USING EMBRYONIC STEM CELLS" header that the page 3 outline promises, going straight to 1.4.1.
