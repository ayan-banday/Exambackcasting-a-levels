Question 1 [8 marks]

(a) Changes in DNA amount and chromosome numbers were monitored during one cycle of meiosis. Results are shown in Fig. 1.1.

Figure not recovered. Described from the source paper: Fig. 1.1, a step graph with two vertical axes. The left axis is amount of DNA per nucleus in arbitrary units, marked at 2 and 4. The right axis is number of chromosomes per nucleus, marked at 2n and 4n. The x-axis runs Interphase I, Telophase I, Telophase II. A solid line shows the amount of DNA: it sits at 2, steps up to 4 during interphase I, holds at 4 until telophase I, steps down to just above 2, holds, then steps down again at telophase II. A dashed line shows the number of chromosomes: it sits at 2n throughout interphase I and meiosis I, then steps down at telophase I to below 2n and holds there through telophase II. A key labels the solid line amount of DNA and the dashed line number of chromosomes.

Explain why DNA amount and chromosome numbers remain constant during the two anaphases in Fig. 1.1. [2]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (a)

(a) During anaphase I and II, even though homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids are moving towards the poles respectively.

The genetic material still remains within the same cell, the nucleus is not formed yet and the cell has not divided into 2 individual cells.

So DNA content and chromosome number will remain unchanged / will remain constant.

Note on the counting convention, since the figure plots both quantities per nucleus. The step in each trace is read at telophase, once the nuclei have re-formed, which is why the anaphases look flat. If you were instead counting centromeres, chromosome number would double at anaphase II the moment the centromeres divide and return to the daughter value at cytokinesis. Both conventions are accepted. Halving chromosome number and DNA content at the same stage is accepted under neither.

Question 2 [5 marks]

The diagram below shows the distribution of cells of varying DNA content during interphase.

Figure not recovered. Described from the source paper: a graph of number of cells against DNA content, the x-axis marked 2C and 4C. A tall narrow peak sits at 2C and is boxed and labelled X. A low flat trough between 2C and 4C is boxed and labelled Y. A smaller peak sits at 4C and is boxed and labelled Z.

Which of the following statements can be concluded from the diagram above?

1 Most of the cells are in G1 phase. 2 All cells in area Z will eventually complete the nuclear division. 3 Area Y represents the number of cells undergoing anaphase.

A 1 only

B 1 and 3 only

C 2 and 3 only

D 1, 2 and 3

For every one of the three statements, say whether it is correct and why, then give the option. [5]

---

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer

The key is A.

Statement 1 is correct. Peak X sits at 2C, the unreplicated diploid DNA content, so the cells in X are in G1, and X is by far the tallest peak, so most cells are in G1. The reason it is the tallest is the general one: on a snapshot of an asynchronous population the number of cells caught in a phase is proportional to how long that phase lasts, and G1 is the longest phase of the cycle.

Statement 2 is wrong. Area Z holds cells at 4C, which have completed replication and are in G2 or M, but there is no guarantee that every one of them completes nuclear division: a cell that fails a checkpoint is held there, and a cell may leave the cycle instead. Reading a peak as a promise about what happens next is the error.

Statement 3 is wrong. Area Y holds cells with a DNA content between 2C and 4C, so these are cells caught partway through replication, which is S phase, not anaphase. A cell in anaphase has already finished replicating and therefore sits in the 4C peak with Z. Note also that the stem says the distribution is of cells during interphase, and anaphase is not interphase.

Gap check for this question. B3-16 is statement 1, on a histogram you have not seen before, with the phase not named for you. B3-15 is statement 3, because the only way to place a cell in anaphase on this axis is to remember that anaphase changes chromosome number and not DNA content, so anaphase cells still read 4C.

Source: VJC Prelim 2024 P1 Q11, stem, figure, all three statements and all four options exactly as the paper prints them, with the figure extracted from the question paper. The VJC answer table gives the letter A only, so the statement-by-statement reasoning is [INFERRED] and the marks were raised from the paper's 1 to 5 to force a justification of all three statements. This is the only multiple choice part in the drill, and it is here because gap B3-16 is about reading a histogram and so has to use one. Concepts fused: peak height against phase duration, DNA content against chromosome number, 2C and 4C notation, G1, S and G2 read off a profile, checkpoint arrest and G0 as reasons a cell may not divide, why anaphase cells are not in the trough.

Question 3 [6 marks]

Describe the differences in behaviour of homologous chromosomes during mitosis and the first division of meiosis. [6]

---

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer

Six differences, point by point, mitosis against meiosis I.

Basis of comparisonMitosisMeiosis I
SynapsisDuring prophase, no synapsis / no pairing of homologous chromosomes.During prophase I, synapsis occurs / homologous chromosomes pair up to form bivalents.
Crossing overDuring prophase, no crossing over occurs between homologous chromosomes.During prophase I, crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes at chiasmata.
Arrangement at the equatorDuring metaphase, all the individual chromosomes arrange in a single row on the equator of the cell.During metaphase I, homologous chromosomes arrange in two rows on the equator of the cell.

AVP.

The question as a whole is the decay check on the mitosis-against-meiosis comparison, which you produced well last time.

Question 7 [10 marks]

With the aid of large and clearly labelled diagrams, explain the significance of the behaviour of chromosomes during meiosis, which can lead to genetic variation in plants. [10]

---

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer

Prophase I. Labelled diagram showing homologous chromosomes, crossing over occurring between non-sister chromatids at the chiasmata, and different alleles.

Description: homologous chromosomes pair up; homologous chromosomes have the same size, shape and centromere location, but different alleles at the same gene loci; crossing over.

Significance: exchange of genetic material (alleles) between homologous sections of non-sister chromatids; gives rise to new combinations of paternal and maternal alleles; gametes will have chromosomes that are different from the parental cells; any valid point.

Metaphase I. Labelled diagram showing kinetochore spindle fibre, metaphase plate, paternal homologue, maternal homologue and recombinant chromatid.

Description: pairs of homologous chromosomes align at the metaphase plate; each homologue on one side of the metaphase plate; two or more pairs of homologous chromosomes having different orientations of paternal and maternal recombinant chromosomes towards the poles.

Significance: independent assortment; there is random distribution of paternal and maternal chromosomes in each daughter cell; the number of possible combinations of gametes due to independent assortment is 2ⁿ for a diploid organism, where n is the number of sets of homologous chromosomes; daughter cells have different combinations of chromosomes compared to parental cells; any valid point.

Marking note for a spoken attempt. The diagram marks are for labels, so say every label out loud that you would write on the drawing, then give the description and the significance separately. A description without its significance scores half, and a significance claim that does not tally with the behaviour you described scores nothing.

Gap check for this question. Nothing in this scheme is awarded for mitosis, and the examiner comment on the sister question in this family is that candidates lose marks by confusing sister chromatids with daughter chromosomes and by calling synapsis crossing over, so keep those four nouns apart while you talk.

Question 8 [5 marks]

(a) Explain how a mutation to the centromeric DNA can lead to aneuploidy. [3]

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (a)

(a) Change in sequence of centromeric DNA.

Shape of the mutated centromeric sequence is no longer complementary to the kinetochore protein complex, hence they cannot bind.

Microtubules / spindle fibres are unable to attach to the centromere via the kinetochore.

Non-disjunction occurs, where sister chromatids are not separated properly, giving rise to cells with extra or missing chromosome(s) / 2n+1 and 2n−1.

Or, for the alternative route: change in sequence of centromeric DNA; shape of the mutated centromeric sequence is no longer complementary to cohesin proteins, hence cohesin cannot bind; sister chromatids are not held together prior to anaphase and so spindle fibres from one pole may attach to both sister chromatids; non-disjunction occurs when two sister chromatids are pulled to one pole, giving rise to cells with extra or missing chromosome(s) / 2n+1 and 2n−1.

(b) Cases of mammalian tetraploidy are rare. The Plains Viscacha Rat, Tympanoctomys barrerae, is one of the few polyploid mammals in existence and it is tetraploid (4n) with 112 chromosomes. It was hypothesised that Tympanoctomys barrerae evolved from the same common ancestor as Octomys mimax, a diploid rodent with 56 chromosomes (2n).

Explain how tetraploidy may have first developed in the ancestral rat population. [2]

---

Say your confidence out loud before revealing: high / medium / guessed.
Answer - part (b)

(b) Spindle fibres may not have formed during prophase I of meiosis.

Cytokinesis may not have occurred during meiosis, and therefore the number of chromosomes remains in the diploid state.

The fusion of two diploid gametes could have given rise to the tetraploid rat.

Gap check for this question. Both parts are B3-15 approached from the failure side, which is the hardest place to hold the two counters apart. In (a) the chromosome counter goes wrong while the DNA counter is untouched, because replication happened normally and only the separation failed. In (b) a division is skipped entirely, so the gamete keeps the diploid chromosome number, and the arithmetic only works if you can say what each counter would have been had the division run. If you find yourself reasoning in ploidy levels alone here, go back to the table in the mend doc.
