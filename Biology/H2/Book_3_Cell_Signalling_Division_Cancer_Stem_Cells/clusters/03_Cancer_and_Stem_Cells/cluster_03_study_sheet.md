# PART 1: THE MOLECULAR BASIS OF CANCER

## A. What cancer is

**A1. Cancer is uncontrolled cell division caused by mutation.** **Mutations in the genes that control
cell division** produce a **dysregulation of cellular checkpoints**, so a cell divides uncontrollably.

**A2. Uncontrolled does not mean faster.** The crucial issue is **the rate of cell division against the
rate of cell loss**. Normal tissue holds the two **in balance, so no net accumulation** of new cells
occurs; in cancer, division **far exceeds** loss, giving **net uncontrolled proliferation**. A scheme
rejects excessive or uncontrolled cell **growth** where **division** is meant.

**A3. One mutated cell becomes a clone, and the clone gets worse.** The dividing cell **passes on the
damaged genes** to its daughters, giving **a clone of altered cells** carrying an **unstable genome**.
That clone **accumulates more mutations** and so **escapes normal growth restraints**: **anchorage
dependence** (normal cells **must contact a substratum** before they proliferate, cancer cells
**proliferate in suspension**), **contact and density inhibition** (normal cells stop at **a single
layer**, cancer cells **pile up in disordered, multilayered patterns**), and **replicative senescence**.

**A4. Four more characteristics carry outcomes.** A **high rate of division**, because the cell divides
**in the absence of growth factors**. **Genome instability**, an increased rate of mutation at
chromosomal and gene level. **Inducing angiogenesis** and **metastasis**, both below. And **avoiding
immune destruction**, by disabling the immune components sent to eliminate them.

**A5. Benign against malignant, which LO 2(r) needs.** Benign tumours have **few genetic mutations**,
**do not cause serious health problems**, and **could be completely removed by surgery**. Malignant
tumours are **invasive**, **impair functions of organs**, and mean the individual **is considered to
have cancer**, so they need **systemic treatment** alongside surgery. Cancer progressing from one to the
other is the third line of evidence in section E.

## B. The two classes of cancer-critical gene

**B1. Each class has a one-line definition, and these are the lines to write.** A tumour suppressor
gene is a normal gene that
**encodes a protein which inhibits uncontrolled cell division**. A proto-oncogene is a normal gene that
**encodes a protein which stimulates normal cell division**. The school's analogy fixes direction: the
tumour suppressor is **the brake pedal**, the proto-oncogene the accelerator.

**B2. Five functions of tumour-suppressor proteins.** They **inhibit the cell cycle** through signalling
pathways; **halt cell division if DNA is damaged**; **trigger DNA repair mechanisms**; **initiate
apoptosis** where damage cannot be repaired; and **maintain cell adhesion**, since **metastasis
suppressors** hold **cell-to-cell adhesion** and **anchorage to the extracellular matrix**. That fifth
one is why losing a tumour suppressor can make a tumour invasive rather than merely fast.

**B3. Five functions of proto-oncogene products.** **Growth factors**, the external signals that
stimulate division. **Growth factor receptors**, the membrane proteins that bind them. **Protein
kinases**, which **add phosphate groups** and usually activate. **Inhibitors of apoptosis**, which
**reduce the rate of cell death**. And **transcription factors**, controlling the rate of transcription,
the school's example being **myc**. Two of the five are the reception step of a signalling pathway,
which is where this chapter meets Cluster 1.

**B4. The contrast the outcome is built on.** Specimen P2 Q3(b) asked for the differences for two
marks, in the outcome's own phrases.

| | **Tumour suppressor gene** | **Proto-oncogene** |
|---|---|---|
| Normal product | **inhibits uncontrolled cell division** | **stimulates normal cell division** |
| Mutation | **loss of function**, giving **abolished protein function** | **gain in function**: **over-expressed** or a **hyperactive protein** |
| Copies mutated | **both**, since one intact copy makes **sufficient normal gene product** | **one copy is sufficient** |
| At the level of the cell | **recessive** | **dominant** |
| Mutant is called | a mutated tumour suppressor gene | an **oncogene** |

Two rejects sit on this table: **referring to a loss of function mutation as a single mutation event**,
since both copies means at least two events, and **referring to alleles as genes**.

## C. p53, the tumour suppressor the outcome names

**C1. p53 is mutated in about half of all human cancers.** It is critical for its **triple involvement
in cell cycle control, in apoptosis, and in maintenance of genetic stability**, which is why it is
called the **Guardian of the Genome**.

**C2. The protein is a transcription factor, present only when needed.** It **binds to DNA to trigger
transcription of genes involved in cell cycle inhibition**, and **high levels are only seen when its DNA
is damaged**. It is a stress sensor, not a constant brake.

**C3. p53 acts by three mechanisms, and the second carries the detail.** It **activates DNA repair
proteins**
where DNA has been damaged. It **arrests growth at the G1/S regulation point** on damage recognition, so
**the damaged DNA is not replicated** and there is time to repair it; the route is that activated p53
**promotes transcription of the p21 gene**, and **p21 protein stops the cell cycle by binding proteins
involved in cell cycle progression**, which the school names as Cdks. And it **initiates apoptosis if
the damage proves irreparable**, **activating suicide genes** so a damaged cell does not proliferate.

**C4. Run the answer forwards, from the mutation.** Mutated p53 gives **no functional p53 protein**, so
**the cell cycle is not inhibited**, so damaged DNA is replicated and mutations accumulate, so **cell
division increases**. Two examiner comments penalise the reverse route, and one scheme rejects
**framing from wild-type**.

## D. ras, the proto-oncogene the outcome names

**D1. ras is a small G protein.** The gene encodes a protein belonging to the **low-molecular weight
G-proteins**, which **bind guanine nucleotides, GTP and GDP**, and work in **signal transduction**.

**D2. What normal ras does.** Activated ras **relays signals from a growth factor receptor** to **a
series of protein kinases known as the phosphorylation cascade**, and **the last protein kinase
activates transcription of genes** whose products stimulate cell division. The controlling clause comes
next: the pathway is **normally activated only when a growth factor binds its receptor**. Normal ras is
a switch that something else has to press.

**D3. What the mutation changes.** A gain in function mutation **changes the three-dimensional
conformation** of the ras protein, so **GTP remains bonded as a ras-GTP complex** and the protein sits
in a **constant active state, even in the absence of growth factor**. The result is **increased
signalling and transcription**, so a **hyperactive ras protein** drives **excessive cell division**. The
switch is jammed on rather than pressed harder.

**D4. The chain has three links, because the specimen fixed the mark.** P2 Q3(a) gave two aligned DNA
sequences with their encoded H-ras peptides and asked for the effect on structure and function, for
three marks. The chain is **altered base sequence → altered codon → altered amino acid → altered R-group
interactions → altered conformation → GTP retained → constitutive activity → uncontrolled division**.
One vocabulary caution that two schemes reject: **GTP displaces GDP**, and nothing is phosphorylated at
that step.

## E. Cancer as a multi-step process

**E1. The definition turns on the phrase single cell lineage.** Tumourigenesis is **a multi-step process
characterised by accumulation of mutations in a single cell lineage**. That phrase is where the
somatic-against-germline distinction lives, and it corrects the error on page 1 of your chapter.

**E2. One mutation is not enough, and the process accelerates itself.** Cancer requires **gradual
accumulation of several independent mutations in cancer-critical genes**, and **every step corresponds
to one or a few mutations**. Each mutation **either enhances proliferation or decreases cell death**, so
its progeny **become the dominant clone**, and a larger clone is **a larger population at risk of the
next mutation**.

**E3. Three lines of evidence, asked for by name.** **Incidence of most cancers rises exponentially with
age.** There is a **delay between carcinogen exposure and appearance of the cancer**, the school's
example being that radiation at Hiroshima and Nagasaki **caused leukaemias only after 5 to 8 years**.
And **cancers develop progressively, from benign tumours to increasingly malignant cells**.

**E4. The outcome names three components; your chapter lists four.** LO 2(r) requires **accumulation of
mutations, angiogenesis and metastasis**. The school's fourth step, telomerase activation, has no
outcome behind it. Its list of **cancer-critical genes** is worth holding: **proto-oncogenes**, **tumour
suppressor genes** and **angiogenesis-activating protein genes**.

**E5. Expect this outcome as data rather than recall.** P2 Q3(e) gave three tumour-incidence curves in
mice, for **c-myc**, **H-ras** and **both together**, and asked for the differences. The two-oncogene
mice develop tumours fastest, which is the accumulation argument drawn as a graph. Schemes state that
**relevant supporting data must be quoted**, so give values with units, not the shape alone.

## F. Angiogenesis

**F1. What it is, and why a tumour must have it.** **Angiogenesis is the process by which new blood
vessels are formed**, **tightly regulated** and normally occurring **only when necessary, such as during
growth or repair**. A tumour proliferates faster, so it **requires more nutrients and oxygen** and
**produces more toxic waste products that must be removed**; it therefore **stimulates the formation of
new blood vessels**, a process also called **vascularisation**.

**F2. Two consequences, and the second is the bridge to metastasis.** The new vessels **supply nutrients
and oxygen and remove toxic waste products**, and they **provide the pathways for cancer cells to spread
to other sites**. Metastasis is downstream of angiogenesis, not parallel to it.

**F3. The mechanism is a balance, not a switch.** Cancer cells produce **angiogenesis-activating
proteins**, the two named being **vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)** and **fibroblast growth
factor (FGF)**. These **must overcome the effects of angiogenesis inhibitors** that normally restrain
vessel growth, and **a finely tuned balance between the activating proteins and the inhibitors
determines whether a tumour will induce the growth of new blood vessels**. Naming VEGF and FGF is
description; the balance sentence is the explanation.

---

## G. Metastasis

**G1. Invasion and metastasis are two different abilities.** **Invasion** is the acquired ability to
perform **direct migration and penetration into neighbouring tissues**. **Metastasis** is the ability
**to enter the circulatory system and travel to distant sites**, forming **secondary tumours called
metastases** that are **not physically connected to the primary tumour**. Invasion moves a cell next
door; metastasis moves it to another organ.

**G2. The three-step cascade.** Cancer cells **invade surrounding tissues and penetrate the walls of
lymphatic and blood vessels**, gaining access to the bloodstream. They are **transported by the
circulatory system throughout the body**. They then **leave the bloodstream and enter particular
organs**, where they **establish secondary tumours at distant sites**.

**G3. Without secondary tumours it is not metastasis.** A scheme records an answer as **rejected if
there is no reference to secondary sites or tumours**. Stopping at cells breaking through a vessel wall
describes invasion, and it is marked as invasion.

## H. Causative factors

**H1. The outcome names exactly four.** **Genetic, chemical carcinogens, ionising radiation and loss of
immunity.** The word including makes the school's extras, age and ultraviolet radiation and viruses,
additions rather than substitutes, so secure the four first. **Carcinogens** are **substances that cause
or promote the development of cancer**, and **most cancers develop only after repeated contact** with
them.

**H2. Every mechanism converges on the same last two links.** Whatever the agent, the route ends with a
mutation in a **cancer-critical gene**, then **uncontrollable cell division**, then **tumour
formation**. Write the agent-specific route first and the shared ending second, because that ending is
what turns a description into an explanation.

**H3. Chemical carcinogens work through DNA adducts.** **Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)** from
tobacco smoke reach the lungs and **spread via the bloodstream**, then **bind to DNA to form a physical
complex known as an adduct**. Unrepaired adducts **cause mistakes in DNA synthesis during normal cell
division**, introducing mutations. The link worth quoting is that **PAHs form adducts at several sites
on the p53 gene**, so the mutations **prevent production of functional p53 protein**. **Heterocyclic
amines (HCAs)** form the same way in grilled meat. **Nitrosamines, asbestos and benzene** instead **act
directly on cellular DNA**, the more potent ones only **after being metabolised to a more reactive
form**.

**H4. Ionising radiation works through free radicals.** **X-rays and gamma rays** produce **free radicals
of water, the OH radical**, which are **chemically very reactive** and **interact with cellular DNA to
produce double-stranded breaks**, giving **chromosomal rearrangements and deletions**. Keep this
separate from **ultraviolet radiation**, which is not ionising and instead produces **thymine dimers**,
a **covalent attachment between adjacent pyrimidines** in one strand.

**H5. Predisposition is inherited; cancer is not.** Inheriting **an oncogene or a mutant allele of a
tumour-suppressor gene** puts a person **one step closer to accumulating the necessary mutations**, and
**it is the predisposition to cancer, not the cancer itself, that is inherited**. **Further mutations
coupled with non-genetic factors** are still needed.

**H6. Loss of immunity removes the surveillance; it does not cause the mutation.** The immune system
**monitors for and eliminates the vast majority of incipient tumour cells**. Suppressed by **drugs,
viruses such as HIV**, or prolonged stress, it becomes **unable to detect and destroy cancerous cells**
through **absence or inadequate numbers of immune cells**, so **tumours may develop**. This factor acts
on cells that are already mutated, a different position in the chain from the other three.

# PART 2: STEM CELLS

## I. What defines a stem cell

**I1. Two features, and the exam asked for exactly two.** A stem cell is **an unspecialised cell** from
the embryo, fetus or adult that **has self-renewal capability by reproducing itself for long periods**,
or for adult stem cells **throughout the life of the organism**, and that **can differentiate**, giving
**specialised cells that make up the tissues and organs of the body**. The chapter adds a **large
nuclear to cytoplasmic ratio**. Specimen P3 Q3(a)(ii) asked for two defining features for two marks.

**I2. The phrase wanted is long-term self-renewal.** A scheme rejects **infinite** and **divides
indefinitely**, because the biology is about **remaining unspecialised while dividing**, not about a
division count.

**I3. Three general properties, which is LO 1(t) in list form.** A stem cell is **unspecialised**, having
**no tissue-specific structures** that let it perform specialised functions. It is **capable of dividing
and renewing itself for long periods**, so that if the daughters **continue to be unspecialised, like
the parent stem cells**, they show **long-term self-renewal by mitotic divisions**. And it **can give
rise to specialised cells** by **differentiation**.

---

## J. Differentiation, and the signals that trigger it

**J1. The internal signal is differential gene expression.** During differentiation **certain genes
become activated and other genes become inactivated in an intricately regulated fashion**, so that **a
differentiated cell develops specific structures and performs certain functions**. That mechanism sits
under everything else here and reaches back into Book 2: Specimen P1 Q8 asked why a bone marrow stem
cell placed in skin never makes a skin cell, and the correct answer is about which genes are switched
off, not about stem cells at all.

**J2. Three external signals, a ready-made three-mark list.** **Chemicals secreted by other cells**,
**physical contact with neighbouring cells**, and **certain molecules in the microenvironment**, the
microenvironment being **nutrients and growth factors in the surrounding fluid**.

**J3. Commitment is stable in the body and reversible in a laboratory.** Your chapter says both. Write
the version P1 Q8 tests: **once committed, cells usually do not switch commitment**, and **in a new
environment they will not change the type of cells they generate**.

---

## K. The three levels of potency

**K1. Potency is a range, defined by pathways.** **Stem cell potency is the range of cell types to which
a stem cell can give rise**, determined by **the number of possible pathways it can take** in its
subsequent development.

**K2. The three levels, each with the boundary that defines it.**

| | **Totipotency** | **Pluripotency** | **Multipotency** |
|---|---|---|---|
| The outcome's own example | **zygotic stem cells** | **embryonic stem cells** | **lymphoid and myeloid stem cells** |
| Can produce | **all cell types that make up an organism**: any adult body cell **AND any cell of the extra-embryonic membranes** | cells developing from **all three germ layers**: **mesoderm, endoderm, ectoderm** | **many cell lines within a specific tissue type**, a **limited number of cell types** |
| Defining boundary | none; these cells **can form whole organisms** | they **cannot make the extra-embryonic membranes** | one tissue only, so a blood stem cell makes **all the cells of the blood but not other types** |
| Where it exists | the **zygote** and the **first few cells** of the dividing embryo, **before the blastocyst stage** | the **inner cell mass of the blastocyst** | **adult tissues**, above all **bone marrow** |

**K3. The negative half of pluripotency is where the mark is.** Saying a pluripotent cell can become any
cell type is both wrong and unmarkable. **A pluripotent cell cannot form the extra-embryonic
membranes**, which is exactly why it **cannot on its own form an individual**, and that clause is the
whole totipotent-against-pluripotent distinction.

**K4. Name the lineage, never the category.** A scheme **rejects blood stem cell and adult stem cell as
not specific enough**, and another rejects **the ability to differentiate into white blood cells** where
the specific cell types are wanted. P3 Q3(a)(i) asked for the level of potency of lymphoid stem cells
for one mark, and the answer is **multipotent**.

---

## L. The normal function of embryonic stem cells

**L1. Their normal function is to build the body.** They come from the **inner cell mass (ICM) of the
blastocyst**, and **in time the ICM will form the embryo**. The ICM layers into **ectoderm** nearest the
amniotic cavity and **endoderm** nearer the blastocyst cavity, then **a third layer, the mesoderm, forms
in the middle**. That three-layered structure is the **primordial embryo, or gastrula**, and **once the
three primary germ layers form, the fates of many cells are determined**.

**L2. The defining properties worth holding.** An embryonic stem cell divides **symmetrically without
differentiating**, which the chapter itself glosses as **long-term self-renewal**, so use that phrase
rather than the chapter's word unlimited. It is **pluripotent** and so gives **all three primary germ
layers**, is **capable of developing into all fetal tissues**, and is **clonogenic**, meaning **one ESC
gives a colony of genetically identical cells**.

**L3. The outcome says normal, and in a living organism.** Both words are deliberate, so transplantation
and therapy score nothing against LO 1(u) however well written. The answer is development: these cells
**give rise to all three germ layers and hence to every tissue**, and **each layer retains stem cells as
the organism develops**.

---

## M. Blood stem cells and haematopoiesis

**M1. The haematopoietic stem cell is multipotent and lives in bone marrow.** **All the blood cells in
the adult human originate from multipotent HSCs located in the bone marrow**, and only **1 in 10,000 to
15,000 cells in the bone marrow** is one. **The primary role of HSCs is to replace blood cells**, and
they are **derived from the mesoderm** in the embryo.

**M2. The split into lineages is the part the outcome names.** **Erythroid and myeloid lineages are
derived from the common myeloid progenitor cell. The lymphoid lineage is derived from the common
lymphoid progenitor cell.** Learn that sentence as printed, because your chapter contradicts it nine
pages earlier.

- **Lymphoid lineage.** It produces **lymphocytes**, including **the B and T lymphocytes**, which
  **function for specific adaptive immune responses**. **B lymphocytes make antibodies** and **develop
  in the bone marrow**; **T lymphocytes attack virus-infected cells, foreign cells and cancer cells**
  and **develop in the thymus**.
- **Myeloid lineage.** It **gives rise to the rest of the white blood cells**: **eosinophils,
  neutrophils and basophils, collectively known as granulocytes**, plus **mast cells and monocytes**.
  They provide **defence against pathogens by phagocytosis**, **destruction of cancer cells** arising in
  the body, and removal of **debris from dead or injured cells during wound healing**.
- **Erythroid lineage.** It yields **erythrocytes**, which **transport oxygen and carbon dioxide**, and
  **megakaryocytes**, which give **platelets** for **blood clotting**. This third lineage is the school's
  extra, since the outcome names only lymphoid and myeloid.

**M3. The normal function is continuous replacement, scaled to demand.** Blood cells are short-lived, so
the HSC population replaces them continuously, and **the numbers of each type are regulated according to
physiological need**. The two worked examples to write are that **loss of blood results in an increase
in red blood cell production** and that **infection leads to an increase in lymphocytes and other white
blood cells**.

## N. How a stem cell population sustains itself

**N1. Symmetric and asymmetric division, both by mitosis.** **Symmetric division produces two
genetically identical cells**, identical to each other and to the parental cell. **Asymmetric division
produces one stem cell and one progenitor cell** with limited self-renewal potential, and it is
**preceded by localisation of regulatory molecules** or **differential segregation of cell membrane
proteins**, so the daughters **differ from birth and have different fates**.

**N2. The significance line is a clean two-mark answer.** **Mitotic division of stem cells preserves a
population of undifferentiated cells while steadily producing a stream of differentiating cells.** That
is why a tissue can supply new cells for a lifetime without exhausting its stem cells.

**N3. The niche decides which division happens.** Self-renewal and differentiation are **tightly
regulated by the cells and molecules in their immediate environment, the niche that they inhabit**. In
haematopoiesis **the niche is provided by the bone marrow stromal cells**, whose **secreted signal
proteins** and **haematopoietic growth factors** maintain stem-cell proliferation. This answers any
question about what controls self-renewal against differentiation, and it runs on the reception and
transduction machinery from Cluster 1.

**N4. The commitment cascade, two lines only.** **Pluripotent stem cell → committed stem cell →
progenitor (precursor) cell → differentiated cell.** A progenitor is **partly differentiated**,
**committed to a limited number of pathways of differentiation**, and **more limited in developmental
pathways than a multipotent stem cell**.

## Q. Errors in your notes, and one genuine ambiguity

**Three errors. Learn the corrected version before you encode the printed one.**

1. **Chapter 3.4 page 14 says embryonic stem cells are totipotent**, claiming each cell of the early
   blastocyst **is capable by itself of developing into a healthy individual**. **Embryonic stem cells
   are pluripotent**, and pages 8, 13 and 16 of the same chapter say so correctly: they **make all three
   germ layers** and **cannot make the extra-embryonic membranes**, which is why they cannot alone form
   an individual. This inverts the exact distinction LO 1(t) exists to test, so it is the single most
   dangerous error in Book 3.
2. **Chapter 3.4 page 11 says the myeloid progenitor cell can generate all the different types of blood
   cells.** It cannot: **a common myeloid progenitor cannot give rise to lymphocytes**. Page 20 of the
   same chapter gets it right, that **the lymphoid lineage is derived from the common lymphoid
   progenitor cell**. Page 20 is correct. This one is live, because **Specimen Paper 3 Question 3 opens
   on lymphoid stem cells**.
3. **Chapter 3.3 page 1 says cancer incidence rises through accumulation of mutations from one
   generation to the next.** Mutations accumulate **within one individual's somatic cell lineage over a
   lifetime**, and **somatic mutations are not passed to offspring**; pages 19 and 30 get it right.
   Separately, **the colorectal case study says four genes in its text and at least five in its own
   figure caption**, and the walkthrough that follows names four. It is marked FYI, so quote no number
   from it.

**One genuine ambiguity, and the resolution is worth marks.** Page 13 establishes that **mutated tumour
suppressor genes act in a recessive manner**, and page 27 then calls **BRCA1 and BRCA2**, which are
tumour suppressor genes, **inherited in a dominant manner**. No bridge is given. `[INFERRED, no mark
scheme in the corpus states this]` Both hold, at different levels. **At the level of the cell** the loss
is **recessive**, because **one working allele still makes enough protein**, so **both copies must be
lost** before that cell loses control. **At the level of the organism** the predisposition is inherited
as a **dominant**, because a person who **inherits one faulty copy in every cell needs only one further
somatic hit** rather than two, which sharply raises lifetime risk. If a question hands you the apparent
contradiction, name the level for each half.
