### Question 1

**(a)** *Explain how the mutation shown in Figure 1.1 may change the structure and function of the ras protein.* **[3]** [INFERRED: the specimen papers ship without a mark scheme; the wording below is taken from the school keys that mark the identical H-ras question]

**1.** A **substitution mutation has changed the base sequence of the twelfth codon from GGC to GTC**, that is a **single base substitution of T for G**.

**2.** This **changes the amino acid encoded at that position from glycine to valine**, so the **primary structure of the ras protein is altered**.

**3.** Because **glycine and valine have different R groups**, the **interactions between R groups change, so different hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges and hydrophobic interactions form**, and the **three-dimensional conformation of the protein is altered**.

**4.** The **ras protein is a G protein that switches itself off by hydrolysing GTP**, so an **altered conformation leaves it permanently active / constitutively active**, and the **cell is continuously stimulated to divide even in the absence of a growth factor**.

**5.** Start at the **gene**. A scheme elsewhere in this file rejects an answer that opens at the amino acid and never mentions the DNA.

**(b)** *Describe the differences between gain of function and loss of function mutations.* **[2]** [INFERRED: no official key]

**1.** A **gain of function mutation affects a proto-oncogene and converts it into an oncogene**, whereas a **loss of function mutation affects a tumour suppressor gene such as p53**.

**2.** A **gain of function mutation makes the gene product permanently active or overproduced, so the cell is continuously stimulated to divide**, whereas a **loss of function mutation produces a non-functional protein, so the cell cycle is no longer arrested at a checkpoint, damaged DNA is no longer repaired and apoptosis is no longer triggered**.

**3.** A **gain of function mutation behaves like a dominant allele, so one mutated allele is enough**, whereas a **loss of function mutation behaves like a recessive allele, so both alleles must be inactivated**.

**4.** State **both sides** of each difference. A one-sided answer about oncogenes only scores nothing on a two-mark contrast.

**(c)** *State two factors that can cause mutations.* **[2]** [INFERRED: no official key]

**1.** **Chemical carcinogens**, for example **tar in tobacco smoke, benzo[a]pyrene or other polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons**.

**2.** **Ionising radiation**, for example **X-rays or gamma rays**.

**3.** Also credited: **ultraviolet radiation**; **viral infection**; **spontaneous errors during DNA replication**.

**(d)** *Describe how the c-myc mutation differs from the H-ras mutation.* **[2]** [INFERRED: no official key]

**1.** The **H-ras mutation is a gene mutation, a single base substitution within the gene itself**, whereas the **c-myc mutation is a chromosomal aberration, a translocation of the whole gene from chromosome 8 to chromosome 14**.

**2.** The **H-ras mutation changes the amino acid sequence of the protein, so an altered ras protein is made**, whereas the **c-myc mutation does not change the c-myc protein at all; it changes the amount made, because the gene is now beside a highly active promoter and is over-expressed**.

**(e)** *Describe differences between the effects of c-myc, H-ras and a combination of the two.* **[3]** [INFERRED: no official key]

**1.** **All three groups eventually develop tumours**, so the percentage of mice without tumours falls to zero in every case.

**2.** **H-ras alone causes tumours earlier than c-myc alone**, that is the H-ras curve begins to fall at a younger age and reaches zero sooner.

**3.** The **combination of c-myc and H-ras causes tumours earlier and faster than either oncogene alone**, and the combined curve reaches zero well before either single-oncogene curve.

**4.** The combination is **more than additive**, which is the evidence that **cancer is a multi-step process requiring an accumulation of mutations in one cell lineage** rather than a single event.

**5.** Quote figures off the graph. The command word is *describe* with reference to a figure, so read ages in days off the x-axis for at least one comparison.

**Source:** 9477 Specimen P2 Q3 in full, all five parts verbatim from the question paper, worth **12 marks** on the real paper and kept at 12 here. Figures 1.1, 1.2 and 1.3 are the specimen's own Figures 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3, extracted from the paper. The specimen ships without a mark scheme, so every marking point is inferred; the ras chain in (a) is worded from the school keys for the identical H-ras and K-ras questions in this file, and the gain-against-loss contrast in (b) is worded from the SAJC Prelim 2025 P2 Q8a key.
**Archetype:** sequence-to-protein reasoning, then a structural contrast between two routes to the same oncogene, then data interpretation off a survival-style curve. This is the single most exam-representative question in the drill because it is the actual specimen question for this topic.
**Trap:** starting the answer at the amino acid instead of at the base sequence; saying the c-myc protein is altered when only its quantity is; describing the three curves without quoting an age; treating the combination result as simply "faster" without saying what it demonstrates about multi-step development.
**LOs:** 2(q) gain in function mutation of proto-oncogenes including ras, and the contrast with loss of function mutation of tumour suppressor genes; 2(p) causative factors; 2(r) multi-step development; reaches back to Book 2 LO 2(k) for substitution and translocation, and to Book 1 for R group interactions and three-dimensional conformation.
**Concepts fused:** (1) reading a substitution off two aligned base sequences; (2) base sequence to amino acid sequence to conformation to function; (3) ras as a constitutively active G protein; (4) gain of function as dominant, one allele; (5) loss of function as recessive, two alleles; (6) chemical carcinogens and ionising radiation as mutagens; (7) gene mutation against chromosomal aberration; (8) translocation placing a gene under a new promoter; (9) over-expression without a change in protein sequence; (10) reading a time-to-event curve; (11) two oncogenes together acting more than additively; (12) accumulation of mutations in one cell lineage.

### Question 2

**(a)** *What is a normal function of tumour suppressor genes?* **[1]**

**1.** The key is **C**, to promote the death of cells with DNA damage. [INFERRED: the specimen papers ship without a mark scheme]

**(b)** *Explain your answer in (a), taking each of the four options in turn.* **[3]** [INFERRED: no official key]

**1.** **C is correct.** Tumour suppressor genes such as **p53** code for proteins that **halt the cell cycle at a checkpoint so that damaged DNA can be repaired, and trigger apoptosis if the damage cannot be repaired**, which prevents damaged cells from dividing and passing on mutations.

**2.** **A is wrong** because it is the **opposite** of their function. Tumour suppressor genes **regulate** a checkpoint; it is their **loss** that dysregulates one.

**3.** **B is wrong** because tumour suppressor proteins **cannot stop mutations happening**. They act **after** the damage has occurred, by arresting the cycle, repairing the DNA or killing the cell.

**4.** **D is wrong** because **stimulating oncogene activity would promote uncontrolled division**, which is the job of a mutated proto-oncogene, not of a tumour suppressor gene.

**(c)** *State if the RB1 gene is a tumour suppressor gene or an oncogene. Explain your answer.* **[2]**

**1.** For the state mark, **RB1 is a tumour suppressor gene**.

**2.** For the explanation, **mutated tumour suppressor alleles behave like recessive alleles, so two mutated alleles are required to produce non-functional protein products that cannot inhibit the cell cycle, repair DNA or trigger apoptosis**.

**3.** By contrast, **oncogenes behave like dominant alleles, so the presence of one mutated allele is enough to produce an abnormal protein which overstimulates cell division**.

**(d)** *Explain how an inherited mutated copy of BRCA2 can increase the risk of uncontrolled cell division.* **[2]**

**1.** There is reference to the idea of **losing the second copy / allele of the BRCA2 gene, resulting in loss of function mutation of the BRCA2 gene**.

**2.** **Thus no functional BRCA2 protein, resulting in dysregulation of the cell cycle / damaged DNA not repaired, leading to uncontrolled cell division.**

**3.** **Ignore** an answer that only states BRCA2 is a tumour suppressor gene without explanation. **Reject** referring to a loss of function mutation as a single mutation event. **Reject** referring to alleles as genes. **Reject** little, low amounts or underproduction of BRCA2 protein, since both alleles are lost. **Reject** an answer based on the consequence of inheriting one mutated copy alone.

**4.** The examiner's note, verbatim: **students need to be aware of when to use "allele" and "gene", and that "loss of function mutation" does not equate to a single mutation event. Only when 2 copies / 2 alleles of the BRCA2 gene is inactivated / mutated / lost, that is considered a loss-of-function mutation. Many students made the mistake again by explaining the function of BRCA2 protein, answering the question in reverse argument, which should be avoided.**

**(e)** *State the number of p53 alleles an African elephant has in its genome.* **[1]**

**1.** **40**, that is **20 genes multiplied by 2 alleles per gene**.

**(f)** *Suggest how multiple copies of the p53 genes may help the African elephants escape cancer.* **[2]**

**1.** The presence of multiple copies **lowers the chance of an absence of functional p53 protein due to loss of function mutations in the p53 gene**, and provides **multiple templates for transcription to make more copies of functional p53 protein**.

**2.** It **increases the likelihood of arresting the cell cycle and repairing DNA damage in cells, and gives higher rates of p53-mediated apoptosis compared to other mammals in response to DNA damage**.

**3.** This **reduces the likelihood of accumulation of mutations in other critical genes that could lead to cancer development**.

SAJC Prelim 2025 P2 Q8a, stem and key verbatim (c). School (HCI) Prelim 2025 P3 Q2a, stem and key verbatim including every reject and the examiner's note word for word (d), with the epigenetics clause of the original preamble dropped because this cluster does not cover it. SAJC Prelim 2023 P2 Q8c, stem and key verbatim (e), with the last sentence of the original preamble dropped as it is not needed for the count. SAJC Prelim 2023 P2 Q8d, stem and key verbatim (f).
**Archetype:** definition-level precision on what a tumour suppressor gene does, then the two-hit logic applied three times over, on RB1, on BRCA2 and on a species with twenty copies.
**Trap:** answering (d) by describing what BRCA2 protein normally does, which the examiner names as the commonest error and which the key rejects as a reverse argument; saying "gene" where the mark scheme demands "allele"; treating a loss of function mutation as one event; answering (e) with 20 instead of 40.
**LOs:** 2(q) loss of function mutation of tumour suppressor genes including p53, set against gain in function mutation of proto-oncogenes; reaches back to Book 2 for allele against gene and for gene dosage.
**Concepts fused:** (1) the normal job of a tumour suppressor protein; (2) checkpoint arrest, DNA repair and apoptosis as three separate outputs of that job; (3) tumour suppressor alleles as recessive; (4) oncogenes as dominant; (5) the two-hit requirement; (6) inheriting the first hit; (7) allele against gene as examined vocabulary; (8) forward argument against reverse argument; (9) allele counting in a diploid genome; (10) gene dosage as protection; (11) more templates giving more functional protein; (12) fewer accumulated mutations giving less cancer.

### Question 3

**(a)** *Explain four reasons why gene mutations may not always lead to cancer formation.* **[4]**

**1.** **In a diploid organism, both copies of the tumour suppressor gene must be mutated so that no functional gene product can be produced, to result in abnormal cell proliferation.** Put the other way, **if mutation occurs only in one copy of the tumour suppressor gene, the other copy is able to code for sufficient functional proteins to prevent the cell from dividing abnormally.**

**2.** **Accumulation of mutations in several genes involved in the control of the cell cycle is required in a single cell lineage before the cell becomes cancerous.**

**3.** **Mutations may have occurred within introns, which are excised from the primary RNA transcript, hence are non-coding.**

**4.** **The changed codon might still code for the same amino acid due to the degeneracy of the genetic code.**

**5.** **The changed codon might code for a different amino acid with a similar R group / chemical property, hence not affecting protein structure.**

**6.** **The amino acid affected by the mutation may not serve a critical role in the protein in which it is found**, or **the mutations occurred in genes that do not regulate cell division**.

**7.** Any valid point, for example that **named checkpoints arrest the cell cycle when there are DNA mutations or when DNA cannot be repaired**.

**(b)** *Suggest the differences in the cell cycle of a cancer cell compared with that of a normal cell of the same type.* **[2]**

**1.** The **cell cycle is shorter / interphase is shorter / division is more frequent**. **Reject "the cell cycle is irregular" and "inappropriate".**

**2.** **Cell cycle checkpoints are not controlled / are unregulated**, giving **uncontrolled cell division**.

**3.** The **cell cannot be stimulated to undergo apoptosis, even when errors occur during the cell cycle**.

**(c)** *Suggest why cancer cells of different tumours may exhibit different levels of resistance to the same drug.* **[2]**

**1.** **Different tumour cells exhibit variation, due to at least one random mutation to certain genes, or differential gene expression, or gene amplification.**

**2.** This leads to the **formation of new alleles coding for more functional proteins**, which may be **transporter proteins that pump out drugs**, **tubulin with a non-complementary binding site where the drug usually binds**, **enzymes that hydrolyse the drug**, or **inhibitors that bind to the drugs and render them ineffective**.

**3.** Any other valid protein is credited, for example a **cell surface receptor** or a **protein that regulates the cell cycle**.

**Source:** DHS Prelim 2023 P2 Q6a, stem and key verbatim (a); SAJC Prelim 2017 P3 Q3c(i), stem and key verbatim including the reject (b); CJC Prelim 2018 P2 Q2d(ii), stem and key verbatim (c).
**Archetype:** the reverse question. Every other cancer question in this drill asks how a mutation causes cancer; this one asks why it usually does not, which is where the degeneracy of the code, the position of introns and the two-hit requirement all become the answer.
**Trap:** giving four versions of the same reason; calling the cancer cell cycle "irregular", which the key rejects by name; explaining drug resistance by the drug rather than by variation between tumour cell populations.
**LOs:** 2(q) and 2(r) read backwards; reaches back to Book 2 LOs on silent and neutral mutations, introns, and the degeneracy of the genetic code.
**Concepts fused:** (1) the two-hit requirement as a brake on cancer; (2) accumulation in one cell lineage as a second brake; (3) introns as non-coding; (4) degeneracy giving silent mutations; (5) similar R groups giving neutral mutations; (6) non-critical residues; (7) genes that have nothing to do with division; (8) shorter interphase in a cancer cell; (9) unregulated checkpoints; (10) failure of apoptosis; (11) variation between tumour populations; (12) named resistance mechanisms at protein level.
