### Question 1

**(a)** *How the change in Figure 1.1 leaves the cell dividing with no growth factor bound.* **[7]**

**1.** Start at the gene, not at the protein. There is a **substitution of a single base** in the *H-ras* gene: the **G in the twelfth codon GGC is replaced by T to give GTC**. A scheme in this course rejects the opening marking points where an answer states only that an amino acid has changed **without the idea that it occurred in the DNA / gene**, so name the base and the codon first.

**2.** Because a base has been **replaced rather than added or removed**, the **reading frame is not shifted** and **at most one codon and one amino acid change**. This is a **missense mutation**: the codon now codes for **valine instead of glycine**, so **one amino acid in the primary structure of the ras protein is changed** and every other residue is untouched. R: calling this a frameshift, since **no base has been added or removed, so the frame cannot shift**.

**3.** **Valine has a much larger, non-polar (hydrophobic) R group than glycine, whose R group is a single hydrogen atom.** Both halves of that are needed; naming the two amino acids without their R group properties is half a point. Because the **primary structure has changed**, the **R groups now form different interactions** where they sit, so **hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds** form in different places and the polypeptide **folds into a different three-dimensional conformation**. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 1]

**4.** The residue that has changed lies **in the binding site of the ras protein**, the site that holds the guanine nucleotide, so the **altered conformation alters that binding site** and hence **alters the function of the protein**. Where a substitution changes what a protein does, the changed residue is part of the **active site or binding site**; a residue buried away from it usually gives a **neutral** mutation with **no detectable change in function**.

**5.** What normal ras does, for the contrast. The *ras* gene encodes a protein belonging to the **low-molecular weight G-proteins**, which **bind guanine nucleotides, GTP and GDP**, and work in **signal transduction**. Activated ras **relays signals from a growth factor receptor** to **a series of protein kinases known as the phosphorylation cascade**, and **the last protein kinase activates transcription of genes** whose products stimulate cell division. The controlling clause, which is where the stem's no-growth-factor condition is answered, is that the pathway is **normally activated only when a growth factor binds its receptor**. Normal ras is a switch that something else has to press. [CROSS-CLUSTER, Cluster 1]

**6.** In the switching step, **GTP displaces GDP**, and two schemes in this course carry an explicit reject for saying the **GDP is phosphorylated to become GTP**; nothing is phosphorylated at that step. The altered conformation means **GTP remains bonded as a ras-GTP complex**, because the protein **can no longer hydrolyse its bound GTP to GDP**, so the **intrinsic GTPase activity** that normally shuts the G protein off is lost. The protein therefore sits in a **constant active state, even in the absence of growth factor**, which is a **hyperactive ras protein**. The switch is jammed on rather than pressed harder. [CROSS-CLUSTER, Cluster 1]

**7.** The consequence runs forwards: **increased signalling and transcription** of the genes whose products stimulate division, so **excessive cell division** follows with **no growth factor bound to any receptor**. The gene is now an **oncogene**, produced from a **proto-oncogene** by a **gain in function mutation**, and the gene product is **hyperactive** rather than **produced in excess**. **One copy is sufficient**, because **a mutation of one of the two copies of the proto-oncogene into an oncogene is sufficient to cause abnormal cell proliferation** and **the mutation results in the formation of a dominant allele** at the level of the cell. Write the chain in this order: **altered base sequence, altered codon, altered amino acid, altered R-group interactions, altered three-dimensional conformation, GTP retained, constitutive activity, uncontrolled cell division.** Two examiner comments penalise **answering in the reverse argument**, and one scheme records **Reject: framing from wild-type**. R: **excessive or uncontrolled cell growth** where **cell division** is meant.

**(b)** *The two routes compared, and which a protein gel could tell apart.* **[4]**

**1.** The *c-myc* change is a **chromosome mutation, a translocation, in which a whole gene is moved from chromosome 8 to chromosome 14**, and chromosomes 8 and 14 are **non-homologous**. The *H-ras* change is a **gene mutation, the substitution of a single base within the gene**. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 2]

**2.** The *c-myc* translocation **does not change the base sequence of the gene, nor the amino acid sequence of its protein**. It changes only **where the gene sits**: the gene has been **placed under the control of enhancers or of a more active promoter**, so it is **over-transcribed** and there is **overproduction of the c-myc protein**. What changes is the **amount of protein produced**. The *H-ras* substitution instead **changes one amino acid and therefore the structure and the function of the protein**, while the **quantity produced stays the same**. Both are **gain in function mutations converting a proto-oncogene into an oncogene**, and the outcome's own two routes are that the gene product is either **over-expressed** or a **hyperactive protein**. This pair is one of each. [CROSS-CLUSTER, Book 2]

**3.** The judgement. A gel showing amount and size **can detect the c-myc route**, because the amount of protein in the cancerous cells is **greater** than in the normal cells. The same gel **cannot detect the H-ras route**: the mutant ras protein is **the same length, since one codon has been swapped for another and no stop codon has been created**, and it is **produced in the same quantity**, so the band appears **at the same height and with the same intensity in both lanes**. Detecting it needs the **base sequence** or the **activity** of the protein, not its amount.

**4.** Why *c-myc* over-production drives division at all: the *c-myc* protein is a **transcription factor**, and transcription factors are one of the classes of proto-oncogene product, alongside **growth factors**, **growth factor receptors** and **protein kinases**. More of it means **a higher rate of transcription** of the genes it controls, and hence **more mitosis**.

### Question 2

**(a)** *Whose cell is dividing uncontrollably now, and why.* **[7]**

**1.** The judgement first: **the woman's colon cell is dividing uncontrollably; the man's cells are not.** Everything below is the reason.

**2.** A **proto-oncogene** is a normal gene that **encodes a protein which stimulates normal cell division**. A **tumour suppressor gene** is a normal gene that **encodes a protein which inhibits uncontrolled cell division**. The school's analogy fixes the direction: the proto-oncogene is the accelerator, the tumour suppressor the brake pedal.

**3.** A **gain in function mutation** of a proto-oncogene gives a product that is **hyperactive or produced in excess**, and **a mutation of one of the two copies is sufficient to cause abnormal cell proliferation**, because **the mutation results in the formation of a dominant allele**. The woman's one mutated *ras* copy is therefore already enough. A **loss of function mutation** of a tumour suppressor gives a product that is **non-functional or absent**, and **both copies of the tumour suppressor gene must be mutated so that no functional gene product can be produced**, because **the mutation results in the formation of a recessive allele**. The reason both copies are needed is that **one intact copy still makes sufficient normal gene product** to inhibit the cell cycle, so a cell with **one working p53 allele is regulated normally**.

**4.** Say **allele**, not gene, throughout this comparison, because a scheme records **R: referring to alleles as genes**. And a loss of function mutation is **at least two mutation events**, not one, because a scheme records **R: referring to loss of function mutation as a single mutation event**.

**5.** What normal p53 does, because 2(q) names it. p53 is **critical for its triple involvement in cell cycle control, in apoptosis, and in maintenance of genetic stability**, which is why it is called the **Guardian of the Genome**, and it is **mutated in about half of all human cancers**. It is a **transcription factor** which **binds to DNA to trigger transcription of genes involved in cell cycle inhibition**, and **high levels are only seen when its DNA is damaged**, so it is a stress sensor, not a constant brake. Its three mechanisms are that it **activates DNA repair proteins**; that it **arrests growth at the G1/S regulation point** on recognising damage, so **the damaged DNA is not replicated**, by **promoting transcription of the p21 gene** whose **p21 protein stops the cell cycle by binding proteins involved in cell cycle progression**; and that it **initiates apoptosis if the damage proves irreparable**, **activating suicide genes**. [CROSS-CLUSTER, Cluster 2 for the G1/S regulation point]

**6.** Run the p53 answer forwards, from the mutation. Where **both** copies are lost there is **no functional p53 protein**, so **the cell cycle is not inhibited**, so **cells with damaged DNA are no longer stopped at the checkpoint and continue to divide**, so **further mutations accumulate** and **cell division increases**. Two examiner comments penalise the reverse route and one scheme records **Reject: framing from wild-type**. What has happened to the woman is that **one gain-in-function mutation in one copy of ras** in **one somatic cell** gives a **hyperactive ras protein**, so that cell **divides in the absence of growth factors** and **passes the damaged genes on to its daughters**, giving **a clone of altered cells**. What has happened to the man is that every cell carries **one mutated and one normal p53 allele**, so **every cell still makes enough functional p53** and **no cell has yet lost control**; he is **one step closer to accumulating the necessary mutations**, not further along than that.

**7.** Two qualifications that keep the judgement honest. Uncontrolled is not the same as fast: the crucial issue is **the rate of cell division against the rate of cell loss**, and normal tissue holds the two **in balance, so no net accumulation** occurs, while in cancer division **far exceeds** loss. Schemes reject **excessive or uncontrolled cell growth** where **cell division** is meant, and reject **cell cycle is irregular or inappropriate**. And **one mutation is not enough for cancer**: cancer requires **gradual accumulation of several independent mutations in cancer-critical genes**, so the woman's cell has **begun** the process, and describing her as having cancer today would be wrong.

**(b)** *Three generations of cancers, from a mutation that is recessive inside a cell.* **[4]**

**1.** Both statements hold, at **different levels**, and the answer must name the level for each half. [INFERRED, no scheme in the corpus states the bridge, although the school's own chapter asserts both halves without joining them]

**2.** **At the level of the cell** the loss is **recessive**, because **one working allele still makes enough protein**, so **both copies must be lost** before that cell loses control.

**3.** **At the level of the organism** the predisposition is inherited as a **dominant**, because a person who **inherits one faulty copy in every cell needs only one further somatic hit** rather than two, which sharply raises lifetime risk and brings the cancers forward in age. That second hit is a **mutation in the other normal allele**, described in the schemes as **loss of heterozygosity**, and it **leads to non-functional p53 protein which is unable to halt the cell cycle, activate DNA repair or induce apoptosis**.

**4.** The mutation is in **germline cells**, which are **involved in the formation of gametes**, so it is **passed down to the progeny in the process of sexual reproduction**; a **somatic mutation would not be passed down**. What has been passed down, in the outcome's own terms, is that **it is the predisposition to cancer, not the cancer itself, that is inherited**, and **further mutations coupled with non-genetic factors** are still needed. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 2]

### Question 3

**(a)** *The three groups in Figure 3.1, and what the graph says about how a cancer arises.* **[7]**

**1.** Quote the data. Schemes in this course state that **relevant supporting data must be quoted** and mark answers as **rejected without the quote**, and 13.3 per cent of structured parts in this Book open with a with-reference-to instruction. Give values with units, not the shape alone.

**2.** **The combination of c-myc and H-ras causes tumours earliest and in all of the mice.** The percentage without tumours **begins to fall at about 20 days**, has fallen to **30 per cent by 50 days**, and reaches **0 per cent by about 180 days**, so **every mouse has a tumour**.

**3.** With **H-ras alone**, tumours appear from about **25 days** and the fall is much more gradual, leaving about **43 per cent of the mice tumour-free at 200 days**. With **c-myc alone**, tumours appear **latest, at about 65 days**, and the fall is slowest, leaving the largest proportion, about **67 per cent, still tumour-free at 200 days**. So **at every age the percentage without tumours is lowest for the combination and highest for c-myc alone**, and the two single-oncogene curves sit between them in the order H-ras then c-myc.

**4.** The effect of the two together is **far greater than the effect of either alone, and greater than the sum of the two**: they **act synergistically**. Note that the combination curve reaches 0 per cent while neither single curve falls below 43 per cent.

**5.** What that shows about how a cancer arises. Tumourigenesis is **a multi-step process characterised by accumulation of mutations in a single cell lineage**, and the phrase **single cell lineage** is where the mark sits. Cancer requires **gradual accumulation of several independent mutations in cancer-critical genes**, and **every step corresponds to one or a few mutations**. One mutation is not enough, which is why even the single-oncogene mice mostly stay tumour-free.

**6.** Each mutation **either enhances proliferation or decreases cell death**, so the progeny of that cell **become the dominant clone**, and a larger clone is **a larger population at risk of the next mutation**. The process accelerates itself, which is why the combination curve falls steeply rather than twice as fast. Time is the variable on the x-axis, and time is what accumulation costs: a mouse carrying **two** oncogenes starts **two steps** along the sequence, so **fewer further mutations are needed** and they are reached **sooner**.

**7.** Two things worth adding. Both genes here are **proto-oncogenes converted to oncogenes by gain in function mutation**, so the graph shows accumulation across **two hits of the same class**, whereas a real human cancer also needs **loss of function mutations in both copies of several tumour suppressor genes**. And the honest limit of the reading is that the graph shows **when tumours appeared, not how many mutations each mouse acquired**, so the accumulation model is the **explanation the data are consistent with**, not something the graph measures directly. R: describing the trend in words with no values quoted. R: reading the curves as percentage of mice **with** tumours; the axis is **without**.

**(b)** *The third observation, and what the three have in common.* **[3]**

### Question 4

**(a)** *Why one tumour stops at 1 mm and the other reaches the liver.* **[7]**

**1.** The first tumour is **benign** and the second **malignant**. **Benign tumours have few genetic mutations, do not cause serious health problems, and could be completely removed by surgery**, which is what happened to the first. **Malignant tumours are invasive and impair functions of organs**, and the individual **is considered to have cancer**, so **systemic treatment** is needed alongside surgery.

**2.** Why a tumour cannot grow past about a millimetre without help. A tumour proliferating faster than the tissue around it **requires more nutrients and oxygen** and **produces more toxic waste products that must be removed**, and diffusion alone cannot supply a mass beyond that size.

**3.** **Angiogenesis is the process by which new blood vessels are formed.** It is **tightly regulated** and normally occurs **only when necessary, such as during growth or repair**. The second tumour **stimulates the formation of new blood vessels**, also called **vascularisation**, and Figure 4.1 shows this directly as **new blood vessels stimulated to grow to supply cancer cells with a blood supply**.

**4.** The mechanism is a **balance, not a switch**. Cancer cells produce **angiogenesis-activating proteins**, the two named being **vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)** and **fibroblast growth factor (FGF)**, and these **must overcome the effects of angiogenesis inhibitors**. **A finely tuned balance between the activating proteins and the inhibitors determines whether a tumour will induce the growth of new blood vessels.** Naming VEGF and FGF is description; the balance sentence is the explanation, and it is what the first tumour has failed to tip. R: naming VEGF and FGF without the balance against inhibitors.

**5.** The new vessels do two things. They **supply nutrients and oxygen and remove toxic waste products**, which is what lets the second tumour reach 4 cm. And they **provide the pathways for cancer cells to spread to other sites**. **Metastasis is downstream of angiogenesis, not parallel to it.** R: treating angiogenesis and metastasis as parallel events rather than the first enabling the second.

**6.** Separate the two abilities the second tumour has acquired. **Invasion** is **direct migration and penetration into neighbouring tissues**, which Figure 4.1 labels as **cancer cells invade into local tissue**. **Metastasis** is the ability **to enter the circulatory system and travel to distant sites**, forming **secondary tumours called metastases** that are **not physically connected to the primary tumour**. Invasion moves a cell next door; metastasis moves it to another organ. The three-step cascade, which is what the liver deposit records, is that cancer cells **invade surrounding tissues and penetrate the walls of lymphatic and blood vessels**, are **transported by the circulatory system throughout the body**, then **leave the bloodstream and enter particular organs**, where they **establish secondary tumours at distant sites**. The liver growth is therefore a **secondary tumour**, and its cells **carry the mutations of the breast tumour**, which is how it is known to be a metastasis rather than a second primary. A scheme records an answer as **rejected if there is no reference to secondary sites or tumours**; stopping at cells breaking through a vessel wall describes **invasion**, and it is marked as invasion.

**7.** What the second tumour has additionally lost. Tumour suppressor proteins **maintain cell adhesion**, since **metastasis suppressors** hold **cell-to-cell adhesion** and **anchorage to the extracellular matrix**, so losing them lets cells **break off from the primary tumour**, as Figure 4.1 labels. The same cells have also escaped **anchorage dependence**, since normal cells **must contact a substratum** before they proliferate while cancer cells **proliferate in suspension**, which is what survival in the bloodstream requires. So the difference is an **accumulation of further mutations in the second lineage**: enough to tip the angiogenic balance, and enough to lose adhesion and anchorage dependence. The first tumour stopped because it acquired **neither**.

**(b)** *A drug blocking the VEGF receptor, and its limitation.* **[4]**

**1.** VEGF is a **ligand** and the protein it binds on the endothelial cell is a **receptor**. Binding depends on the ligand having a **specific three-dimensional conformation complementary** to the receptor's **binding site**. Call it a **binding site**, never an active site, because **a receptor is not an enzyme**; an examiner comment in this course penalises exactly that error. [CROSS-CLUSTER, Cluster 1]

**2.** With the binding site occupied by the drug, **no ligand-receptor complex forms**, so the receptor **does not undergo the conformational change** that would trigger transduction, and **no signal is transduced** into the endothelial cell. [CROSS-CLUSTER, Cluster 1]

**3.** So the endothelial cells are **not stimulated to divide and form new vessels**, the balance tips back **towards the inhibitors**, and the 4 cm tumour is **deprived of its supply of nutrients and oxygen** and cannot remove **toxic waste products**, so its growth **slows or stops** and it may shrink. The 1 mm tumour is **unaffected**, because it **never induced angiogenesis in the first place** and is already living within diffusion distance. Blocking a process a tumour was not using changes nothing.

**4.** The limitations, any one of which earns the mark. The cells already **established as secondary tumours in the liver** are **not removed** by the drug, which can only restrict their further growth once they too need a blood supply, so metastasis that has already happened cannot be undone by cutting off angiogenesis. Angiogenesis is also a **normal process occurring during growth or repair**, so blocking it **impairs wound healing and normal tissue repair**. And the tumour may **acquire further mutations** raising production of **other angiogenesis-activating proteins** such as **FGF**, so the balance tips back and the tumour escapes the drug.

### Question 8

**(a)** *Why a transferred stem cell never makes the local cell type.* **[5]**

**1.** Start from the premise the stem hands you: every cell carries the same genome, so the explanation **cannot be that different stem cells have only the genes required for their particular cell line**. That option is offered as a distractor on the specimen paper and it is wrong.

**2.** Differentiation is **differential gene expression**. During differentiation **certain genes become activated and other genes become inactivated in an intricately regulated fashion**, so that **a differentiated cell develops specific structures and performs certain functions**. The mechanism that keeps them inactivated is control of expression, and on the specimen paper the credited route is that **genes not required for the differentiation of a particular cell line are methylated**; methylation of the promoter **silences the gene without altering the base sequence**. So the bone marrow stem cell in the dermis **still carries the skin genes** but has them **switched off**, and the skin stem cell in the marrow **still carries the blood genes** with the same result. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 2]

**3.** Commitment is stable, and this is the sentence the question is really testing. **Once committed, cells usually do not switch commitment**, and **in a new environment they will not change the type of cells they generate**. That is why the new surroundings do not rescue it. The external signals are present: **chemicals secreted by other cells**, **physical contact with neighbouring cells**, and **certain molecules in the microenvironment**, the microenvironment being **nutrients and growth factors in the surrounding fluid**. The cell can receive them and still not respond by changing lineage, because self-renewal and differentiation are **tightly regulated by the cells and molecules in their immediate environment, the niche that they inhabit**, and a stem cell responds to its niche only within the range its expression state still allows. [CROSS-CLUSTER, Cluster 1]

**4.** There is a second, independent reason in the potency itself. A bone marrow stem cell is **multipotent**, so it gives **many cell lines within a specific tissue type**, a **limited number of cell types**, and **all the cells of the blood but not other types**. A skin cell is outside its range whatever signals arrive.

**5.** It still divides in its new site because **self-renewal and differentiation are separate properties**: it retains **long-term self-renewal by mitotic divisions** while **remaining unspecialised**, which is exactly what the stem describes it doing, and the daughters it produces are **genetically identical to it and to each other**, since **mitosis produces two genetically identical nuclei**. State the general principle the two observations share: **a cell's fate is set by which genes are expressed and silenced, not by where it is and not by which genes it has.** One qualification your chapter itself makes is that commitment is **stable in the body and reversible in a laboratory**; write the version tested here, that in the body **commitment does not switch**. R: **different stem cells have only the genes required for their particular cell line**. R: answering that the environment lacks the right signals, when the stem states the cell is in skin. [CROSS-CLUSTER, Cluster 2]

**(b)** *The normal functions, and the two demand examples.* **[3]**

**1.** The outcome says **normal** functions and **in a living organism**. Both words are deliberate, so transplantation and therapy score nothing here however well written. R: stem cell therapy, transplantation or treating leukaemia as an answer to normal functions.

**2.** Embryonic stem cells: their normal function is **to build the body**. They come from the **inner cell mass of the blastocyst**, and **in time the inner cell mass will form the embryo**. Being **pluripotent**, they **give rise to all three primary germ layers, mesoderm, endoderm and ectoderm, and hence to every tissue**, and they are **capable of developing into all fetal tissues**. **Each layer retains stem cells as the organism develops.**

**3.** Blood stem cells: **the primary role of haematopoietic stem cells is to replace blood cells**. Blood cells are short-lived, so the population **replaces them continuously** throughout life, dividing by **mitosis** and differentiating down the **lymphoid and myeloid** lineages. The demand examples, which the outcome expects scaled, are that **the numbers of each type are regulated according to physiological need**: **loss of blood results in an increase in red blood cell production**, and **infection leads to an increase in lymphocytes and other white blood cells**.

**(c)** *A lifetime of supply, and the same cell giving a leukaemia.* **[5]**

**1.** Stem cells divide by **mitosis**, and there are two kinds of division to name. **Symmetric division produces two genetically identical cells**, identical to each other and to the parental cell. **Asymmetric division produces one stem cell and one progenitor cell** with **limited self-renewal potential**, so one daughter **remains a stem cell** while the other **goes on to differentiate**. Asymmetric division is **preceded by localisation of regulatory molecules** or **differential segregation of cell membrane proteins**, so the two daughters **differ from birth and have different fates**; the asymmetry is set up before the division, not decided afterwards. R: describing asymmetric division as the daughters taking different paths after the division.

**2.** The significance line, which is the clean answer to the first half: **mitotic division of stem cells preserves a population of undifferentiated cells while steadily producing a stream of differentiating cells.** That is why a tissue can supply new cells for a lifetime without exhausting its stem cells. The property that makes it possible is **long-term self-renewal**: the daughters that stay stem cells **continue to be unspecialised, like the parent stem cells**, and the population shows **long-term self-renewal by mitotic divisions**. R: **infinite** or **divides indefinitely**, because the biology is about **remaining unspecialised while dividing**, not about a division count.

**3.** Which of the two divisions happens is decided by the **niche**: self-renewal and differentiation are **tightly regulated by the cells and molecules in their immediate environment, the niche that they inhabit**, and in haematopoiesis **the niche is provided by the bone marrow stromal cells**, which act through **secreted signal proteins** and **haematopoietic growth factors** that **maintain stem-cell proliferation**. A growth factor is a **ligand** binding a **binding site** on a **cell surface receptor**, causing a **conformational change** and a **signal transduction pathway** ending in **a change in gene expression**, so the output is matched to demand rather than fixed. [CROSS-CLUSTER, Cluster 1]

**4.** Now the same properties read as a leukaemia. The mitosis that preserves the population also copies the fault: **semi-conservative replication in S phase gives identical daughter DNA molecules**, so **daughter cells are genetically identical to the parent cell**, and what guarantees fidelity also guarantees that **a mutation is inherited by every descendant**. The dividing cell therefore **passes on the damaged genes** to its daughters, giving **a clone of altered cells** carrying an **unstable genome**, and **accumulation of mutations occurs in a single cell lineage**. Long life is the second half of the problem: a cell that **self-renews for the life of the organism** has **far longer to accumulate the several independent mutations in cancer-critical genes** that a cancer needs, while a differentiated blood cell dies within days or months and takes its mutations with it. A cell that already divides continuously is also **already expressing the machinery for division**, so **fewer further mutations are needed** before division escapes control, which means **mutations in the genes that control cell division** producing a **dysregulation of cellular checkpoints**, requiring at least one **gain in function mutation of a proto-oncogene** and **loss of function mutations in both copies of tumour suppressor genes**. R: **excessive or uncontrolled cell growth** where cell **division** is meant; the crucial issue is **the rate of cell division against the rate of cell loss**, held **in balance, so no net accumulation** in normal marrow and **far exceeding** loss in leukaemia. R: describing leukaemia as the stem cells dividing faster. [CROSS-CLUSTER, Cluster 2]

**5.** The property that does both, which is what the stem asks you to name. **Long-term self-renewal by mitotic division** is what sustains the pool, and it is also **the same property that gives the mutation a lineage to travel down and the time to accumulate more**. **Asymmetric division** is what keeps supply and self-renewal in step, and losing that balance in favour of self-renewal is what fills the marrow with undifferentiated cells. The two behaviours are **not opposites**: a tissue that can renew itself for a lifetime is a tissue that must keep a self-renewing lineage alive for a lifetime, and that lineage is the one place a cancer can most easily start. R: treating the two halves as unrelated, when the same property causes both.
