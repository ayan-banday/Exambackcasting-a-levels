## A. The vocabulary this chapter is marked on

**A1. Chromatin and chromosome are one material** at two levels of condensation. Chromatin is the
complex of **DNA and associated histone and non-histone proteins**, **less condensed**, present during
interphase; a chromosome is **the condensed form of chromatin**, packed with the help of **scaffolding
proteins**. Chromatin divides into **euchromatin**, decondensed and **transcriptionally active**, and
**heterochromatin**, highly condensed and **transcriptionally inactive**. The two trade against each
other, because DNA wound that tightly cannot be reached by polymerase.

**A2. Homologous chromosomes against sister chromatids** is the highest-value pair in the chapter.

| | Homologous chromosomes | Sister chromatids |
|---|---|---|
| What they are | a pair, **one homologue from each parent** | **replicated forms of one chromosome joined at the centromere** |
| Structurally | **similar**: same **size, shape, centromere position and sequence of gene loci** | **identical**, adding **number of genes** to that list |
| Genetically | **not identical**, carrying **different alleles at the same gene loci** | identical, from **semi-conservative replication**, until crossing over |
| Separate at | **anaphase I** | **anaphase of mitosis, and anaphase II** |

Schemes police the wording. **Same number of genes is rejected** as a difference between homologues,
and saying homologues have **the same alleles** is rejected outright. So are the loose nouns: never
duplicated chromosomes, never V-shaped chromosomes.

**A3. The centromere is DNA**; what the spindle grips is protein built on it. It is **the region where
two sister chromatids are joined after replication**, a DNA sequence **found in heterochromatin,
repeated in tandem and never transcribed**, and **the last location to separate**. On it assembles the
kinetochore, **the structure to which the spindle microtubules attach**, and the two kinetochores **face
opposite poles**, which makes the pull bidirectional. Centromere is the syllabus noun. Specimen Paper 2
Q6(b) asked for **the roles of centromeres** for three marks, so carry three: it **joins the sister
chromatids after replication**, **provides the site where the spindle attaches**, and **holds them
together until anaphase**, so separation happens at one moment.

**A4. Centrioles organise the spindle**, and plant cells manage without them. They are
**barrel-shaped**, **found only in animal cells**, a **pair perpendicular to each other**. The
**centrosome** is the region containing them, where **assembly of spindle microtubules begins**, also
called the **microtubule organising centre**. **Plant cells have no centrioles and still assemble a
spindle**, which is what most plant-mitosis questions are built on.

| Type | Where it runs | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Astral** | **from the centriole towards the periphery** | anchor the poles; only in cells with centrioles |
| **Kinetochore** | pole to the kinetochore at a centromere | **pull the chromatids to opposite poles during anaphase** |
| **Polar** | **pole to pole, overlapping at the equator** | **elongate the cell along the polar axis** |

Specimen Paper 2 Q6 turned that table into a graph of centromere-to-pole, centromere-to-centromere and
pole-to-pole distances against time. Read each row as a prediction about one distance.

## B. The cell cycle

**B1. Interphase is nearly the whole cycle.** The cell cycle is **the sequence of growth, DNA
replication, growth and cell division** that all cells go through to form genetically identical cells:
**interphase (G1, S, G2)**, then **mitosis**, then **cytokinesis**, which usually **occurs
simultaneously with telophase**. Interphase is **about 90% of the cycle**, so **most cells on a prepared
slide are in interphase**, which answers why a root-tip squash shows few dividing cells. Cells that stop
dividing exit into **G0**, the **quiescent** state. Watch the wording: for **phases**, answer G1, S, G2
and M; for **stages**, interphase, mitosis, cytokinesis.

**B2. Each sub-phase has a job**, and only one replicates DNA.

| | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **G1** | **increase in size and acquire ATP**; **synthesis of organelles and proteins** | the cell **begins after the previous cytokinesis**, **small and low in ATP** |
| **S** | **semi-conservative DNA replication**, giving **two identical DNA molecules**; **histones associate with each** | replication needs the DNA accessible, so it **stays extended and uncoiled** |
| **G2** | **centrioles replicate and the spindle begins to form** | **forming new DNA is energy-consuming**, so a **second growth stage** is needed |

**B3. A dividing cell is transcriptionally silent**, which follows from the condensing. An interphase
cell has **decondensed chromatin**, an **intact nuclear envelope**, a **nucleolus**, and is
**transcriptionally active**; a mitotic cell has **highly condensed chromosomes**, **no envelope or
nucleolus**, and is **transcriptionally inactive**.

**B4. DNA content, chromosome number and ploidy** move at different moments. Count chromosomes by
counting **centromeres**. A human somatic cell in G1 is **2n = 46, DNA content x**. **S doubles the DNA
to 2x while chromosome number stays at 46**, because a replicated chromosome is still one chromosome
with one centromere. **Mitotic anaphase doubles chromosome number to 92** the instant centromeres
divide, and cytokinesis returns each daughter to **46 and x**. Meiosis splits the two events: **anaphase
I halves chromosome number to 23 while DNA is still 2x**, because the centromeres held, and **anaphase
II halves DNA to x**. That offset is how a DNA-content graph tells you which division you are looking
at.

## C. The four stages of mitosis

Mitosis is **the nuclear division of one nucleus into two genetically identical nuclei**, producing
**two daughter cells identical to each other and to the parent cell**. It **occurs in somatic cells**,
and because daughter nuclei receive **precisely the same number of chromosomes as the parental
nucleus**, the **diploid condition is maintained**.

**C1. Describe every stage against all four structures** the outcome names.

| Stage | Chromosomes | Nuclear envelope and nucleolus | Centrioles and spindle |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prophase**, the **longest** | chromatin **coils, shortens and thickens**, condensing into **discrete chromosomes visible under a light microscope** | **envelope disintegrates into small vesicles, which disperse**; **nucleolus gradually disappears** | **centriole pairs migrate to opposite poles**; the spindle that **began to form in G2** develops |
| **Metaphase** | **shortening and thickening at its maximum**; chromosomes **align singly at the metaphase plate**, **equidistant from the spindle poles**; **no pairing of homologues** | gone until telophase | **kinetochore microtubules attach at the centromeres** |
| **Anaphase**, the **shortest** | **centromeres divide and sister chromatids separate**, now **daughter chromosomes**, **pulled poleward as their kinetochore microtubules shorten**, moving **centromere leading**, giving a **V shape** | absent | **polar microtubules slide past each other**, **elongating the cell** |
| **Telophase** | chromosomes **reach their poles** and **decondense into chromatin by uncoiling** | **nucleolus and nuclear envelope re-form**, giving **the granular appearance of interphase** | **microtubules disassemble**; **a pair of centrioles ends up in each daughter cell** |

**C2. Separation and movement are two events.** Merging them loses a mark. A scheme rejects the
phrasing that **spindle fibres shorten, separating sister chromatids**. What separates them is **the
centromere dividing**; only then do kinetochore microtubules **pull the daughter chromosomes poleward**
while polar microtubules **push the poles apart**. A scheme also rejects naming only one class of
microtubule where the movement needs both. One applied line, since it recurs as a stem: **colchicine
interferes with spindle function and can arrest cells at metaphase**.

**C3. Each stage exists** because of what would go wrong without it. Replication happens **before the
nuclear membrane disintegrates**, using **the parental DNA as the template for identical copies**.
Prophase coils chromatin **to prevent entanglement and DNA breakage during separation**. Metaphase
alignment and anaphase separation **ensure each daughter cell gets the complete diploid set**.

## D. Cytokinesis, the cell surface membrane half of both outcomes

**D1. Cytoplasmic division overlaps nuclear division.** Cytokinesis is **the division of the cytoplasm
to produce two daughter cells** and **begins simultaneously with telophase**, by which point the
**organelles are evenly distributed towards the two poles**. Splitting the nucleus fairly is worth
nothing if one daughter gets no mitochondria.

**D2. Animals pinch inwards; plants build outwards.** The wall is the reason.

| In animals | In plants |
|---|---|
| **a cleavage furrow pinches the cell in two**, first as **a shallow groove near the metaphase plate** | **no cleavage furrow forms**, because **the cell wall** cannot be pulled inwards |
| **on the cytoplasmic side is a contractile ring of microfilaments**, and **as it contracts the furrow deepens** | **vesicles from the Golgi apparatus fuse in the middle, producing a cell plate**, growing **across the metaphase plate during telophase** |
| each daughter gets **a complete nucleus and share of cytosol and organelles** | vesicles supply **a primary cell wall** and **a middle lamella cementing adjacent walls**; the plate grows **until its membrane fuses with the plasma membrane**, and **cellulose forms the wall** |

## E. Significance of mitosis, and the regulation half of 2(o)

**E1. Genetic stability is a chain**, not a claim. **Semi-conservative replication in S phase gives
identical daughter DNA molecules → daughter chromosomes are distributed equally → there is no variation
in genetic information → daughter cells are genetically identical to the parent cell → genetic stability
is preserved across generations of cells, and hence in the organism.**

**E2. Growth, repair and regeneration**, quoted back in the outcome's own words. For growth, **new cells
must be genetically identical to the existing cells to carry out the same functions**. For repair,
**damaged cells are replaced by exact copies**, restoring the tissue **to its former condition**. For
regeneration, mitosis rebuilds **missing parts, such as legs in crustaceans**.

**E3. Asexual reproduction produces clones**, which is the point of it. **Many animal and plant species
are propagated by asexual reproduction involving mitotic division**. In amoeba, **the division of one
cell reproduces an entire organism**; in plants the example is **vegetative propagation**. The stated
advantage is **preservation of favourable traits**, which is also the weakness, since there is no
variation to select on.

**E4. Regulation, only as far as the outcome goes.** Required: **checkpoints exist, and their
dysregulation can result in uncontrolled cell division and cancer**. Not required: how a checkpoint
works. One precision point, since Specimen Paper 1 Q16 built a wrong option out of 2(o) vocabulary,
is that uncontrolled does not mean fast. Schemes **reject excessive or uncontrolled cell growth** where
**cell division** is meant, and what matters is **the rate of division exceeding the rate of cell
loss**.

## F. Meiosis I

Meiosis **occurs only in the gonads** and produces **gametes**, which are **haploid (n)** and **cannot be
produced from diploid cells by mitosis**. It is **preceded by an interphase where semi-conservative
replication occurs**, **the chromosomes replicate only once**, and **the centrioles also replicate during
interphase**. **Two successive divisions** then give **four haploid daughter cells**.

**F1. Prophase I does everything mitotic prophase does**, plus three things it does not. Shared: **the
nucleolus disappears and the nuclear membrane disintegrates**, and **chromatin condenses until the
chromosomes become discrete**. Unique:

1. **Homologous chromosomes pair up along their lengths**, a pairing that is **precise** and brings
   **the genes on each chromosome into precise alignment**. Alleles at the same locus must sit directly
   opposite before anything can be swapped without losing or duplicating a gene.
2. **Crossing over occurs, where non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes exchange alleles.**
   **Sister chromatids are now genetically non-identical**, and are called **recombinant chromatids**,
   which is why every later claim about them in meiosis has to be hedged.
3. The exchange leaves **an X-shaped, microscopically visible region**, forming **at one or more
   points** along the pair.

Write **non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes** in full. Specimen Paper 1 Q17 offered **sister
chromatids** as a deliberate wrong option, wrong for a reason worth knowing: sister chromatids are
copies, so swapping between them exchanges identical alleles and generates nothing.

**F2. Metaphase I: pairs align, orientation is random.** **Kinetochore microtubules attach to one
homologue, while those from the opposite pole attach to the other**, so the homologues get pulled apart
rather than the two chromatids of one chromosome. **Homologous chromosomes randomly align at the
metaphase plate**, and **the orientation of the homologues in any one pair is independent of any other
pair**. Combinations in gametes therefore number **2ⁿ**, where **n is the number of pairs of
homologues**, giving **2²³, about 8 million, in humans**.

This is a **metaphase I** event. Specimen Paper 1 Q17 offered **anaphase II** as a deliberate wrong option.
Anaphase only separates what metaphase has arranged, so the random step has to be the arranging one.

**F3. Anaphase I: the centromeres hold.** **The two homologous chromosomes of
each pair separate and move to opposite poles**, **centromeres leading**, giving a **V shape**. The
defining line is that **the centromeres remain intact and the sister chromatids remain attached**. That
one fact separates meiosis I from both mitosis and meiosis II, and it is why chromosome number halves
here while DNA content does not. The separation is **disjunction**; its failure is **non-disjunction**,
which **results in mutation**.

**F4. Telophase I: haploid, but each chromosome is still two chromatids.** Chromosomes **arrive
at opposite poles**, **microtubules disassemble**, and in animals and some plants **chromatids decondense
and a nuclear envelope reforms around each set**. **The nuclei are haploid (n) because chromosome number
and ploidy have been halved**, and **each chromosome still exists as two chromatids joined at the
centromeres, which may not be identical due to crossing over**. **Cytokinesis I occurs simultaneously
with telophase I** and **does not occur in all species**.

**Meiosis I is reduction division.** **The homologues separate and the sister chromatids do not**, **each
daughter cell receives only one homologue of each pair**, and **the amount of DNA is the same as the
parent cell prior to replication**. What halves here is chromosome number and ploidy, not DNA relative
to the unreplicated parent.

## G. Meiosis II

**G1. Meiosis II is mitosis run on a haploid cell**, with one thing carried over. **No further
replication occurs between meiosis I and II**, and that interphase is **very short or non-existent**.
**Meiosis II is equational division**, so **the chromosome number does not change**. What carries over
is that **the sister chromatids may not be genetically identical**, because of crossing over in prophase
I, which is why separating them here is not the same event as separating them in mitosis.

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Prophase II** | **nucleoli disperse and nuclear envelopes disintegrate**; chromatin **condenses to re-form discrete chromosomes**; **centrioles move to opposite poles**; **new spindle fibres appear at right angles to the spindle of meiosis I** |
| **Metaphase II** | **kinetochore microtubules attach at the centromeres**; chromosomes **align singly at the metaphase plate**, singly because no homologous partner is left; the plate is **perpendicular to that of meiosis I** |
| **Anaphase II** | **centromeres divide and the two chromatids separate**, becoming **daughter chromosomes**, **pulled to opposite poles centromeres leading** |
| **Telophase II** | chromosomes **decondense by uncoiling**; **a pair of centrioles ends up in each daughter cell**; **nuclear envelopes reform** |

Cytokinesis II produces **four haploid, genetically non-identical daughter cells from one diploid parent
cell**, each with **half the chromosome number and ploidy of a somatic cell**, so that **fertilisation
restores a diploid zygote with a constant chromosome number**.

## H. Significance of meiosis

**H1. Halving makes fertilisation survivable.** **Meiosis produces gametes with half the number
of chromosomes in the parent cell.** Without it, **fertilisation would double the chromosome number in
each successive generation**. So meiosis **maintains a constant chromosome number in every generation of
a species**.

**H2. Three sources of variation**, and 2(t) names the third explicitly.

1. **Crossing over at prophase I**, which **facilitates recombination of segments between non-sister
   chromatids** and gives **new combinations of alleles** in gametes. Gametes carrying them are
   **recombinant gametes**; the others are **parental gametes**.
2. **Independent assortment at metaphase I.** **The orientation of each pair is random**, so there is
   **a 50% chance a daughter cell gets the paternal or the maternal chromosome**, and since **one pair
   is independent of another**, the division gives **random assortment of paternal and maternal
   chromosomes between daughter nuclei**.
3. **Random fertilisation.** **Gametes carrying different combinations of chromosomes fuse at random,
   adding to the genetic variation of the zygote.** The two 8-million figures are independent, so they
   multiply rather than add.

**H3. Stage by stage**, in the shape the scheme wants. Under **increasing genetic variation**, prophase
I gives **new combinations of paternal and maternal alleles in each chromatid** and metaphase I gives
**random distribution of paternal and maternal chromosomes in each gamete**. Under **distributing half
the nuclear DNA to each gamete**, **DNA replicates only once**, **anaphase I segregation halves the
chromosome number**, and alignment at both metaphases **ensures equal distribution of chromosomes**.

## I. Mitosis against meiosis, compared on chromosome behaviour

Specimen Paper 2 Q6(c) asked for **four ways the behaviour of chromosomes in meiosis differs from
mitosis**, for four marks, command word **state**. Four one-line statements, no development, every one
restricted to **chromosome behaviour**. The number of daughter cells and where the process occurs score
nothing there, true though they are.

Three similarities: **interphase and semi-conservative replication precede both**; **microtubules attach
to the kinetochore** in both; **cytokinesis takes place at the end of both**.

| Stage | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Prophase | **homologues remain separate**; **no crossing over** | **homologues pair up**; **crossing over may occur** |
| Metaphase | chromosomes **line up singly on the equatorial plate**; **no independent assortment** | **pairs line up on the equatorial plate**; **independent assortment between homologues** |
| Anaphase | **centromeres divide**; **sister chromatids separate**; **the separating chromatids are identical** | **centromeres do not divide**; **homologues separate, each still two chromatids**; **they may not be identical, due to crossing over** |
| Telophase and cytokinesis | daughter cells hold **the same chromosome number as the parent**; **both homologues of each pair present** | daughter cells hold **half the chromosome number**; **only one homologue of each pair present** |
| Occurrence | **haploid, diploid or polyploid cells**; **forms somatic cells** | **only diploid or polyploid cells**; **forms gametes** |

The first three rows are behaviour and are the ones a behaviour question can use. Schemes **reject
yes/no comparisons**, so state both sides positively: not that mitosis lacks crossing over, but that
homologues stay separate in mitosis and pair up in meiosis.

**Identify stages from a picture and from a graph**, not only from a description. The school corpus
rates stage identification at **1.1%** of questions, low enough to look ignorable, but Specimen Paper 2
Q6(a)(i) asked you to **identify two stages of mitosis** for two marks, inside an 11-mark question, off a
distance-against-time graph. Trust the specimen over the corpus. The cues:

- **aligned singly at the equator** gives metaphase of mitosis, or metaphase II
- **aligned in pairs at the equator** gives **metaphase I**, and nothing else
- **moving apart, each now one chromatid** gives anaphase, or anaphase II
- **moving apart, each still two chromatids** gives **anaphase I**, and nothing else
- **centromere-to-pole distance falling while pole-to-pole distance rises** is **anaphase**, kinetochore
  microtubules shortening and polar microtubules sliding at the same time
