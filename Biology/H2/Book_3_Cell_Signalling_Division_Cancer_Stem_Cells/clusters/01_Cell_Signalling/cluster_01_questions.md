# Cluster 01 Drill: Cell Signalling
### Biology H2 (9477) · Book 3 · attempt on Loom/Wispr Flow, reasoning aloud, BEFORE reading answers

**Eight questions, 130 marks, 19 parts. Eight, not ten, and here is the reason.** Cluster 1 carries
exactly four learning outcomes, 3(m), 3(n), 3(o) and 3(p), and the study sheet teaches nine sections
under them. Grouped by the concept that has to surface, those collapse into eight questions, each
fusing at least five concepts. A ninth would have to repeat a primary concept, which is the defect you
have named twice, so there is no ninth.

**The single biggest trap in this Book has its own question, Q2.** 9477 Specimen Paper 1 Q22 prints a
five-stage pathway and asks which stages show signal amplification and which show enzyme activation.
Amplification is where one molecule causes many to be produced. Activation is where a molecule is
switched on. They are different steps and chapter 3.1 never separates them. If you have only learnt
that cascades amplify, you cannot answer it.

**Seven of the eight questions open on a real figure or a real data table**, because 38.3% of the
684 questions in the cell-signalling corpus are figure-led and data interpretation is the largest
single archetype in the Book. Two questions are multiple choice, Q2(a) and Q6(a), and in both you have
to say whether **every** row is right and why, not find the odd one out. The letter on its own is worth
almost nothing.

**Reaches back into Books 1 and 2.** Q1(b) needs Book 1 cold: hydrophobic R groups and hydrophobic
interactions with the fatty acid tails as the reason a receptor stays in the membrane. Q1(a) needs the
enzyme material, because the whole question is a receptor set against an enzyme. Q5(a) needs membrane
fluidity. Q6(a) needs a transcription activator from Book 2. Q8(a) and Q8(b) need Book 2's control of
gene expression, since the outcome words the cellular response as a change in gene expression. A Book 1
or Book 2 gap surfacing here is worth more than a clean Book 3 answer, so say it out loud.

**Scope, so you do not waste an answer.** Homeostasis, negative feedback, set point and diabetes return
zero hits in the 9477 syllabus, and 22.8% of the cell-signalling question bank tests them or tests
excluded receptors. Nothing here does. Intracellular receptors are excluded in the wording of LO 3(m),
so only two receptor types appear: the G-protein linked receptor and the receptor tyrosine kinase.
Nothing from Book 3 chapters 3.2, 3.3 or 3.4 appears either, because you have not studied them.

**How to run it.** One part at a time, aloud, mark scheme untouched. Rate your confidence out loud
before every reveal: high, medium or guessed. Check (a) before you start (b). When you hit a blank, say

# Mark Schemes

### Question 1

**(a)** *Decide whether a hormone binding its receptor and a substrate binding its enzyme are the same
event.* **[10]**

**1.** The student is **partly right and partly wrong**. The **binding** is the same kind of event, but
**what happens next is not**, because a **receptor is not an enzyme** and nothing is converted into a
product.

*Similarities, max 4:*

**2.** **Both receptor and enzyme have sites with specific 3D conformation which only molecules of complementary shape will fit into**, and **both hormone and substrate bind to specific sites, the receptor site and the active site, on receptor and enzyme respectively.**

**3.** **Both show high specificity in their interactions.**

**4.** **Temporary weak bonds such as ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions are formed between hormone and receptor and between enzyme and substrate during the interactions.** In Fig. 1.1 these form at the **signal-binding site**. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 1: non-covalent R-group interactions]

**5.** **Both receptor and enzyme can be reused**, that is, both **remain unchanged at the end of the interaction**.

*Differences, point to point, max 5:*

**6.** **Site for binding:** the receptor has a **binding site**, whereas the enzyme has an **active site**. An examiner comment records that **a number of students referred to the NS protein binding to the receptor's active site, which implied that they incorrectly thought of the receptor as an enzyme.** R: calling the site in Fig. 1.1 an active site.

**7.** **Lowering of activation energy:** the enzyme **enables reactions to proceed faster by lowering activation energy**, whereas the receptor **does not**.

**8.** **Conformational change in the site for binding:** the **receptor changes shape upon binding of hormones**, whereas the **active site does not change shape upon binding of substrates**, following the **lock and key hypothesis**.

**9.** **Induced fit:** the enzyme shows it, because **as the substrate enters and binds to the active site, it induces a conformational change in the shape of the enzyme such that the substrate can fit even more snugly into the active site**; the receptor **does not**.

**10.** **Fate of the bound molecule:** there are **no changes to hormones after binding, and hormones dissociate from the receptor after activation of other proteins**, whereas there are **changes to substrate after binding, products formed**, since the **substrate is broken down or converted to product after reaction**. The complex formed is a **hormone-receptor complex** rather than an **enzyme-substrate complex**.

**11.** **Cofactors:** **cofactors may be required** by an enzyme, whereas for a receptor **cofactors are not required**.

**12.** **Function:** the receptor's function is **cell signalling**, the enzyme's is **catalysing biological reactions**.

**QWC:** the answer must be **clearly expressed using the correct terminology and communicated without ambiguity, with at least 1 similarity and 1 difference addressed.** A related scheme **rejects the term competitive inhibitor for Tamoxifen even though the mechanism is competition for a binding site, because a receptor is not an enzyme**, so describe the mechanism rather than naming it.

**(b)** *Why the insulin receptor sits in the membrane, and what holds it there.* **[6]**

**1.** **Insulin is a large molecule or protein which is polar or hydrophilic.**

**2.** Therefore insulin is **repelled by, or unable to pass through, the hydrophobic core of the phospholipid bilayer**, so it **needs to bind to a receptor at the extracellular domain or ligand-binding site to trigger an intracellular response**.

**3.** A receptor in the cytosol would never be reached, so the receptor must be **transmembrane**, with an **extracellular domain facing the exterior of the cell** and an **intracellular domain facing the cytoplasm of the cell**.

**4.** The **polypeptide is folded** in such a way that the **transmembrane region mainly consists of amino acids with hydrophobic R groups**. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 1: R-group chemistry decides where a residue sits]

**5.** **These amino acids form hydrophobic interactions with the fatty acid tails of the phospholipids in the cell surface membrane**, so the receptor is **stably embedded in the cell surface membrane**.

**6.** The **polar and charged R groups interact with the hydrophilic polar phosphate heads of the membrane**, which **allows the receptor to function on both sides of the membrane**, that is, to **bind signal molecule in the extracellular environment and generate intracellular signals in the cytosolic environment**.

The figure is the school's own Fig. 10, chapter 3.1 p. 14. The claim-to-be-tested framing in (a) is the extension. **[INFERRED — extension, no official key]** for the framing only.

**Trap:** enzyme vocabulary applied to a receptor. Active site, induced fit, lowering activation energy and competitive inhibitor are all rejected for a receptor. Second trap: giving only similarities, when the question turns on the differences.
**Concepts fused:** (1) complementary 3D conformation as the shared binding principle; (2) non-covalent bonds at a binding site, traced back to Book 1 R groups; (3) binding site against active site as a named distinction; (4) activation energy as the thing an enzyme does and a receptor does not; (5) lock and key against a receptor that changes shape on binding; (6) induced fit as an enzyme-only property; (7) the fate of the bound molecule, product against dissociation unchanged; (8) reusability as a genuine similarity; (9) a hydrophilic ligand unable to cross the hydrophobic core; (10) hydrophobic R groups and hydrophobic interactions with fatty acid tails as the anchoring mechanism; (11) polar R groups against phosphate heads; (12) extracellular and intracellular domains as the reason a transmembrane protein can transduce.

### Question 2

**(a)** *Which stages show signal amplification and which show enzyme activation.* MCQ key: **C**.
[INFERRED — no official key, Cambridge has published no mark scheme for the specimen papers] **[6]**

**1.** The test is mechanical. **Amplification happens wherever one activated enzyme molecule catalyses the production or modification of many product molecules.** **Enzyme activation happens wherever the step converts an inactive enzyme into an active one.** Ask at each arrow whether **the number of molecules increases**.

**2.** **Stage 1 is neither.** It is **ligand binding to a receptor**, and **a receptor is not an enzyme**, so nothing is activated as an enzyme and nothing is produced in greater number.

**3.** **Stage 2 is activation only.** The activated receptor, through a G protein, **activates adenyl cyclase**. One enzyme molecule is switched on and the count does not rise.

**4.** **Stage 3 is amplification.** **One adenyl cyclase molecule converts many ATP molecules into many cyclic AMP molecules.**

**5.** **Stage 4 is activation only.** **Cyclic AMP activates protein kinase A**, switching on an enzyme without increasing molecule number at that step.

**6.** **Stage 5 is both.** **One activated protein kinase A phosphorylates and activates many molecules of the next protein kinase**, so the step amplifies, and because those kinases are switched on from inactive to active it is also **enzyme activation**.

**7.** **C is correct**, giving **amplification at 3 and 5 only** and **activation at 2, 4 and 5 only**. Both cells must be right for the mark.

**8.** **A is wrong.** It marks **every** stage as amplification, which is the belief that every step of a cascade amplifies. Stage 1 is ligand binding and stages 2 and 4 are switch-on events with no increase in number.

**9.** **B is wrong.** It puts amplification at **2 and 3** and activation at **4 and 5**. Stage 2 does not amplify, and stage 2 is itself an activation step, which B omits.

**10.** **D is wrong**, and it is the mirror image of C. It swaps the two columns almost exactly, marking **4 and 5** as amplification and **2 and 3** as activation, which credits stage 4 with amplification when only one kinase is switched on, and calls stage 3 an activation when cyclic AMP is not an enzyme.

**(b)** *Count the amplification steps in the adrenaline cascade.* **[4]**

**1.** **Four amplification steps.**

**2.** **The receptor activates many G proteins**, so one activated receptor gives many activated G proteins. **Amplification.**

**3.** **Each G protein activates one adenylyl cyclase.** One to one, so this is **activation, not amplification**.

**4.** **Each adenylyl cyclase converts many ATP molecules into many cyclic AMP molecules.** **Amplification.**

**5.** **Cyclic AMP activates protein kinase A.** **Activation, not amplification.**

**6.** **Protein kinase A phosphorylates many phosphorylase kinase molecules**, and **each phosphorylase kinase activates many glycogen phosphorylase molecules.** **Two further amplification steps.**

**(c)** *A drug that shortens the active lifetime of every relay protein.* [INFERRED — extension, no
official key] **[4]**

**1.** The **response gets smaller**, even though **every protein in the pathway is still present and still activates the next one**.

**2.** Amplification needs two things, and the drug removes the second: **presence of multiple steps in the transduction pathway**, and **persistence of proteins in the active form long enough to process numerous molecules of substrate** before they become inactive again.

**3.** The number of steps is unchanged, so **the drug has broken persistence of the active form**.

**4.** The mechanism is catalytic turnover. An activated kinase amplifies only because **while it stays active it phosphorylates many substrate molecules**; cut its active lifetime and **each activated enzyme processes fewer substrate molecules**, so **the number of activated products at each step falls towards the number at the step before** and the pathway relays the signal without magnifying it.

### Question 3

**(a)** *Every protein phosphatase in a liver cell is switched off.* [INFERRED — extension, no official
key] **[8]**

**1.** A kinase **catalyses the transfer of phosphate group from ATP (or GTP) to the targeted protein, leading to its activation or inactivation**, in **phosphorylation**. A protein phosphatase does the reverse, **rapidly removing phosphate groups from proteins**, in **dephosphorylation**.

**2.** Together the pair acts as **a molecular switch in the cell, turning cellular activities on or off as required**, and the activity of a regulated protein depends on **the balance between active kinase molecules and active phosphatase molecules**.

**3.** With the phosphatases off, that balance is **pushed entirely towards the phosphorylated state**. Every relay protein the cascade phosphorylates **stays phosphorylated**.

**4.** The response is therefore **longer**. The pathway cannot be returned to its inactive state, because **dephosphorylation and inactivation of protein kinases and other relay proteins** is what **turns off the pathway when the signal molecule is no longer present**, so **glycogenolysis continues after glucagon has gone**.

**5.** It is also **larger**, because amplification depends on **persistence of proteins in the active form long enough to process numerous molecules of substrate**. Removing the enzyme that ends that persistence **lengthens the active lifetime of every kinase**, so **each one phosphorylates more substrate molecules** and the number of activated products at each step rises.

**6.** So the answer is **both**, and the two effects have different causes: the response lasts longer because **termination has been removed**, and it is bigger because **turnover per activated enzyme has risen**.

**7.** There is a cost. Phosphatase activity **allows protein kinases to be available for reuse** so the cell **can respond again to another extracellular signal**. With no phosphatases the cell **cannot reset**, so it **cannot respond to a second glucagon signal** and **loses its sensitivity to any further change**.

**8.** One qualification, because **phosphorylation does not always lead to the activation of a protein; in some cases, it can decrease a protein's activity**. In this same pathway **protein kinase A phosphorylates glycogen synthase to inhibit it**, so with no phosphatase **glycogen synthase also stays inhibited**, and **glycogenesis stays switched off** as well.

**(b)** *Compare the three termination enzymes and rank them.* **[8]**

*What each acts on, max 3:*

**1.** **Protein phosphatase activity** catalyses **the dephosphorylation and inactivation of protein kinases and other relay proteins**, so its substrate is a **phosphorylated protein** and it works **on the relay proteins throughout the pathway**, impeding it downstream.

**2.** **Intrinsic GTPase activity of G protein rapidly catalyses hydrolysis of bound GTP to GDP, inactivating the G protein**, so its substrate is a **nucleotide bound to the G protein itself** and it works **at a single point, immediately after the receptor**.

**3.** **Phosphodiesterase activity catalyses the conversion of cAMP to AMP**, so its substrate is a **second messenger, not a protein**, and it works **between the effector enzyme and the first kinase**, decreasing the cyclic AMP concentration.

*Contrasted on two further axes, max 2:*

**4.** **Where the enzyme lives:** the GTPase is **built into the G protein itself**, so the switch carries its own off-switch, whereas the **phosphatase and the phosphodiesterase are separate enzymes** that must find their substrates.

**5.** **What is reset:** the phosphatase **allows protein kinases to be available for reuse**, the GTPase returns the **G protein to its inactive GDP-bound form** and lets the effector **return to its original inactive state, available for reuse**, and the phosphodiesterase simply **removes the second messenger**, leaving the proteins as they were.

*The ranking, max 3:*

**6.** **Losing the intrinsic GTPase leaves the most of the pathway running.** The G protein **remains permanently in the GTP-bound active form and keeps stimulating adenylyl cyclase**, so **every step downstream continues to be fed**, and the pathway runs **even after the ligand dissociates from the receptor**. This is exactly the mutation that produces **constitutive signal transduction**.

**7.** **Losing phosphodiesterase is next.** **cAMP levels will increase further, which in turn will activate PKA**, so the kinase arm stays driven, but the **phosphatases still work**, so individual relay proteins can still be switched off and the cell retains a partial brake.

**8.** **Losing protein phosphatase leaves the least running, in the sense that it acts last.** The upstream steps still switch themselves off, since **the G protein still hydrolyses its own GTP** and **cAMP is still destroyed**, so the signal at the top ends normally; what persists is the **already phosphorylated tail of the cascade**. The ranking follows **position in the pathway, not importance of the enzyme**: the earlier the terminating enzyme acts, the more of the pathway keeps running without it.

**(b)** *Compare cyclic AMP, inositol trisphosphate and calcium ions, then judge.* **[8]**

**(b)** *Two mutations that give glycogen breakdown with no glucagon, and which is harder to block.*

### Question 8

**(a)** *How IL6, which never enters the cell, changes which proteins the cell makes.* **[8]**

**1.** **IL6 is hydrophilic or water-soluble** and so **cannot pass through the hydrophobic core of the phospholipid bilayer of the cell surface membrane**. Since it binds an extracellular receptor, it **cannot act directly on the DNA in the nucleus**, so the message must be **carried inward by a chain of proteins**.

**2.** **IL6 binds to IL6R, and the IL6-IL6R complex then binds to gp130.**

**3.** **gp130 dimerises, leading to the activation of JAK, the tyrosine kinase**, **resulting in cross-phosphorylation**, in which each JAK adds a phosphate to the other.

**4.** **JAK phosphorylates a tyrosine residue on the cytoplasmic tail of gp130 of its own subunit**, and **gp130 is fully activated**. Those phosphotyrosines are the **docking sites**.

**5.** **JAK phosphorylates STAT3**, and **phosphorylation of STAT changes its conformation** so that it is **complementary in shape and charge to another phosphorylated STAT**.

**6.** **Phosphorylated STAT3 proteins then dimerise and enter the nucleus.**

**7.** In the nucleus the dimer **activates transcription of genes that induce inflammation**. It does this as a **transcription factor**: **the activated transcription regulator binds to specific regulatory DNA sequences and recruits RNA polymerase, or promotes assembly of the transcription initiation complex, resulting in transcription of the target gene**. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 2 LO 2(i): transcriptional control by activators at control elements]

**8.** The **mRNA is then translated**, so the cell makes **more of the proteins those genes encode**. This is the third stage of cell signalling as LO 3(m) words it, a **change in gene expression**, meaning **turning specific genes on or off and hence synthesis of enzymes or other proteins**. Note that **the target genes induce inflammation, they do not code for STAT3**, and note that an examiner comment records students **misinterpreting STAT3 target genes that induce inflammation and wrongly explaining that the target genes code for STAT3**.

**(b)** *Same rise in cyclic AMP, two different cells, two different outcomes.* **[8]**

**1.** First, remove the wrong explanation. **Cyclic AMP is a second messenger and hence is a small intracellular molecule that relays signals received by cell surface receptors to effectors.** It is **not a ligand and does not bind a cell surface receptor**, so the difference **cannot** be that the two cells have different receptors for cyclic AMP. An examiner comment records that **more than 40% of the students selected the wrong options, all of which made reference to cAMP acting as the ligand and thus binding to the cell surface receptor.**

**2.** The correct explanation is the specificity argument. **The specific combination of signalling proteins, including receptors, relay proteins in transduction pathways and proteins needed to carry out the response**, gives a cell type **specificity in both the signals it detects and the responses it produces**.

**3.** So **two cells that respond differently to the same signal differ in one or more of the proteins that receive, transduce and/or respond to the signal**, which happens because **different human cells contain different genes that are expressed**, so the two cells **carry different relay and response proteins**. [CROSS-BOOK, Book 2 LO 2(i): differential gene expression is why two cells of one organism carry different protein sets]

**4.** **The difference cannot lie at reception.** Cyclic AMP is **downstream of the receptor**, and the stem fixes the cyclic AMP concentration as **the same in both cells**, so whatever happens at the receptor has already happened identically as far as this point.

**5.** **It must lie at transduction, at the response, or at both.** At transduction, the cells may hold **different relay proteins sensitive to the cytosolic concentration of second messengers**, so the **cascades below cyclic AMP differ**. Cyclic AMP's immediate effect is usually **activation of protein kinase A**, which then phosphorylates other proteins **depending on the cell type**.

**6.** At the response, the cells hold **different proteins needed to carry out the response**, so even an identical protein kinase A ends on **different substrate proteins**. A liver cell holds **glycogen phosphorylase and glycogen synthase**, so its response is a change in **cell metabolism**, that is, **regulation of enzyme activity**; a cell whose kinase reaches the nucleus gives a change in **gene expression** instead.

**7.** The general statement of the argument, and the one worth memorising: **human cells express different cell surface receptors that bind to the same ligand, or the same receptor that binds to the same ligand, with different relay proteins, activating different responses in each cell.** In this case the second half of that sentence is what applies.

**8.** The same logic answers the reverse case. **Different types of cells will result in different cellular responses when the same ligand binds to the cell due to difference in proteins present in each cell**, and a **single ligand can activate different cell types simultaneously**, which is why one hormone in the blood can coordinate several tissues at once.
