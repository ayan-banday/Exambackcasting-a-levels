## A. Signalling runs in three stages, and the outcome names all three

**A1. Reception is detection of the signal.** It is the target cell's **detection of an extracellular
signal molecule**. The signal molecule acts as a **ligand**, binding a **specific complementary site**
on the receptor to form a **ligand-receptor complex**, which **causes the receptor protein to undergo a
conformational change**. The two words schemes want for any binding event are **complementary** and
**3D conformation**. Call the site on a receptor a **binding site**, never an active site, because **a
receptor is not an enzyme**.

**A2. Hydrophilic signals force the receptor into the membrane.** They are **unable to diffuse across
the hydrophobic core of the cell surface membrane**, so they bind **transmembrane** cell surface
receptors. **Peptide hormones and neurotransmitters** are the named examples, and insulin and glucagon
are both peptide hormones, which is why LO 3(p) pairs them with membrane receptors.

**A3. Transduction converts an extracellular signal into an intracellular one** that **results in a
specific cellular response**. With cell surface receptors it runs as a **multistep signal transduction
pathway consisting of a series of relay molecules**, usually enzymes, operating in sequence. **The
original signal molecule is not physically passed along the pathway.** What travels is a
**conformational change** handed down the chain, each protein **activating or inhibiting the protein
immediately downstream**.

**A4. The response is cytoplasmic or nuclear, and the outcome foregrounds the nuclear one.** A
cytoplasmic response is **changes in cell metabolism**, including **regulation of enzyme activity** and
**cytoskeletal arrangement**. A nuclear response is **changes in gene expression**, meaning **turning
specific genes on or off** and hence **synthesis of enzymes or other proteins**. LO 3(m iii) words
stage three as **change in gene expression**, so lead with the nuclear one.

## B. The phosphorylation cascade is the transduction mechanism

**B1. A kinase adds phosphate, using ATP.** The general definition is an enzyme that **catalyses the
transfer of phosphate group from ATP (or GTP) to the targeted protein, leading to its activation or
inactivation**, and the process is **phosphorylation**.

**B2. A phosphatase removes it.** Protein phosphatases **rapidly remove phosphate groups from
proteins**, a process called **dephosphorylation**. Together the pair is **a molecular switch in the
cell, turning cellular activities on or off as required**, and the activity of any regulated protein
depends on **the balance between active kinase molecules and active phosphatase molecules**.

**B3. The cascade is sequential phosphorylation, and every step is a conformational change.** Relay
proteins are **sequentially phosphorylated**, forming **a phosphorylation cascade that transmits the
signal received at the cell surface into the cell**. Each phosphorylation brings a conformational
change **which results from the interaction of the newly added phosphate groups with charged or polar
amino acids**. The last kinase phosphorylates **a protein that brings about the cell's response**,
which is where the cascade stops being kinases acting on kinases.

**B4. Phosphorylation does not always activate.** Page 10 says the change is typically brought about
by phosphorylation **which activates the protein**; page 12 corrects it, saying phosphorylation **does
not always lead to the activation of a protein; in some cases, it can decrease a protein's activity**.
The chapter's own glucagon figure has **protein kinase A phosphorylating glycogen synthase to inhibit
it**. The safe rule: **phosphorylation changes conformation**, and whether that activates or inhibits
**depends on the protein**.

**B5. LO 3(o) asks for these two enzymes inside amplification specifically.** The kinase is the
amplifying element, because while it stays active it **phosphorylates many substrate molecules**, so
one activated kinase yields many activated products at the next step. The phosphatase terminates and
resets, **dephosphorylating and inactivating protein kinases**, **turning off the pathway when the
signal molecule is no longer present**, which **allows protein kinases to be available for reuse** so
the cell **can respond again to another extracellular signal**.

## C. Amplification and activation are different steps

**C1. Signal amplification is enhancing signal strength as the signal is relayed through a
transduction pathway**, so that **the response is amplified**. Three key features:

- **At each catalytic step in the cascade, the number of activated products is much greater than in the preceding step.**
- **A small number of extracellular signal molecules (first messenger) is sufficient to elicit a cellular response.**
- **Response of the target cell is large**, because many activated molecules are produced at the end of the cascade.

**C2. Two things make amplification possible:** **presence of multiple steps in the transduction
pathway** between reception and response, and **persistence of proteins in the active form long enough
to process numerous molecules of substrate** before they become inactive again.

**C3. Amplification is one molecule causing many to be produced. Activation is one molecule being
switched on.** They are different steps, and the chapter never separates them. 9477 Specimen Paper 1
Q22 prints a **five-stage pathway** of receptor, **adenyl cyclase**, **cyclic AMP**, **protein kinase
A** and a further protein kinase, then asks **which stages show signal amplification and which show
enzyme activation**. The test is mechanical: at that step, **does the number of molecules increase?**
If yes it amplifies; if the count holds and something merely switches on, it activates. Run the
chapter's own cascade through it:

| Step | Count increases? | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| One receptor activates **about 100 molecules of G protein** | yes | **amplification** |
| Each G protein activates **a molecule of adenylyl cyclase** | no | **activation** |
| Each adenylyl cyclase catalyses synthesis of **many molecules of cyclic AMP** | yes | **amplification** |
| Each cyclic AMP molecule **activates a protein kinase** | no | **activation** |
| That kinase activates **several copies of a specific enzyme** | yes | **amplification** |
| Each of those enzymes **catalyses many chemical reactions** | yes | **amplification** |

Net effect: **a small number of epinephrine molecules** on a liver cell **can lead to the release of
hundreds of millions of glucose molecules from glycogen**. The specimen paper spells the enzyme
**adenyl cyclase**, your chapter uses **adenylyl** and **adenylate**, and all three are the same enzyme.

## D. The G-protein linked receptor

**D1. Structure, level by level.** A GPLR is **made up of 1 polypeptide chain**, which **comprises
seven α-helices spanning the cell surface membrane, connected by non-helical segments**. **Hydrophobic
interactions between the seven transmembrane α-helices result in a barrel-shape conformation**. The
**N-terminus and 3 non-helical segments form the extracellular domain**, the **seven α-helices form
the membrane-embedded domain**, and the **C-terminus and 3 non-helical segments form the intracellular
domain**. It carries **different binding sites for the signal molecule and for the G protein**, one
facing out, one facing in.

**D2. Structure maps onto function, five pairings.**

| Structure | Function it produces |
|---|---|
| **Hydrophilic amino acid residues** in the inter-helical loops and the two termini | domains are **soluble in aqueous medium** and can **interact with water-soluble ligands and G-protein** |
| **Hydrophobic amino acid residues** in the seven α-helices, interacting with the **fatty acid tails of phospholipids** | membrane-embedded domain is **stabilised and embedded within the membrane bilayer** |
| Specific amino acids at the **signal-binding site** | **specific 3D conformation that allows for interaction with specific ligand**, giving **a huge diversity of ligands** across GPLRs |
| Specific amino acids at the **G-protein interaction site** | **specific 3D conformation to bind and activate G-protein** |
| Ligand binding causes a **conformational change** | receptor can **initiate signal transduction pathways via activation of G-protein** |

**D3. The working principle, in order.** Before the steps: the G protein sits **on the cytoplasmic
side of the membrane** and **functions as a molecular switch that is either on or off, depending on
whether GDP or GTP is attached**; **when GDP is bound the G protein is inactive**.

These are your chapter's Fig. 13 boxes, word for word. Learn them in this order and in these words.

**1. Signal RECEPTION.** *The signal molecule (ligand) binds to the (extracellular side of the) GPLR
and causes a change in receptor conformation, activating the GPLR.*

**2. Signal TRANSDUCTION.** *With an increased affinity for the G protein, the cytoplasmic side of the
GPLR binds an inactive G protein, causing a GTP to displace the GDP bound to the G protein.*

**3.** *The G protein is activated.*

**4.** *The activated G protein dissociates from the GPLR and diffuses along the membrane.*

**5.** *The activated G protein binds to a target protein (effector), usually an enzyme (or sometimes a
channel protein), thereby altering target protein activity.*

**6.** *Change in target protein (enzyme) activity initiates a cascade of signal transduction events by
triggering the next step in the transduction pathway inside the cell, including the production of
cyclic AMP (cAMP), OR the production of inositol trisphosphate (IP₃) and release of calcium (Ca²⁺).
cAMP, IP₃ and Ca²⁺ serve as second messengers in signal transduction.*

**7. Cellular RESPONSE.** *The last activated molecule in the transduction pathway triggers a cellular
(cytoplasmic or nuclear) response.*

**GTP displaces GDP, and nothing is phosphorylated at that step.** Two schemes carry an explicit
reject for saying the GDP is phosphorylated to become GTP, the most repeated error in this chapter's
question corpus.

**Where this sequence is examinable, and where it is not.** Steps 1 to 7 answer **LO 3(m)**, which names
the three stages, and step 6 feeds **LO 3(n)** on second messengers including cyclic AMP. So this is
required. **It is NOT required as part of an insulin or glucagon answer**, because LO 3(p) limits that
to the conformational change and the downstream pathway and states that details of second messengers
and specific kinases are not needed. Same content, examinable under one outcome and excluded under
another. See section I.

**D4. Shut-off is built into the G protein.** Verbatim: *The intrinsic GTPase activity of the G protein
soon hydrolyses its bound GTP to GDP, so that the G protein is inactive again. The signal molecule has
also dissociated from the GPLR.* Then *the inactive G protein leaves the enzyme, which returns to its
original inactive state. The G protein is now available for reuse.*

## E. Second messengers

**E1. Second messengers carry the signal onward from the membrane.** **Non-protein signal molecules
are called second messengers**, and they are **small, non-protein, water-soluble molecules or ions**,
freely diffusible. They **transmit the message carried by the extracellular signal molecule, the first
messenger, into the target cell's interior**. **The two common second messengers are cyclic AMP and
calcium ions**, with **inositol trisphosphate (IP3)** and **diacylglycerol (DAG)** as the others.

**E2. Every role follows from one of those properties.** Being **small and water-soluble** they **can
readily spread throughout the cytosol by diffusion**, so a signal received at one point on the
membrane reaches the whole interior fast. Binding of first messenger **stimulates an increase in the
concentration of second messengers**, and because **a large variety of relay proteins are sensitive to
the cytosolic concentration of second messengers**, that rise **alters the behaviour of the relay
proteins**. They **enable cells to mount a large-scale, coordinated response following stimulation by
a single extracellular signal molecule**. LO 3(n) asks for **roles and nature**, so naming cyclic AMP
alone answers half.

**E3. Cyclic AMP is made by adenylyl cyclase and destroyed by phosphodiesterase.** **Adenylyl
cyclase**, an enzyme **embedded in the plasma membrane**, **converts ATP to cyclic AMP** in response to
a signal molecule binding a GPLR. Its
immediate effect is usually **activation of a serine/threonine kinase called protein kinase A**, which
then phosphorylates other proteins **depending on the cell type**. It does not persist, because
**phosphodiesterase converts the cAMP to AMP**.

**E4. Calcium runs the same argument through a different molecule.** It works because **its
concentration in the cytosol is normally much lower than the concentration outside the cell**, so a
**brief increase** is a usable signal against a quiet background. The rise comes from the ER, through
an **IP3-gated calcium channel** that **IP3 binds and opens**.

## F. The receptor tyrosine kinase

**F1. The receptor is itself an enzyme.** RTKs are **cell surface receptors that possess enzymatic
activity**, because the part extending into the cytoplasm **functions as an enzyme known as tyrosine
kinase, which catalyses the transfer of a phosphate group from ATP to the amino acid tyrosine on a
substrate protein**. **Before the signal molecule binds, the receptor exists as two individual
polypeptide subunits**, each with **an extracellular signal-binding site**, **an α-helix spanning the
membrane**, and **an intracellular tail containing multiple tyrosines and a tyrosine kinase domain**.

**F2. The working principle, in order.** Your chapter's Fig. 20 boxes, word for word. Note that all
four reception steps are labelled **Signal RECEPTION**, which is not obvious and is worth keeping.

**1. Signal RECEPTION.** *The signal molecule (ligand) binds to a subunit of the RTK, resulting in
receptor aggregation (i.e. the two subunits associate closely / cluster with each other) and
dimerisation (the two subunits form a dimer).*

**2. Signal RECEPTION.** *Dimerisation leads to the activation of the tyrosine kinase activity of the
receptor, resulting in autophosphorylation / cross phosphorylation.*

**3. Signal RECEPTION.** *Each tyrosine kinase domain adds a phosphate from an ATP molecule to a
tyrosine on the tail of its own or the other polypeptide subunit.*

**4. Signal RECEPTION.** *The RTK is fully activated.*

**5. Signal TRANSDUCTION.** *The activated RTK binds cytoplasmic relay proteins, thereby altering their
activity, localisation or ability to interact with other intracellular signalling proteins. Each relay
protein recognises and binds to a specific phosphorylated tyrosine. The bound relay protein becomes
activated, in many cases by undergoing a conformational change.*

**6. Signal TRANSDUCTION.** *Each activated relay protein triggers a transduction pathway, thus
initiating a cascade of signal transduction events inside the cell.*

**7. Cellular RESPONSE.** *The last activated molecule in each transduction pathway triggers a cellular
(cytoplasmic or nuclear) response.*

**F3. One RTK triggers several pathways at once, and the reason is the tail.** Verbatim: *Multiple
transduction pathways can be triggered by the activation of one RTK, as each phosphorylated tyrosine is
recognised by a different relay protein. Consequently, several different cellular responses are
generated.* The chapter puts the figure at **ten or more**. This is the single cleanest difference from
a GPLR and the reason an RTK answer is not just a GPLR answer with different nouns.

## G. GPLR against RTK, both sides stated positively

Schemes reject yes-or-no comparisons, where one side gets a feature and the other is described only as
lacking it. Each row is one axis, stated positively on both sides.

| Axis | GPLR | RTK |
|---|---|---|
| Polypeptides before binding | **one chain**, **seven α-helices in a barrel** | **two subunits**, **one α-helix each** |
| Enzymatic activity | works **through a separate G protein** | receptor **is itself a tyrosine kinase** |
| Effect of ligand binding | **conformational change**, raising affinity for the G protein | **aggregation and dimerisation**, switching on kinase activity |
| First transduction event | **GTP displaces GDP** | **autophosphorylation** of tail tyrosines |
| Second messenger | **used**: cyclic AMP, or IP3 and calcium | **not used**, as the chapter presents insulin |
| Pathways per receptor | one G protein **binds one target protein** | **several at once**, one per phosphorylated tyrosine |
| Shut-off | **intrinsic GTPase** hydrolyses GTP to GDP | **protein phosphatase** removes the phosphates |
| Example in LO 3(p) | **glucagon receptor** | **insulin receptor** |

## H. Termination and specificity

**H1. Three termination mechanisms, three named enzymes.** A cell stays receptive because of **the
reversibility of the changes that the signals produce**, and **signal termination** returns **the
receptor and each of the components of the signal transduction pathway to their inactive states**.

- **Protein phosphatase activity**, which catalyses **the dephosphorylation and inactivation of protein kinases and other relay proteins**, impeding the pathway downstream.
- **Intrinsic GTPase activity of G protein**, which **rapidly catalyses hydrolysis of bound GTP to GDP, inactivating the G protein**.
- **Phosphodiesterase activity**, which **catalyses the conversion of cAMP to AMP**, decreasing the cyclic AMP concentration.

**H2. Specificity comes from which proteins a cell owns.** **The specific combination of signalling
proteins, including receptors, relay proteins in transduction pathways and proteins needed to carry out
the response**, gives a cell type **specificity in both the signals it detects and the responses it
produces**. So **two cells that respond differently to the same signal differ in one or more of the
proteins that receive, transduce and/or respond to the signal**.

## I. Insulin and glucagon

**I1. Two antagonists, two cell types, one organ.** **Insulin and glucagon are antagonistic hormones
which oppose each other's action**, and **it is the ratio of the amounts of these two hormones that
determines the net effect, not their absolute amounts**. Blood glucose is **detected by the islets of
Langerhans of the pancreas**: high glucose stimulates **β cells to secrete more insulin** and inhibits
glucagon, low glucose stimulates **α cells to secrete more glucagon** and inhibits insulin.

**I2. Insulin works through a receptor tyrosine kinase and lowers blood glucose.** Binding to **the
insulin receptor**, which **is a receptor tyrosine kinase**, **activates its tyrosine kinase activity,
resulting in autophosphorylation**. An **insulin response protein binds to a specific phosphorylated
tyrosine** and is activated, triggering a transduction pathway. Two responses carry the outcome.
Cytoplasmic vesicles carrying **GLUT4 glucose transporters** are stimulated to **migrate to and fuse
with the plasma membrane**, **increasing the number of glucose transporters** so the **rate of glucose
uptake via facilitated diffusion** rises. And **glycogen synthase** is activated, driving
**glycogenesis, the synthesis of glycogen from glucose**. Insulin also **inhibits glycogenolysis and
gluconeogenesis**.

**I3. Glucagon works through a G-protein linked receptor and raises blood glucose.** Glucagon binds a
specific **GPLR**, which **activates a specific G protein**, which **activates adenylyl cyclase**,
whose active form **catalyses the synthesis of large amounts of intracellular cAMP from ATP**. **cAMP
binds to and activates protein kinase A**, and the phosphorylations that follow end in
**glycogenolysis, the breakdown of glycogen to glucose**, while the same kinase **phosphorylates
glycogen synthase, inhibiting its catalytic activity** and so preventing glycogenesis. Glucagon also
stimulates **gluconeogenesis, the synthesis of glucose from a non-carbohydrate precursor, in liver
cells**, and the **glucose formed diffuses into the bloodstream**.

**I4. Draw the boundary before you write, because this outcome is deliberately narrow.** LO 3(p) limits
the outline to **how the ligand induces a conformational change in a membrane-bound receptor to trigger
downstream signalling pathways that elicit physiological changes in blood glucose concentration**, and
states outright that **details of different second messengers and specific kinases activated in the
pathway are not required**.

**What a 3(p) answer must contain, and this is the whole of it:** insulin binds an **RTK** and glucagon
binds a **GPLR**; each ligand **induces a conformational change** in its membrane-bound receptor; that
**triggers a downstream signalling pathway**; and the pathway produces a **physiological change in blood
glucose**, insulin raising uptake and storage, glucagon mobilising glucose into the blood.

**[Not in the learning outcomes, for 3(p)]** Everything in I2 and I3 past the conformational change is
context, not recall: **adenylyl cyclase**, **cAMP**, **protein kinase A**, **glycogen phosphorylase
kinase**, **glycogen phosphorylase**, and the eight numbered steps of the glucagon figure. Read them
once so the pathway makes sense; do not encode them as the answer to a 3(p) question.

**But do not over-cut, because cAMP is examinable elsewhere.** **LO 3(n) requires the roles and nature
of second messengers, naming cyclic AMP.** So cAMP is fully examinable in its own right under 3(n),
and section E is where you learn it. What 3(p) excludes is dragging it into the insulin and glucagon
answer. Same molecule, required under one outcome and excluded from another.

**One verbatim fact worth holding, because it is a clean discriminator.** Your chapter states:
*No second messengers are involved in signal transduction in insulin receptor signalling.* That is the
sharpest single contrast between the two hormones.

## L. Errors in your notes, do not encode these

Found by checking chapter 3.1 against the syllabus and against itself.

1. **Glucagon is said to act on skeletal muscle** on pages 36, 37 and 41, contradicting page 34, which
   correctly restricts it to **hydrolysis of liver glycogen**. Muscle has **no glucagon receptor** and
   no **glucose-6-phosphatase**, so it cannot release glucose into the blood. **Write liver, and only
   liver, for glucagon.**
2. **The same muscle overreach appears on the insulin side.** Page 37 has insulin activating
   **glucokinase** in skeletal muscle, but that is the liver isoform and muscle uses hexokinase. Page
   36 lists liver among the GLUT4 targets, while the chapter's own Fig. 34 caption restricts GLUT4
   recruitment to **adipose tissue and resting skeletal muscle**. Liver takes up glucose through
   **GLUT2**, which is insulin-independent.
3. **Page 11 defines kinases circularly**, saying PK **phosphorylates and thus activates protein
   kinases**. Read literally, kinases only ever act on kinases. A kinase acts on whatever substrate
   protein it is specific for, and Fig. 9 shows the last one phosphorylating **a protein that brings
   about the cell's response**.
4. **IP3 is spelled two ways.** **Trisphosphate**, on pages 13 and 19, is correct: three separate
   phosphate groups at three positions on the ring. Triphosphate, on page 3 and page 18, is a chain of
   three phosphates end to end, which is what ATP has.
5. **No second messengers are involved in insulin signalling is a teaching simplification**, not a
   general truth. LO 3(p) protects it by excluding second-messenger detail from these two pathways, so
   use it for the RTK against GPLR contrast and no further.
6. **Concept Check 1 tests three receptor types the chapter has already declared out of syllabus.**
   Leave the intracellular receptor and ligand-gated ion channel rows; do the RTK and GPLR rows.
